AI stocks had another bad day on Monday, 16 November: the Nasdaq fell 2.5%, the S&P 500 lost 1.9% and Nvidia dropped 4.1%, bringing its loss in market value since its CEO scandal broke to about **$735 billion**. On our data Nvidia now trades about **32% below its best monthly close**, the semiconductor ETF SMH about 30% and CoreWeave almost 70%. The sell-off is not about one company any more: four separate pressures are hitting the whole AI trade at once.

## The sell-off spread from Nvidia to the whole sector

Last week the story was Nvidia's leadership crisis after Jensen Huang resigned. This week other chipmakers, which had briefly risen on hopes of winning share from Nvidia, turned lower too. Broadcom fell for a second day, AMD and CoreWeave dropped more than 5%, and the power-equipment maker GE Vernova, a bet on data-centre electricity, lost 4.9%.

The pain moved to Asia overnight. South Korea's Kospi fell 2.6% and closed below 5,000 for the first time since February, with Samsung down 3.1% and SK Hynix 1.8%. Taiwan's main index lost 2.3%, and Japan's Advantest, which makes chip-testing machines, slid 4.4%.

## Four pressures at the same time

**1. Nvidia's leadership vacuum.** Interim CEO Colette Kress presents quarterly results on Tuesday after the US close. Nvidia guided to about $108 billion of revenue for the quarter. Investors will listen for the CEO search as much as for the numbers.

**2. Politics and data centres.** At the midterms, campaigns spent more than $380 million on TV ads that mentioned data centres, and Democrats won at least five more governorships. About three in four voters told the AP they oppose a new data centre in their own community. Fewer new data centres would mean fewer chips sold, which is why this hits Nvidia, Broadcom and power companies together.

**3. New chip rules.** On Monday the Trump administration issued rules restricting remote access to advanced US chips and servers, a way some foreign buyers rented AI computing power instead of importing the hardware.

**4. Expensive money.** The 10-year US Treasury yield is around 5.1%, up from 4.8% earlier this month. When safe bonds pay that much, investors are less willing to pay high prices for growth stocks whose profits lie mostly in the future.

## The two sides of the debate

**Why some think the drop is overdone.** AI spending has not stopped: S&P 500 companies are on track for earnings per share about 32% higher than a year ago, according to FactSet. Political rules take years to change building plans, and the biggest buyers of chips are still the most profitable companies in the world.

**Why others think it has further to go.** AI stocks were priced on the assumption that demand would keep growing for years. If voters, regulators and higher interest rates slow data-centre building at the same time, that assumption gets weaker, and stocks priced for perfection can fall a long way, as CoreWeave already shows.

Both can be partly true: AI can keep growing as a technology while the stocks of AI companies stay volatile for a long time.
