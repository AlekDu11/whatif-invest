The **S&P 500** is a list of about 500 of the largest companies listed in the United States — Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Amazon and hundreds more. When people say "the market went up today", they usually mean this index.

You cannot buy an index directly. But you can buy a fund that copies it, and that is one of the simplest ways to own a small piece of hundreds of big companies at once.

## What the S&P 500 actually is

- It is run by S&P Dow Jones Indices, which picks companies by size, profitability and other rules.
- It is **weighted by market value**: the biggest companies have the most influence on the index.
- Companies are added and removed over time, so the index renews itself.
- Many of these companies earn money all over the world, not only in the US.

## Index funds and ETFs in plain words

An **index fund** is a fund that simply buys all the companies in an index, in the same proportions. Nobody tries to "beat the market" — the fund just follows it. That keeps costs low.

An **ETF (exchange-traded fund)** is a fund that trades on a stock exchange like a normal share. You buy and sell it through a broker during market hours.

Well-known US-listed S&P 500 ETFs include:

1. **SPY** — SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust, the oldest (1993) and most traded.
2. **VOO** — Vanguard S&P 500 ETF, popular with long-term investors because of its very low fee.
3. **IVV** — iShares Core S&P 500 ETF, another low-cost option.

All three hold almost the same companies, so their results are very close. The main differences are the yearly fee and how heavily they are traded. Check the current numbers on our [SPY page]({lang}/spy/).

## How to invest from outside the US

The path depends on where you live, but it usually looks like this:

1. **Choose a regulated broker** that accepts clients from your country. Check that it is licensed by your local financial regulator.
2. **Check which funds you can buy.** In some regions (for example the EU and UK) US-listed ETFs are often not available to retail investors. Local versions exist — for example UCITS S&P 500 ETFs listed in Europe — that track the same index.
3. **Open and verify the account** with your ID.
4. **Deposit money** in your local currency or in dollars. Watch out for currency-conversion fees.
5. **Buy the ETF** — many brokers let you buy fractional shares, so you can start with a small amount.

Some funds pay out dividends; others (called "accumulating") reinvest them automatically. Accumulating funds can be simpler for long-term regular investing.

## What it costs

Costs matter because they are paid every year, whatever the market does.

- **Expense ratio** — the fund's yearly fee. For big S&P 500 ETFs it is usually well under 0.1% a year (about $1 or less per $1,000 invested).
- **Broker fees** — commission per trade, account fees or inactivity fees. Many brokers now charge zero commission on ETFs, but read the price list.
- **Currency conversion** — often the hidden cost for people outside the US. A 0.5–1% conversion fee on each deposit adds up.
- **Taxes** — dividend withholding tax and capital gains tax depend on your country and the fund's location.

## Why many people buy it regularly (DCA)

**Dollar-cost averaging (DCA)** means investing the same amount every week or month, whatever the price. With the S&P 500 it has some practical benefits:

- You do not need to guess the "right time" to buy.
- In falling markets your fixed amount buys more shares.
- It is easy to automate with a broker that supports recurring buys.

The S&P 500 has also had painful falls: about −50% in 2000–2002, about −57% in 2007–2009 and about −34% in a few weeks in early 2020. Recovery took from months to years. Read more in [what is DCA]({lang}/blog/what-is-dca/).

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Buying a fund you do not understand** — leveraged "2x" or "3x" S&P funds behave very differently over time.
- **Ignoring currency costs** — small fees on every deposit can cost more than the fund's own fee.
- **Selling in a panic** — big drops are a normal part of stock investing.
- **Putting money you will need soon** into stocks.

## FAQ

### Can I buy the S&P 500 with a small amount?

Yes. Many brokers offer fractional shares or low-priced local ETFs, so you can start with the price of a coffee.

### SPY or VOO — which is better?

They track the same index and their results are very close. VOO usually has a lower yearly fee; SPY is traded more actively. For long-term holders the fee often matters more.

### Is the S&P 500 only for Americans?

No. People in most countries can buy S&P 500 funds through a local or international broker, sometimes via locally listed versions of the fund.

### Can the S&P 500 lose money?

Yes. It has fallen by more than 50% in the past and can do so again. Long holding periods have historically helped, but there is no guarantee.
