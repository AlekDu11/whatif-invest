**Bitcoin** is digital money that no bank or government controls. It runs on thousands of computers around the world that keep the same shared record of who owns what. You can send it to anyone, anywhere, at any hour — and nobody can print more of it on a whim.

## A short origin story

In October 2008, in the middle of the global financial crisis, someone using the name **Satoshi Nakamoto** published a nine-page paper describing "a peer-to-peer electronic cash system". The network started in January 2009. Satoshi's real identity is still unknown.

The first known purchase with Bitcoin happened in 2010: two pizzas for 10,000 BTC. Today Bitcoin is traded on exchanges worldwide, held by public companies and offered through exchange-traded funds in several countries.

## How Bitcoin works, in plain words

Think of Bitcoin as a public notebook that everyone can read but nobody can erase.

1. **The blockchain** is that notebook. Every transaction is written into a "block", and blocks are chained together in order, about one every 10 minutes.
2. **Nodes** are computers that keep a full copy of the notebook and check that every new entry follows the rules — for example, that nobody spends the same coin twice.
3. **Wallets** hold your keys. A *public address* is like an account number you can share. A *private key* is the signature that proves the coins are yours. Whoever has the private key controls the coins.

There is no central server. If one computer goes offline, thousands of others carry on.

## What is mining?

**Miners** are specialised computers that compete to add the next block. To win, a miner has to find a number that solves a hard mathematical puzzle. This takes huge amounts of computing power and electricity — that cost is what makes cheating very expensive.

The winning miner receives:

- newly created bitcoins (the *block reward*), and
- the fees paid by the people whose transactions went into that block.

The network automatically adjusts the puzzle's difficulty about every two weeks, so blocks keep arriving roughly every 10 minutes no matter how many miners join.

## The 21 million cap

Only **21 million bitcoins** will ever exist. This limit is written into the software and cannot be changed without nearly everyone in the network agreeing. Most of the coins have already been mined; the last fractions are expected around the year 2140.

That fixed supply is why some people call Bitcoin "digital gold". Compare it with traditional money, where central banks can increase the supply. You can see how both performed in our article [Bitcoin vs gold vs the S&P 500]({lang}/blog/bitcoin-vs-gold-vs-sp500/).

## What is the halving?

Roughly every four years (every 210,000 blocks), the block reward for miners is cut in half. This is the **halving**.

- 2009: 50 BTC per block
- 2012: 25 BTC
- 2016: 12.5 BTC
- 2020: 6.25 BTC
- 2024: 3.125 BTC

Each halving slows the creation of new coins. Past halvings were followed by big price rises, but also by deep crashes later — and past cycles are not a guarantee for the next one. See the countdown to the next halving on our [Bitcoin halving page]({lang}/bitcoin-halving/).

## Why is Bitcoin so volatile?

Bitcoin's price is set only by supply and demand on the market. There are no earnings or dividends to anchor it, and the market is open 24/7. News, regulation, large holders selling and borrowed money (leverage) can push the price sharply in both directions.

Bitcoin has gone through several crashes of roughly 75–90% from its peak:

- **2011** — from about $30 to about $2.
- **2013–2015** — down more than 80% after the first big rally and the collapse of the Mt. Gox exchange.
- **2017–2018** — from about $20,000 to about $3,200.
- **2021–2022** — from about $69,000 to under $16,000, during the collapses of Terra and FTX.

Each time it later recovered and set new highs, but that took years, and there is no promise it will happen again. Check how far the price is from its record right now on our [all-time-high page]({lang}/ath/).

## What is Bitcoin used for?

- **Long-term holding** as a scarce asset — the most common use.
- **Sending money abroad**, sometimes faster or cheaper than banks.
- **Protection from local currency problems** in countries with high inflation or capital controls.
- **Payments** — still limited, but possible, especially via the Lightning Network.

## Risks to understand

- **Price risk** — large and fast drops are normal.
- **Custody risk** — lose your private key or recovery phrase and the coins are gone.
- **Platform risk** — exchanges can fail or freeze withdrawals.
- **Scams** — fake investment "advisers", phishing sites and giveaway frauds.
- **Regulation** — rules and taxes differ by country and can change.

## FAQ

### Who controls Bitcoin?

No single person or company. The rules are enforced by the software that thousands of independent nodes run; changes need wide agreement.

### Can Bitcoin be hacked?

The Bitcoin network itself has never been successfully rewritten. Losses usually come from hacked exchanges, stolen keys or scams — not from breaking Bitcoin's code.

### Do I have to buy a whole bitcoin?

No. One bitcoin is divisible into 100 million satoshis, so you can buy any small amount an exchange allows.

### What happens when all 21 million are mined?

Miners will be paid only by transaction fees. That is expected around 2140.
