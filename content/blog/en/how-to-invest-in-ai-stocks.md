Artificial intelligence is the biggest stock market theme of this decade. Chips, cloud computing and AI software have driven a large share of US market gains since 2023. So a lot of people now ask the same question: **how do I invest in AI stocks without betting everything on one company?**

There are two main routes: buy **single stocks** (Nvidia, Microsoft, AMD, Palantir and others) or buy an **ETF** that holds a whole basket. This guide compares both with real historical numbers, explains concentration risk and shows how regular buying (DCA) fits in.

## What counts as an "AI stock"?

There is no official list. In practice, investors talk about three layers:

- **Chips and hardware** — companies that design or make the processors AI runs on: Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, TSMC.
- **Cloud and platforms** — companies that rent out computing power and build AI into products used by millions: Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta.
- **AI software** — companies that sell AI tools and data platforms to businesses and governments, such as Palantir.

Each layer earns money differently. Chipmakers sell hardware and can see orders swing sharply. Cloud giants have many other businesses, so AI is only part of their revenue. Pure software players are often valued on growth expectations, which makes their share prices very sensitive to news.

## Single stocks: the case for and against

**Why people buy single AI stocks:**

1. You can pick the companies you understand and believe in.
2. If you pick a big winner, the upside is far larger than an index.
3. No ETF management fee.

**The risks:**

1. **Concentration risk.** One company can fall 50–80% even when the theme is doing well. Nvidia, for example, has had several drawdowns of more than 50% in its history.
2. **Event risk.** Earnings misses, export restrictions, a lost customer or a change of CEO can move a single stock 10–20% in one day.
3. **Valuation risk.** Popular AI stocks often trade at high multiples of earnings. If growth slows, the price can drop even if the business is still healthy.
4. **Picking is hard.** In the dot-com era, many well-known tech leaders never returned to their 2000 peaks. Being right about a technology does not mean being right about which company will profit.

## ETFs: owning the whole basket

An ETF (exchange-traded fund) holds dozens or hundreds of stocks in one product. For AI exposure, three common options are:

- **SMH (VanEck Semiconductor ETF)** — about 25 large chip companies. Very concentrated in the biggest names, so it still moves a lot.
- **SOXX (iShares Semiconductor ETF)** — about 30 US-listed chip companies, with caps on the largest holdings, so it is a bit more spread out.
- **QQQ (Invesco QQQ, tracks the Nasdaq-100)** — the 100 largest non-financial Nasdaq companies. AI is a big part of it through Microsoft, Nvidia, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta, but it also holds retail, health care and consumer companies.

**Pros of ETFs:** instant diversification, no need to guess the single winner, easy to buy regularly with a broker. **Cons:** a yearly fee (about 0.20–0.35% for these three funds), and you also own the weaker companies in the basket. Sector ETFs like SMH and SOXX are still focused on one industry, so they can fall hard together — SMH lost more than 80% from 2000 to 2009.

## Concentration risk in plain words

Imagine two people who each invest $3,650 a year in "AI". One puts everything into a single stock. The other buys a broad ETF. If the single company hits a scandal or loses a big customer, the first person might see the whole position fall by half. The second person barely notices, because that company is one of many holdings.

A simple way some investors think about it:

- **Core:** a broad index (S&P 500 or Nasdaq-100) that already includes the AI giants.
- **Satellite:** a smaller share in a sector ETF or a few single stocks they follow closely.

This is not a recommendation for any split — only a way to see how much of your money depends on one theme. Remember that the S&P 500 itself is already heavily weighted towards a handful of tech companies.

## DCA: why regular buying suits a volatile theme

AI stocks swing a lot. **Dollar-cost averaging (DCA)** — buying the same amount at regular intervals — removes the need to guess the right moment. When prices fall, the same $10 buys more shares.

## How to start, step by step

1. **Decide your goal and time frame.** Money you need within 1–3 years usually does not belong in a volatile theme.
2. **Choose the route.** Broad index, sector ETF, single stocks — or a mix.
3. **Pick a broker** with low fees and fractional shares, so small regular buys make sense.
4. **Set up automatic purchases** weekly or monthly.
5. **Review once or twice a year**, not every day. Check that one stock has not grown into a much bigger part of your money than you planned.

## FAQ

### Is it better to buy Nvidia or an AI ETF?

Neither is "better" in general. A single stock can beat an ETF by a lot or lose by a lot. An ETF spreads the risk across many companies. Many beginners start with a broad fund and add single stocks only once they understand the business.

### Does the S&P 500 already include AI stocks?

Yes. Nvidia, Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Broadcom are among the largest companies in the index, so an S&P 500 fund already gives meaningful AI exposure.

### How much money do I need to start?

With brokers that offer fractional shares, you can start with a few dollars. What matters more than the starting amount is keeping a regular habit and keeping fees low.

### Can AI stocks fall a lot?

Yes. Chip stocks and growth software stocks have fallen more than 50% several times in the past. Only invest money you can leave alone through such drops.
