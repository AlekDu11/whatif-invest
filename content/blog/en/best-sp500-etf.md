**An S&P 500 ETF is one share that owns about 500 of the biggest US companies.** The four largest — VOO, IVV, SPYM and SPY — hold the same companies in the same weights, so their returns are almost identical. What really differs is the yearly fee and, outside the US, which version you can legally buy.

> **Key takeaways**
> - SPYM is the cheapest at 0.02% a year; VOO and IVV charge 0.03%; SPY charges 0.0945%.
> - On $10,000 that is about $2, $3 and $9.45 a year — small, but it compounds over decades.
> - SPY is the most traded fund, which matters to traders, not to people who buy and hold.
> - In the EU and UK most people buy Irish-domiciled UCITS versions (CSPX, VUAA, SPYL) instead.

## Which one should you pick?

- **You buy regularly and hold for years:** VOO, IVV or SPYM. The fee is what you pay every single year, so the lowest one wins over time.
- **You trade often or use options:** SPY, because it has the most volume and the tightest prices.
- **You already own one of them:** there is usually no reason to switch. Selling can trigger tax, and the fee gap between VOO and SPYM is 0.01% a year.

## If you live in Europe, the UK or elsewhere

Many brokers in the EU and UK do not sell US-domiciled ETFs to ordinary investors. Instead, they offer UCITS versions that track the same S&P 500:

- **CSPX / SXR8** — iShares Core S&P 500 UCITS, 0.07% a year, the largest in Europe.
- **VUAA / VUSA** — Vanguard S&P 500 UCITS, 0.07% (accumulating / distributing).
- **SPYL** — SPDR S&P 500 UCITS, about 0.03%, one of the cheapest in Europe.

"Accumulating" funds reinvest dividends for you; "distributing" funds pay them out. Irish-domiciled funds also pay a lower US tax on dividends (about 15%) than many investors would pay holding US funds directly. Tax rules depend on your country, so check them before you buy.

## What it means if you buy regularly

The S&P 500 has had deep falls — about −50% in 2008–2009 and −34% in early 2020 — and recovered from each one, but there is no guarantee it always will, or how fast. Buying the same amount every week or month means you buy more shares when prices are low. Choose a low-fee fund, a broker you trust, and an amount you can keep investing in bad years too.
