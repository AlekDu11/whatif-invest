**How did Dogecoin start?** Two software engineers, Billy Markus and Jackson Palmer, launched it on 6 December 2013 as a joke about how seriously people took crypto. The joke grew into one of the best-known coins in the world — and its wild price swings are the best lesson about memecoins you can get for free.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Dogecoin was the first famous **memecoin** (a coin built around an internet joke, see the [memecoin glossary]({lang}/memes/glossary/)). Its logo is Kabosu, a Shiba Inu dog from a 2010 photo.
> - In 2021 it rose from under $0.01 in January to about $0.73 on 8 May 2021 — and then fell back for years. Today it trades at {{price:doge}}.
> - Unlike Bitcoin, Dogecoin has **no supply limit**: new coins are created every minute, about 14 million a day.

This lesson continues [How to check a memecoin before buying]({lang}/blog/how-to-check-a-memecoin-before-buying/).

## Chapter 1 — 2013: a joke coin with a dog

In late 2013 there were already hundreds of Bitcoin copies. Markus and Palmer wanted to make fun of them, so they took the most popular meme of the time — "Doge", a Shiba Inu looking sideways with broken-English captions — and made a coin out of it. It launched on **6 December 2013**. Markus wrote the code for the first versions; Palmer made the website.

People loved it. According to the Dogecoin project, within two weeks there were more Dogecoin transactions per day than Bitcoin ones, and the website had over a million visitors in the first month. For its first years one coin usually cost less than one cent, so people used it for tips and small gifts online.

## Chapter 2 — 2014: the "friendly coin" pays for good causes

The community became famous for charity:

- **Jamaican bobsled team, 2014.** Fans sent 26.5 million DOGE (about $30,000 at the time) so the team could go to the Winter Olympics in Sochi.
- **Water wells in Kenya, March 2014.** Over 40 million DOGE (also about $30,000 then) went to build clean-water wells.

In 2014 both founders stepped back from development, and a volunteer team took over. Markus later said he sold all his coins in 2015 for about the price of a used car.

## Chapter 3 — 2021: the big rally

For years nothing much happened. Then, in early 2021, crypto prices rose fast and Dogecoin became the internet's favourite bet. Elon Musk posted about it again and again. On 28 April 2021 he announced he would host the TV show *Saturday Night Live* on 8 May, and the price jumped 15% in a day.

On **8 May 2021** Dogecoin reached a record of about **$0.73**. The whole coin was worth about $95 billion — almost as much as BNB. That night Musk hosted the show, and after it the price fell. In the following years it often traded under $0.20.

Think about what that means: someone who bought at the top in May 2021 saw most of their money disappear on paper, while someone who bought in January 2021, under one cent, had multiplied it many times. Same coin, different moment. Its distance from the record is now {{fromath:doge}}.

## Chapter 4 — 2024: goodbye to Kabosu

On **24 May 2024** Kabosu, the real dog behind the meme, died at about 18 years old. Her owner, Atsuko Sato, had adopted her from a shelter in 2008. The news briefly pushed the price up — a reminder that memecoins move on emotions and headlines, not on business results.

## What beginners can learn from it

1. **A meme can last — but most do not.** Dogecoin survived for over a decade because a huge community kept using it. Most new memecoins die within days (see [What memecoins are]({lang}/blog/what-are-memecoins/)).
2. **Hype has a peak.** The record came on the same day as the biggest celebrity moment. When everyone is talking about a coin, prices are often near a top.
3. **Read the supply rules.** Dogecoin has no cap. New coins every minute slowly reduce each holder's share — very different from Bitcoin's fixed 21 million.
4. **The price is a ride, not a line.** From under one cent to $0.73 and back down — the 12-month change today is {{chg1y:doge}}. Only put in money you can afford to lose.

> **⚠️ Watch out**
> - **Fake "Dogecoin 2.0" tokens.** Every famous memecoin has hundreds of copies with similar names. Real DOGE trades on big exchanges under the ticker DOGE.
> - **Celebrity giveaways.** "Send 1,000 DOGE, get 2,000 back" posts using famous names are always scams.
> - **Buying because of a viral post.** By the time a coin trends on social media, early buyers are often selling to newcomers.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Regular small buys smooth out the timing problem from Chapter 3: you do not buy everything on one hype day. $10 a day in Dogecoin for 5 years ({{dcainv:5}} in total) turned into {{dca:doge:5}} ({{dcaroi:doge:5}}); in Bitcoin it was {{dca:btc:5}}. Past results, not a forecast — and with memecoins, keep it to a tiny part of your money.
