Every few weeks a new headline asks whether artificial intelligence is a bubble. Tech companies are spending hundreds of billions of dollars on data centres, chip stocks have multiplied in value, and a few giant companies now make up a big part of the US stock market.

Nobody can say for sure how this ends. But history gives regular investors a useful reference point: the dot-com boom and crash of 1995–2002. This guide sets out both sides of today's debate and what that earlier crash can teach.

## What is a bubble, in plain words?

A bubble is when prices rise far above what the underlying businesses can realistically earn, mainly because people expect prices to keep rising. Bubbles usually have a real, important technology at the centre — railways in the 1840s, the internet in the 1990s. The technology survives. Many investors who paid too much for it do not.

## The case that AI is a bubble

People who worry about a bubble usually point to these signs:

- **Spending far ahead of revenue.** In 2024, Sequoia Capital partner David Cahn estimated that the AI industry would need around $600 billion in yearly revenue to justify its data-centre spending — far more than it was earning.
- **Weak returns for many companies.** A widely cited 2025 MIT report on business AI projects found that most corporate generative-AI pilots had not produced measurable financial returns yet.
- **Circular deals.** Chipmakers have invested in AI labs that then buy their chips, and cloud providers have financed customers who rent their computing power. Critics say this can make demand look stronger than it is.
- **Concentration.** A handful of tech giants account for a large share of the S&P 500, so a fall in AI stocks would hit almost every index fund.
- **Warnings from insiders and officials.** In 2025, OpenAI chief Sam Altman said he thought investors were overexcited about AI, and the Bank of England and the IMF both warned that stretched AI valuations could lead to a sharp market correction.

## The case that it is not (or not yet)

People on the other side argue that today is different from 2000 in important ways:

- **Real profits.** In 2000, many internet companies had little or no revenue. Today's AI leaders — Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Nvidia — are among the most profitable companies in the world.
- **Spending paid from cash flow.** Much of the data-centre build-out is funded from the big companies' own earnings, not only from new debt or stock sales.
- **Valuations are high but not at dot-com levels.** At the 2000 peak, the Nasdaq-100 traded at far higher price-to-earnings ratios than the largest tech stocks do today, according to several market analysts.
- **Real demand.** Hundreds of millions of people use AI tools every week, and cloud providers say they are limited by how fast they can add capacity.

Both sides can be partly right. A technology can be transformative and its stock prices can still run too far ahead at the same time.

## What happened in the dot-com crash

The numbers from 2000–2002 are worth knowing:

- The **Nasdaq Composite** peaked at 5,048.62 on 10 March 2000 and fell to 1,114.11 on 9 October 2002 — a drop of about **78%**.
- It did not close above its 2000 high again until **April 2015**, more than 15 years later.
- On our monthly data for **QQQ** (the Nasdaq-100 fund), the fall from March 2000 to September 2002 was about **81%**, and it took until late 2014 to get back to the old peak.
- Famous leaders fell even more. Cisco, briefly the world's most valuable company in 2000, lost about 86% of its value, and its share price then spent more than two decades below that peak.

Meanwhile, the internet itself kept growing. Amazon fell more than 90% and later became one of the largest companies in the world. Many others went bankrupt. Being right about the technology was not enough.

## Why DCA matters if a bubble pops

The worst position in the dot-com crash was putting a large lump sum into tech stocks near the March 2000 peak and then selling in panic in 2002. Someone who bought the Nasdaq-100 at the top waited about 15 years just to break even.

Someone who kept buying a fixed amount every month had a very different experience. Their purchases during 2001–2003 were at prices 70–80% below the peak, which pulled their average cost down sharply. They broke even years earlier than the lump-sum buyer at the top.

What this means in practice:

1. **You do not need to predict the bubble.** Regular buying spreads your entry points across highs and lows.
2. **Diversification matters more in a boom.** A broad fund is less exposed than a few hot stocks.
3. **Keep money you will need soon out of volatile assets.**
4. **Have a plan for a 50% fall** before it happens. If you would sell at that point, your position may be too large.

## FAQ

### Is AI a bubble right now?

There is no agreement. Some investors and central banks see bubble-like warning signs; others point to real profits and demand. Nobody can reliably time when a bubble starts or ends.

### How much did the Nasdaq fall after 2000?

The Nasdaq Composite fell about 78% from March 2000 to October 2002 and needed until 2015 to set a new closing high.

### Should I sell my AI stocks because of bubble talk?

We cannot give personal advice. Many long-term investors decide in advance how much of their money they are comfortable having in one theme, and adjust that share rather than trying to guess the top.

### Does DCA protect you from a crash?

It does not stop your investments from falling. It lowers your average purchase price during a fall, which usually shortens the time needed to recover compared with buying everything at the peak.
