In 2025, wallet-drainer scams took **$83.85 million from 106,106 people**, according to the security firm Scam Sniffer — about $790 per victim. Almost every one of those losses started the same way: a link to a "claim" page, a wallet connection and one signature the victim did not read. The good news is that nine habits, set up once, block nearly all of it.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Drainer losses fell 83% from $494 million in 2024, but Scam Sniffer says new groups keep replacing the old ones, and losses rise when the market rallies.
> - In large incidents, 38% of losses came from "permit" signatures — a message that looks harmless but gives someone the right to move your tokens.
> - A real airdrop never asks for your seed phrase, never asks you to send crypto first and is never "only for the next 30 minutes".

## Real airdrops are boring; fake ones are urgent

A genuine airdrop is announced on the project's own website and its verified X account, usually weeks after a snapshot that has already happened. There is nothing to rush. Fake ones add a countdown, a "you are eligible for $2,400" banner or a DM from "support". Urgency is the scam's main tool, so treat any timer as a red flag.

## The nine rules

1. **Use a separate wallet for activities.** Keep savings in one wallet (ideally a hardware wallet) and do airdrops, quests and new apps from a second "farming" wallet that holds only what you need that week. If it gets drained, you lose the small one.
2. **Never type your seed phrase anywhere.** Not on a site, not in a form, not to "support". No legitimate app needs it. Anyone who asks is stealing.
3. **Get links only from the official source.** Open the project's site from its verified X account or from a tracker you trust, then bookmark it. Do not click links in replies, DMs, Telegram groups or search ads — fake claim sites often buy ads above the real one.
4. **Read what you sign.** If the wallet says *Permit*, *setApprovalForAll*, *Approve unlimited* or shows a token you did not expect, stop. Claiming an airdrop should cost a small network fee and give you tokens, not give someone else access to yours.
5. **Never send crypto to "unlock" a reward.** "Pay a small gas fee to this address to release your tokens" is always a scam.
6. **Ignore tokens that appear in your wallet by themselves.** Scammers airdrop worthless tokens whose name is a website. Visiting it is how the trap closes. Hide them; do not try to sell them.
7. **Revoke old approvals once a month.** Tools such as revoke.cash or your wallet's own approvals screen show which apps can still move your tokens. Remove the ones you no longer use.
8. **Turn on transaction previews.** Modern wallets (OKX Wallet, Rabby, Phantom and others) simulate a transaction and warn about known drainers before you sign. Keep that switched on.
9. **Assume DMs are scams.** Real teams do not message first. "Support" in your DMs is never support.

## What farming actually costs

The hidden cost of airdrop hunting is not scams but time and fees. Many programs now reward months of real use rather than one-off clicks, and filter out people who split activity across dozens of wallets. In November 2026, a widely shared critique of points programs argued that several of them would have to pay far more than past airdrops just to cover what users spent on fees. Points are a promise with no fixed value; plan as if they might be worth zero.

## What it means if you buy regularly

If you invest a fixed amount every month, your main asset is the wallet or exchange account where that stack grows. Keep it completely separate from anything you use for airdrops. A $10-a-day Bitcoin habit kept for 5 years would now be worth {{dca:btc:5}} — losing that to one bad signature on a free-token page is the worst trade available in crypto. Small, separate, boring: that is the setup that lets you try new things without risking the savings.
