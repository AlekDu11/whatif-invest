**AI capex** is one of the most important numbers in the stock market right now, and one of the least explained. Every time Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon or Meta reports earnings, investors skip straight to one line: how much the company plans to spend on data centers. That single figure can move Nvidia, chip makers and the whole S&P 500 within minutes.

This guide explains what capex means, who receives the money, why the market reacts so strongly and how a regular investor can think about it without trying to trade every earnings call.

## What "capex" means

**Capex** is short for *capital expenditure*: money a company spends on long-lasting things such as buildings, machines and equipment, instead of day-to-day costs like salaries.

For Big Tech, AI capex mostly means:

- **AI chips** (GPUs from Nvidia and AMD, custom chips designed with Broadcom);
- **servers, memory and networking** that connect thousands of chips;
- **the data centers themselves** — land, buildings, cooling;
- **power** — electricity contracts, transformers, sometimes whole new power plants.

The cost is not booked all at once. It is spread over several years as *depreciation*, which is why heavy spending today shows up as lower profits for years afterwards.

## How big the numbers are

The four largest cloud and platform companies — Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta, often called **hyperscalers** — are on track to spend **more than $700 billion combined in 2026**, compared with roughly $410 billion in 2025, according to estimates summarised by Forbes. Several Wall Street firms expect the total to pass $1 trillion in 2027.

A few company figures from the latest results:

- **Meta** expects 2026 capex of $135–145 billion, narrowed from a previous range of $130–145 billion, and says it will keep investing aggressively next year.
- **Alphabet** lifted its 2026 range to $195–205 billion, while Google Cloud revenue roughly doubled from a year earlier.

To put that in perspective, a single year of this spending is larger than the annual economic output of most countries.

## Who receives the money

One company's capex is another company's revenue. That is the key to understanding why these announcements move so many stocks:

- **Nvidia** sells the GPUs and systems at the center of most AI data centers.
- **Broadcom** designs custom AI chips and networking parts for several hyperscalers.
- **TSMC** manufactures most of the world's advanced chips, including Nvidia's.
- **Micron** makes high-bandwidth memory (HBM) that sits next to AI chips.
- **Arista Networks** and others sell the networking gear; **Vertiv** and utilities supply cooling and power.

## Why capex moves stocks in two directions

Here is the part that confuses many beginners: the same announcement can push some stocks up and others down.

**For the suppliers** (Nvidia, Broadcom, TSMC, Micron), higher capex usually reads as good news — it means more orders. A hint that spending is slowing is the biggest risk to their share prices.

**For the spenders** (Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta), higher capex is a double-edged sword. It shows confidence in AI demand, but it also eats into free cash flow. Investors often punish a spender's stock when the budget rises faster than the revenue it brings in. Meta, which has no cloud business to rent out its capacity, has seen sharp share-price drops after raising its spending plans.

## The two sides of the debate

**The optimistic case.** Demand looks real: cloud revenue at Alphabet and Microsoft is growing fast, order backlogs run into the hundreds of billions, and these companies are very profitable, so they can afford the bill. Bottlenecks in power and chips suggest the market is short of capacity, not drowning in it.

**The cautious case.** Capex now uses up most of the operating cash flow at several of these firms, and part of it is financed with debt. Companies that were once "asset-light" are turning into capital-heavy businesses, which investors usually value lower. History also offers a warning: in the late 1990s telecom firms laid far more fibre-optic cable than the world needed, and their suppliers' stocks crashed when the spending stopped. We cover that history in [Is AI a bubble?]({lang}/blog/is-ai-a-bubble/).

Both views can be partly true at once: AI can be a lasting technology and the spending can still overshoot for a few years.

## What this means for a regular (DCA) investor

1. **You probably already own AI capex.** The four spenders plus Nvidia make up a large share of the S&P 500, so an index fund is already heavily exposed.
2. **Suppliers are the more volatile side.** Their revenue depends on someone else's budget. If budgets are cut, their sales can fall fast.
3. **Diversify the theme.** A semiconductor ETF such as SMH or SOXX spreads the risk across many chip companies instead of one. Our guide [How to invest in AI stocks]({lang}/blog/how-to-invest-in-ai-stocks/) compares the options.
4. **Let the schedule decide, not the headline.** Buying a fixed amount every week or month means a capex scare buys you more shares at lower prices, while a capex boom does not tempt you to go all in at the top.

## FAQ

### Is AI capex the same as AI revenue?

No. Capex is what companies spend to build capacity. Revenue is what customers pay to use it. The big question for investors is whether revenue will grow enough to justify the spending.

### Why do Nvidia shares move when Microsoft or Meta report?

Because their spending plans are, in effect, Nvidia's future orders. A higher budget suggests more chip sales; a lower one suggests fewer.

### Can capex fall suddenly?

Yes. Companies can delay or cancel projects within a few quarters, as telecom firms did in 2001. That is why supplier stocks react so strongly to small changes in guidance.
