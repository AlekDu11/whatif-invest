**What is an ETF?** An ETF (exchange-traded fund) is a basket of many stocks or bonds that you buy and sell like one share. An **index fund** is a fund that simply copies a list of companies (an **index**, such as the S&P 500), so with one purchase you own a small piece of hundreds of businesses.

> **Key takeaways**
> - One ETF share = a tiny piece of every company in the basket. If one company fails, it is a small part of the whole.
> - Index ETFs do not try to beat the market; they copy it. That is why their fees can be very low (from about 0.03% a year).
> - An ETF still falls when the market falls. It spreads the risk between companies, it does not remove it.

## How an ETF works, in plain words

Imagine a fruit basket. Instead of buying one apple, you buy the whole basket, and the basket contains 500 different fruits. That is an ETF.

- The fund company (for example Vanguard, iShares or State Street) collects money from many investors and buys all the stocks in the index.
- The ETF trades on a **stock exchange** during the day, like a normal share. Its price follows the value of everything inside it.
- The fund charges a yearly fee called the **expense ratio** (or TER). It is taken from the fund automatically — you never get a bill, but it lowers your result.

The first US ETF, SPY, which follows the S&P 500, started trading in January 1993. Today there are ETFs for whole countries, for the whole world, for bonds and for gold.

## Index fund vs picking stocks yourself

| | One stock | Index ETF |
|---|---|---|
| What you own | One company | Hundreds or thousands of companies |
| If one company goes bankrupt | You can lose almost everything | A small dip in the fund |
| Research needed | A lot | Little: choose the index and the fee |
| Typical cost | Broker fee only | Broker fee + yearly fee (from ~0.03%) |

## A worked example: what the fee really costs

Vanguard's S&P 500 ETF ([VOO]({lang}/voo/)) has a yearly fee of **0.03%**. On $10,000 that is **$3 a year**. A fund that charges **1%** a year would take **$100** from the same $10,000 — 33 times more, every year, and the difference grows as your money grows.

So two funds that hold almost the same companies can give you a different result only because of the fee. Always check it before you buy.

## Step-by-step: check your first ETF in 6 steps

You can do all of this before you spend any money.

1. **Step 1 — Choose what you want to own.** The US market (S&P 500), the whole world, or your own country. Beginners often start with a broad index, not a narrow theme.
2. **Step 2 — Find the ETF and its ticker.** Open the [stocks page]({lang}/stocks/) or search on WhatIfStack: S&P 500 funds are, for example, [SPY]({lang}/spy/) and [VOO]({lang}/voo/).
3. **Step 3 — Check the fee.** Look for "expense ratio" or "TER" on the fund company's page. For a broad index fund, lower than 0.2% a year is common.
4. **Step 4 — Check the size.** Bigger funds (billions of dollars) usually trade more easily and are less likely to be closed.
5. **Step 5 — Look at the chart over 5–10 years.** Find the bad years too. If you would panic at a 30% fall, the amount you plan to invest is too big.
6. **Step 6 — Add it to your watchlist.** Press ★ to follow it on your [watchlist]({lang}/watchlist/) or track a test amount in your [portfolio]({lang}/me/). The next lesson shows how to actually buy one.

> **⚠️ Watch out**
> - **Leveraged and "inverse" ETFs are not for beginners.** The SEC and FINRA warn that funds which promise 2× or −1× the daily move reset every day, so over weeks the result can be very different from what you expect.
> - **A famous name does not mean a low fee.** Two S&P 500 funds can differ a lot in cost. Compare the expense ratio, not the logo.
> - **Narrow "hot theme" ETFs are still concentrated.** A fund with 30 companies in one trend can fall as hard as a single stock.
