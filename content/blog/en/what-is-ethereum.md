**Ethereum** is a global network that runs programs nobody can switch off. Bitcoin was built mainly to be digital money. Ethereum was built to be a shared computer: people use it to send money, but also to run apps for trading, lending, games, digital collectibles and stablecoins.

The network's own coin is called **ether (ETH)**. One ETH costs {{price:eth}} right now, and like Bitcoin it can be split into tiny parts, so you can buy a small amount.

## A short history

- **2013** — a 19-year-old programmer, Vitalik Buterin, publishes the Ethereum white paper.
- **2015** — the network goes live.
- **2016** — a large project on Ethereum, "The DAO", is hacked. The community decides to reverse it, and the chain splits in two: Ethereum and Ethereum Classic.
- **2020–2021** — "DeFi" (decentralised finance) and NFTs take off, and fees spike because the network is crowded.
- **September 2022** — **The Merge**: Ethereum switches from mining to staking and cuts its energy use by about 99.95%.

Unlike Bitcoin, Ethereum has a well-known founder and an active team of researchers that keeps upgrading the network.

## What are smart contracts?

A **smart contract** is a small program stored on the blockchain. It runs exactly as written, every time, and nobody — not even its author — can quietly change the result.

A simple way to picture it is a vending machine: put in the right coin, and the snack comes out. No shop assistant needed.

Smart contracts power things like:

1. **Stablecoins** — tokens that track the US dollar (read [what is a stablecoin]({lang}/blog/what-is-a-stablecoin/)).
2. **Decentralised exchanges** — swap one token for another without a company in the middle.
3. **Lending apps** — lend crypto and earn interest, or borrow against it.
4. **NFTs and games** — proof of ownership for digital items.

The flip side: a bug in a smart contract can be exploited, and there is often no one to call for a refund.

## What is gas?

Every action on Ethereum costs a small fee called **gas**. You pay it in ETH, and it rewards the network for doing the work.

- A simple transfer uses little gas; a complex trade uses more.
- When many people use the network at once, the price of gas rises — like surge pricing for taxis.
- Part of every fee is **burned** (destroyed), which slowly reduces the amount of ETH in circulation when the network is busy.

To make things cheaper, many users now work on **layer 2 networks** (for example Arbitrum, Optimism or Base). They bundle many transactions together and settle them on Ethereum, so fees are often just cents.

## What is staking?

Since The Merge, Ethereum is secured by **validators** instead of miners. A validator locks up 32 ETH as a deposit. If it follows the rules, it earns rewards in ETH; if it cheats or goes offline for long, part of the deposit can be cut.

Most people do not run a validator. Instead they stake through an exchange or a staking service that pools many small deposits. Rewards vary over time and are not guaranteed, and you take on the risk of the platform you use. Rules and taxes on staking also differ by country.

## Ethereum vs Bitcoin: the key differences

- **Main idea.** Bitcoin: digital money, "digital gold". Ethereum: a platform for apps and smart contracts.
- **Launched.** Bitcoin: 2009. Ethereum: 2015.
- **Supply.** Bitcoin: hard cap of 21 million. Ethereum: no fixed cap — new ETH is issued and some is burned.
- **Security.** Bitcoin: mining (proof of work). Ethereum: staking (proof of stake).
- **Typical uses.** Bitcoin: saving and sending value. Ethereum: stablecoins, DeFi, NFTs and apps.

Neither is "better" in general. They try to do different jobs, and many long-term investors hold both. Compare their history side by side in our [calculator]({lang}/compare/).

## Price history: big rises, deep drawdowns

ETH has been one of the most volatile large assets in the world. It has fallen by 70–95% from its peak more than once:

- **2018** — from about $1,400 to under $100 after the ICO bubble burst (around −94%).
- **2021–2022** — from about $4,800 to under $900 during the Terra and FTX collapses (around −80%).

It later recovered both times, but that took years, and there is no promise it will happen again. See how far ETH is from its record today on our [all-time-high page]({lang}/ath/).

## Risks to understand

- **Price risk** — drops of 50% or more have happened many times.
- **Smart contract risk** — apps built on Ethereum can be hacked.
- **Custody risk** — if you hold ETH yourself and lose your recovery phrase, it is gone.
- **Competition** — other blockchains compete for the same users.
- **Regulation** — rules for crypto and staking change from country to country.

## FAQ

### Is Ethereum the same as ether?

Not quite. Ethereum is the network; ether (ETH) is its coin. In everyday speech people often say "Ethereum" for both.

### Is there a limit on how many ETH can exist?

No hard cap like Bitcoin's 21 million. New ETH is paid to validators, and part of every fee is burned, so the supply can grow or shrink a little depending on how busy the network is.

### Why are Ethereum fees sometimes high?

Space in each block is limited. When demand is high, users bid more for gas. Layer 2 networks usually offer much lower fees.

### Do I need 32 ETH to stake?

Only to run your own validator. Exchanges and staking services let you stake smaller amounts, but you take on their platform risk.
