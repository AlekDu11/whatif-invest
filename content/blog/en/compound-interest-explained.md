**Compound interest** is when your money earns a return, and then that return starts earning a return too. It is "interest on interest" — and over long periods it does most of the heavy lifting in investing.

This guide explains the idea without heavy maths, shows real examples, and explains why starting early usually matters more than starting big.

## The idea in one example

Say you put $1,000 somewhere that grows 10% a year.

- After year 1: $1,000 + $100 = **$1,100**.
- After year 2: $1,100 + $110 = **$1,210** (the extra $10 is interest on last year's interest).
- After year 3: $1,210 + $121 = **$1,331**.

Each year the gain is a bit bigger, because it is calculated on a bigger amount. With **simple** interest you would get $100 every year, forever. With **compound** interest the yearly gain keeps growing.

These numbers are only an illustration — real investments do not grow by a fixed percentage every year.

## The formula in plain words

The classic formula is:

> Final amount = Starting amount × (1 + yearly rate) ^ number of years

In words: take your money, multiply it by "one plus the rate", and repeat that once for every year.

A quick mental shortcut is the **rule of 72**: divide 72 by the yearly rate to estimate how many years it takes to double.

- 4% a year → about 18 years to double.
- 6% a year → about 12 years.
- 8% a year → about 9 years.

You do not need to calculate it by hand — our [compound interest calculator]({lang}/compound-interest/) does it for any amount, rate, period and monthly deposit.

## Time matters most

Compounding is slow at first and fast later. Most of the growth arrives in the final years. That is why **time in the market** usually beats a slightly bigger amount.

Compare two people, both earning an illustrative 7% a year:

- **Anna** invests $200 a month from age 25 to 35 (10 years), then stops and leaves the money to grow until 65.
- **Ben** starts at 35 and invests $200 a month for 30 years, until 65.

Ben puts in three times more money ($72,000 vs $24,000). Yet at 65, Anna ends up with roughly the same — in this example, slightly more — because her money had 10 extra years to compound. Try your own numbers in the [millionaire calculator]({lang}/millionaire/), which shows how long a monthly amount could take to reach a target.

The lesson is not "you are too late". It is that the best time to start is usually as early as you can, with whatever amount you can keep up.

## Monthly deposits: compounding plus discipline

In real life, most people do not invest one lump sum. They add money every month. This combines two forces:

1. **Your deposits** — the part you control.
2. **Compounding** — returns on everything already invested.

At the start, deposits do almost all the work. After 10–20 years, the returns on what you have built can become larger than what you add each year. This is the same idea behind [DCA (dollar-cost averaging)]({lang}/blog/what-is-dca/).

## Bank deposits vs index funds

Both can compound, but they behave very differently.

### Savings accounts and term deposits

- **Pros:** predictable rate, low risk, often protected by a deposit guarantee up to a limit.
- **Cons:** rates are usually modest and change with central bank policy. After **inflation**, the real growth can be close to zero or even negative.

### Index funds (for example, an S&P 500 fund)

- **Pros:** you own a slice of hundreds of companies, and their profits and dividends have historically grown over long periods.
- **Cons:** no fixed rate. Prices fall in some years — sometimes by a third or more — and past results do not guarantee the future.

## Inflation: compounding in reverse

Inflation compounds too — against you. If prices rise 3% a year, money kept in cash loses roughly a quarter of its buying power in about 10 years. See how this played out in your country with our [inflation calculator]({lang}/inflation/).

So when comparing options, look at the **real** return: growth after inflation.

## Things that quietly break compounding

- **High fees.** A 1–2% yearly fee also compounds and can take a large share of long-term growth.
- **Withdrawing early.** Taking profits out resets the snowball.
- **Panic selling.** Selling after a crash locks in losses and misses the recovery, if one comes.
- **Chasing "guaranteed" high returns.** Very high fixed returns are a common sign of scams.

## How to use compounding in practice

1. Start with an amount you can keep paying every month, even in bad years.
2. Automate it so it happens without decisions.
3. Keep costs low.
4. Reinvest dividends and interest instead of spending them.
5. Give it time — think in decades, not months.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between simple and compound interest?

Simple interest is paid only on the original amount. Compound interest is paid on the original amount plus all interest already earned, so growth speeds up over time.

### Do stocks pay compound interest?

Not interest as such, but compounding still works: company growth and reinvested dividends build on past gains. Unlike a deposit, the returns are not fixed and can be negative.

### How often should interest compound?

More frequent compounding (monthly vs yearly) helps a little, but the rate and the number of years matter far more.

### Is it too late to start at 40 or 50?

Not necessarily. You have fewer years to compound, so regular deposits and low costs matter more. A calculator helps set realistic expectations.
