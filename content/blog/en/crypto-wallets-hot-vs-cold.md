A **crypto wallet** does not actually hold coins. Your coins live on the blockchain. The wallet holds the **keys** — the secret that proves the coins are yours and lets you move them.

That one idea explains everything else in this guide: **whoever controls the keys controls the coins.**

## Two questions that define every wallet

There are two separate choices, and people often mix them up:

1. **Who holds the keys?** You (self-custody) or a company (custodial).
2. **Is the key online or offline?** Online (hot wallet) or offline (cold wallet).

## Custodial vs self-custody

### Custodial wallets

When you buy crypto on an exchange and leave it there, the exchange holds the keys for you. Your balance is an entry in the company's records.

**Pros:**

- Easy to use, with password reset and customer support.
- Simple to buy, sell and convert to your local currency.

**Cons:**

- If the company is hacked, goes bankrupt or freezes withdrawals, you may lose access. When the FTX exchange collapsed in 2022, customers could not withdraw for a long time.
- Your account can be limited by the company or by local rules.

### Self-custody (non-custodial) wallets

Here you hold the keys yourself, usually through an app or a small hardware device. No company can freeze your coins — but no company can rescue you either.

**Pros:**

- Full control; nobody else can move your coins.
- Direct access to apps on blockchains such as Ethereum.

**Cons:**

- If you lose your recovery phrase, nobody can restore your coins.
- You are responsible for avoiding scams and mistakes.

Many people use both: a reputable exchange to buy (see our [exchanges page]({lang}/exchanges/)) and their own wallet for long-term savings.

## Hot vs cold wallets

### Hot wallets

A **hot wallet** keeps the keys on a device connected to the internet — a phone app, a browser extension or desktop software.

- Convenient for everyday use and small amounts.
- More exposed to malware, fake apps and phishing links.

### Cold wallets

A **cold wallet** keeps the keys offline. The most common type is a **hardware wallet**: a small device that signs transactions inside itself, so the key never touches your computer.

- Much harder to hack remotely.
- Costs money, takes a bit of learning, and can be lost or damaged — which is why the recovery phrase matters.

A simple rule of thumb many people use: **hot wallet for spending money, cold wallet for savings.**

## What is a seed phrase?

When you create a self-custody wallet, it shows you a **seed phrase** (also called a recovery phrase) — usually 12 or 24 ordinary words in a fixed order.

That phrase *is* your wallet. Anyone who has it can recreate the wallet on any device and take everything.

How to treat it:

- **Write it on paper** (or stamp it in metal) and keep it somewhere safe and private.
- **Never type it** into a website, chat, email or "support" form.
- **Never photograph it** or store it in cloud notes or a password-free file.
- Consider keeping a second copy in another safe place in case of fire or flood.

No real exchange, wallet maker or support agent will ever ask for your seed phrase. Anyone who does is a scammer.

## How to keep your crypto safe

1. **Download wallets only from the official site or app store**, and double-check the developer's name. Fake wallet apps are common.
2. **Buy hardware wallets directly from the maker** or an authorised seller, never second-hand.
3. **Use two-factor authentication** with an authenticator app on exchange accounts, not SMS when you can avoid it.
4. **Test with a small amount first** before sending a large transfer.
5. **Check the full address** before sending. Some malware swaps copied addresses.
6. **Bookmark** the sites you use and open them from your bookmarks, not from ads or messages.
7. **Split your holdings** — not everything on one exchange or one device.

## Common mistakes that cost people money

- **Sharing the seed phrase** with a fake "support agent" or a website that asks to "validate" the wallet.
- **Losing the seed phrase** and then losing the phone or device.
- **Sending to the wrong network** — for example, sending a token on one blockchain to an address on another that does not support it.
- **Approving unknown smart contracts** that later drain the wallet.
- **Keeping all savings on one exchange** because it feels easier.
- **Telling people how much crypto you own**, which makes you a target.

## Which option fits whom?

- **Small amounts, just starting:** a reputable exchange or a well-known hot wallet.
- **Growing savings you plan to hold for years:** a hardware wallet with a carefully stored seed phrase.
- **Regular buying (DCA):** many people buy on an exchange on a schedule and move coins to their own wallet from time to time, to keep fees low.

## FAQ

### Is it safe to keep crypto on an exchange?

It depends on the exchange and how much you keep there. Large, regulated exchanges are safer than unknown ones, but you still carry the risk of hacks, freezes or bankruptcy.

### What happens if my hardware wallet breaks?

Your coins are not inside the device — they are on the blockchain. Buy a new device (or use compatible software), enter your seed phrase and your wallet is restored.

### Can someone steal my crypto with just my address?

No. A public address only lets people send you coins and see your balance. Your seed phrase and private keys are what must stay secret.

### Should I keep my seed phrase in a password manager?

Many security experts advise against storing it online at all. If you do, use a strong master password and two-factor authentication — but paper or metal offline is the common recommendation.
