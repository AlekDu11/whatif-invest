The world's most-watched AI stock just lost its founder. On **November 12, 2026**, Nvidia announced that **Jensen Huang resigned as CEO, president and board member, effective immediately**, after 33 years at the top. CFO **Colette Kress** became interim CEO, and the board started a search for a permanent successor. Nvidia closed Friday at **$162.85** (live price: **{{price:nvda}}**) — roughly **29% below its September monthly close**.

Here is what happened, how this fall compares with Nvidia's earlier crashes, and — using our own data — what people who bought a little NVDA every day actually have now.

## What happened at Nvidia

- **The resignation.** Nvidia's official filing says Huang stepped down from all roles at once. Board member Harvey Jones became the company's first-ever board chair. The company did not give a reason.
- **The background.** In the days before, Huang publicly admitted personal misconduct in his private life, and the board opened an independent review of media reports that he had been targeted by Chinese intelligence. Nvidia's board said it had no reason to believe national security was compromised, and Al Jazeera noted there is no established evidence of wrongdoing on that front. US lawmakers are looking into it.
- **The market reaction.** Nvidia's shares fell more than 14% at one point on the day of the news before recovering part of the loss. Reuters reported the company had lost about $300 billion in market value since the board's review was announced, and the stock slipped below a $4 trillion market value for the first time since July 2025.
- **Next big date.** Nvidia still reports its quarterly results on **November 17**, as scheduled — the first test for the new leadership.

## A chip sell-off that started before the news

Nvidia's fall did not happen in a vacuum. On **November 5**, the day after the US midterm elections, the Nasdaq Composite dropped 5.5% and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index plunged 12%, according to Yahoo Finance's live coverage. Investors worried that the new Congress could slow down the construction of AI data centers, and hedge funds sold the year's biggest winners to lock in gains.

The pressure continued this week: on November 13, **AMD fell 8.75%**, Broadcom about 4% and Intel 2.5%, while the S&P 500 slipped only 0.4%. You can follow the full picture on our [news page]({lang}/news/).

## Nvidia has crashed like this before

Our data goes back to 2000. On monthly closing prices, Nvidia has had several deep falls from a peak:

- **2000–2002:** dot-com bust — **−87%**
- **2007–2008:** financial crisis — **−79%**
- **2018:** crypto-mining hangover — **−52%**
- **2021–2022:** rate-hike bear market — **−63%**
- **2026, so far:** from the September peak — about **−29%**

Each time the stock eventually went on to new highs — but a single company has no safety net. A change at the top, a lost customer or new rules can change the story for good, and past recoveries say nothing certain about this one. ## What this means for a regular investor

- **Concentration is the real risk.** If one company makes up most of your portfolio, news about one person can wipe out months of savings.
- **Indexes spread the bet.** An S&P 500 or Nasdaq fund holds Nvidia too, but alongside hundreds of other companies.
- **Time horizon matters more than timing.** The 1-year and 10-year results above are the same stock, just held for different lengths of time. Our [compound interest calculator]({lang}/compound-interest/) shows why years matter.
- **Earnings day is volatile.** Big moves are common around quarterly reports, especially with new leadership. A fixed plan avoids trading on headlines.
