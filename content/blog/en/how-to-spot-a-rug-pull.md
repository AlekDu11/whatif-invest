**How do you spot a rug pull?** A rug pull is when the people behind a token take the money and leave buyers holding something nobody wants. It can be sudden (the creator pulls the liquidity from the pool) or slow (insiders sell their huge holdings bit by bit). Either way, the price goes to almost zero.

Rug pulls are not rare. In its 2025 report, the blockchain analytics firm Solidus Labs classified **98.6%** of the more than 7 million tokens launched on the Pump.fun launchpad as rug pulls or pump-and-dump schemes, and found that only about 97,000 kept at least $1,000 in liquidity. Use the checklist below before you buy any new token.

## Two recent examples

- **HAWK (December 2024).** A memecoin promoted by an internet celebrity reached a market value of about $490 million shortly after launch, then fell about 91% in under three hours. On-chain analysts said a cluster of insider and "sniper" wallets held 80–90% of the supply at launch; one wallet bought 17.5% of the supply seconds after launch and sold over the next 1.5 hours. The promoter denied that she or her team sold.
- **LIBRA (February 2025).** A token promoted on social media by Argentina's president Javier Milei spiked and then collapsed within hours. Insiders cashed out about $107 million, according to on-chain data reported by The Block, and Milei withdrew his endorsement.

Both had famous names, huge volume and excited social media. None of that protected buyers.

## The 10-point checklist

### 1. Is the liquidity locked or burned?

A token trades against a liquidity pool (for example token + SOL). If the creator holds the pool's LP tokens, they can withdraw the pool at any moment. Look for liquidity that is **burned** or **locked** for a long time with a known locker, and check the lock's end date. "Trust me, it's locked" in a chat is not proof.

### 2. Is the mint authority revoked?

On Solana, mint authority lets someone create new tokens. If it is still active, the creator can print billions of new tokens and sell them into the pool. Token pages on tools such as GMGN, Solscan or RugCheck show whether it is revoked.

### 3. Is the freeze authority revoked?

Freeze authority lets someone freeze token accounts. A creator who keeps it can block you from selling while they sell. For a memecoin there is no good reason to keep it.

### 4. How concentrated are the top holders?

Open the holders list. Ignore the pool and known exchange wallets, then add up the top 10. If a handful of wallets own a big share of the supply, they decide the price. Watch out for many "different" wallets that were funded from the same source — bubble-map tools show these clusters.

### 5. Can you actually sell? (honeypot test)

A honeypot lets you buy but not sell, or charges a huge hidden sell tax. Warning signs: almost no sell transactions in the trade history, failed sells, or a contract with unusual transfer rules (more common on Ethereum and BNB Chain). Simulation tools can test a sell before you buy.

### 6. Are the socials real?

Check the website and X/Telegram accounts. Red flags: an account created last week, thousands of followers but no real conversation, copied website text, a "team" with stock photos, comments turned off, or links that differ from the ones on the token page.

### 7. Is the volume real?

A sudden wave of volume from new wallets, trades of almost identical size, or the same wallets buying and selling to each other (wash trading) can be used to push a token onto "trending" lists. Real interest usually comes with many different wallets and gradual growth.

### 8. How did the launch look?

Did a few wallets buy a large share in the first seconds? Did the developer's wallet sell soon after launch? Many tools show the "dev" wallet's history, including earlier tokens it launched — a creator with a trail of dead tokens is a clear warning.

### 9. Who is promoting it and why?

Paid influencers, celebrity launches and "guaranteed 100x" messages often mark the top. Ask: if this person is promoting it, when did they buy, and at what price?

### 10. Does the price chart make sense?

A vertical candle on a token that is a few hours old means early buyers are sitting on large profits — and many will sell into the next buyers. Fear of missing out is exactly the feeling a rug pull is designed to create.

## A quick routine before every buy

1. Copy the contract address from a trusted source.
2. Check mint, freeze and liquidity status.
3. Look at top holders and clusters.
4. Scan the trade history for sells.
5. Decide the most you are willing to lose — then halve it.

If any point fails, walk away. There will always be another token.

## Even "clean" tokens can fall 90%

Passing the checklist only lowers the risk of outright fraud. It does not make a token a good investment. Even the best-known memecoins have fallen sharply from their peaks; you can see the current drawdowns on our [memecoins page]({lang}/memes/) and in the [all-time-high tracker]({lang}/ath/). CoinGecko counted more than half of the tokens it tracked since 2021 as dead by the end of 2025.

## FAQ

### Is a rug pull illegal?

It depends on the country and the details. Many rug pulls are treated as fraud, but on-chain funds are rarely recovered, so prevention is far better than hoping for justice.

### Does a locked liquidity pool mean the token is safe?

No. It removes one way to rug, but insiders can still dump a large share of the supply, and the lock can expire.

### Can I get my money back after a rug pull?

Usually not. Report it to the police and the platform, and beware of "recovery services" that ask for a fee — they are often a second scam.

### Which tools help check a token?

Token pages on GMGN and DEX screeners, block explorers such as Solscan, and checkers such as RugCheck show liquidity, authorities and holders. Use more than one.
