There is no single "right" amount to invest each month. But there are simple rules that work for most people — and real historical numbers that show why even small, regular amounts add up.

This guide covers the order of steps, a few rules of thumb, and how to test your own amount with our calculators.

## Step 1. Emergency fund first

Before investing, most financial educators suggest an **emergency fund**: cash you can reach in a day or two, kept in a savings account.

- A common target is **3–6 months of essential expenses** (rent, food, transport, bills).
- Freelancers, single earners or people in unstable jobs often aim for 6–12 months.

Why first? Markets fall. If your car breaks or you lose your job during a crash, you do not want to be forced to sell investments at a loss. The emergency fund lets your investments stay invested.

You do not need the full fund before starting. Many people split: for example, 70% of spare money to the fund and 30% to investing until the fund is complete.

## Step 2. Pay off expensive debt

If you have credit-card debt at 20–30% interest a year, paying it off is a guaranteed "return" that no market reliably beats. Low-interest debt such as a mortgage is a different case — many people invest while paying it.

## Step 3. Pick a percentage of income

Instead of a fixed number, many people use a share of their take-home pay. Popular rules of thumb:

- **50/30/20 rule:** 50% for needs, 30% for wants, **20% for savings and investing**.
- **"Pay yourself first":** move 10–15% to investing on payday, then spend the rest.
- **Start with 1%:** if money is tight, invest 1% of income and raise it by 1% every few months or with every pay rise.

Example with a monthly take-home pay of $2,000 (or the same amount in your currency):

- **5%** — $100 a month, about $3.30 a day
- **10%** — $200 a month, about $6.60 a day
- **20%** — $400 a month, about $13 a day

What matters most is not the exact share but that it is **automatic and sustainable**. An amount you can keep for 10 years beats a big amount you stop after 3 months.

## How compound growth works

When your investments earn a return, next year's return is calculated on a bigger base. This snowball effect is slow at first and fast later.

For example, $200 a month at an assumed 7% a year (a rough long-term average used in many textbooks, not a promise):

- after 10 years — about $34,000 (you put in $24,000),
- after 20 years — about $104,000 (you put in $48,000),
- after 30 years — about $244,000 (you put in $72,000).

In the last decade of that example, growth adds more than the first two decades combined. Check your own numbers in the [compound interest calculator]({lang}/compound-interest/).

## How to split the monthly amount

There is no universal mix, but common approaches for beginners include:

- **Mostly broad index funds** (for example an S&P 500 or world index ETF) as the core.
- **A small slice of higher-risk assets** such as Bitcoin, sized so a 70–80% drop would not change your life.
- **Some gold** for people who want a hedge against currency problems.

The more volatile the asset, the smaller its share should usually be. Read [Bitcoin vs gold vs the S&P 500]({lang}/blog/bitcoin-vs-gold-vs-sp500/) for a side-by-side look.

## Make it automatic

1. Set a **recurring transfer** from your bank on payday.
2. Set a **recurring buy** with your broker or exchange — see [what DCA is]({lang}/blog/what-is-dca/).
3. Review once or twice a year, not every day.
4. Raise the amount when your income grows.

## Common mistakes

- Waiting for "the perfect moment" and never starting.
- Investing money you will need in the next 1–3 years.
- Stopping regular buys during a crash — historically, that was when DCA bought the cheapest.
- Ignoring fees: a 2% yearly fee can eat a large part of long-term growth.

## FAQ

### Is $50 or $100 a month worth investing?

Yes, it can be. Small regular amounts build the habit, and many brokers and exchanges allow fractional purchases. The table above shows what about $300 a month became historically.

### Should I invest before my emergency fund is full?

Many people build both at the same time, with most money going to the fund first. The key is not to invest money you might need soon.

### What percentage of my salary should I invest?

A common guideline is 10–20% of take-home pay, but any steady amount you can keep up is a good start.

### Is it better to invest monthly or all at once?

Historically, a lump sum often wins in rising markets, while regular buys reduce the risk of bad timing. Compare both in our [DCA calculator]({lang}/compare/).
