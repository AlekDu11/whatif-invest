**What is a memecoin?** A memecoin is a crypto token (a digital coin on a blockchain) built around a joke, an animal, a picture or a trend — not around a product or a business. Its price moves on attention and hype, so most new memecoins lose almost all their value, often within days.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Memecoins usually have no product, no profits and no owner who must pay you back. The price is only what the next buyer will pay.
> - CoinGecko counted 11.6 million crypto tokens that stopped trading in 2025 alone — 86% of all failures it has recorded since 2021.
> - On pump.fun, the biggest memecoin factory, only about 1–2% of new tokens ever reach a real exchange.

## Where memecoins came from

The first memecoin was **Dogecoin**. Two software engineers, Billy Markus and Jackson Palmer, made it in 2013 as a joke, using the "Doge" Shiba Inu dog meme. It copied Bitcoin's code and took only a few hours to build. Years later, the joke became one of the biggest coins by market value.

Today anyone can create a memecoin in a minute and for almost no money. Websites called **launchpads** (token factories) such as pump.fun let you pick a name and a picture and press a button. Pump.fun launched in January 2024; by 2026 it had produced about 11.9 million tokens.

## Why memecoins are different from Bitcoin or stocks

- **A stock** is a piece of a company that sells things and can make a profit.
- **Bitcoin** has a fixed supply and a 17-year track record (see our [Bitcoin page]({lang}/btc/)).
- **A memecoin** usually has none of this. Its value depends on a story, a community and whether people keep talking about it.

This is why prices can jump 10× in a day and fall 90% the next.

## Why almost all new memecoins die

**1. Too many coins, too few buyers.** CoinGecko's data site GeckoTerminal listed about 428,000 tokens in 2021 and almost 20.2 million by 2025. Attention cannot cover millions of coins.

**2. Most never reach a real market.** On pump.fun a token "graduates" to a proper exchange only after enough people buy it. Dune data shows the graduation rate stayed below 1% for weeks in early 2025; over its whole life it is under 2%.

**3. Creators and early bots sell first.** Insiders often buy in the first seconds and sell to the people who arrive later. A 2025 Solidus Labs report labelled 98.6% of pump.fun tokens as rug pulls (the creator takes the money and leaves) or pump-and-dump schemes.

**4. Hype ends.** When a trend is over, buyers leave, and with no business behind the coin, nothing holds the price up.

The result: CoinGecko counted **53.2%** of all tokens ever listed on GeckoTerminal as dead (no longer traded) by the end of 2025, and **11.6 million** died in 2025 alone.

## How to look at a memecoin, step by step

**Step 1 — Check the age.** A coin created a few hours ago is a lottery ticket. Older coins have a price history you can study.

**Step 2 — Check where it trades.** Is it only on one small DEX (a decentralised exchange, where people swap tokens directly), or also on big exchanges?

**Step 3 — Check liquidity and holders.** Liquidity is the money in the trading pool. If it is tiny, or a few wallets own most coins, you may not be able to sell.

**Step 4 — Look at the chart over months, not minutes.** Our [memecoins page]({lang}/memes/) shows established memecoins and fresh tokens side by side.

**Step 5 — Decide your limit before you start.** Only an amount you are ready to lose completely.

> **⚠️ Watch out**
> - **Fake copies.** Many tokens share the same name and logo. Always check the contract address (the coin's unique ID) from an official source.
> - **"This one will 100×" messages.** Groups and influencers are often paid to promote, then sell to their followers.
> - **Buying what you cannot sell.** Some tokens are built so you can buy but not sell (a "honeypot"). Read our [scam guide]({lang}/blog/memecoin-scams-rug-pull/).
