**Stacks** is a blockchain built on top of Bitcoin that adds what Bitcoin itself does not have: smart contracts and apps such as lending, trading and NFTs. ## How it works

Stacks uses a system called Proof of Transfer (PoX). Instead of burning electricity like Bitcoin miners, Stacks miners send bitcoin to bid for the right to write the next Stacks block and receive newly created STX. That bitcoin does not disappear: it goes to people who "stack" — lock their STX for a set number of cycles (about two weeks each) to help secure the network. Every Stacks block is recorded on Bitcoin, so rewriting Stacks history would mean rewriting Bitcoin's. Apps are written in Clarity, a programming language designed to be predictable and easy to check, and it can read what happens on the Bitcoin chain.

## Why people think it has value

STX is needed to pay transaction fees on Stacks, it is what miners earn, and locking it is the way to receive BTC rewards — so demand for the network ties directly into demand for the token. The bigger idea is Bitcoin DeFi: most bitcoin sits idle, and Stacks offers a way to use it in apps without leaving the Bitcoin world. Its main tool for this is sBTC, a token backed 1:1 by bitcoin that can be moved into Stacks apps and redeemed for BTC again.

## Its story so far

Muneeb Ali and Ryan Shea started Blockstack in 2013 after meeting at Princeton. In 2017 the project sold tokens to investors, and in July 2019 it became the first company to offer a token to the public under an SEC-qualified Regulation A+ sale. In January 2021 the Stacks 2.0 mainnet went live with Proof of Transfer and Clarity, and the price rose from about $0.40 to above $2 that year. It fell to about $0.20 by the end of 2022, then rallied with the Bitcoin "Ordinals" boom and set its record on 1 April 2024. The Nakamoto upgrade followed in October 2024, sBTC deposits opened in December 2024 and withdrawals back to bitcoin in April 2025. The price nevertheless slid through 2025 and stayed below $0.40 for most of 2026.

## Alternatives

Among projects that bring apps to bitcoin, Rootstock runs an Ethereum-compatible sidechain secured by Bitcoin miners, [Core]({lang}/core/) is a separate chain with bitcoin staking, and [Babylon]({lang}/baby/) lets people stake bitcoin to secure other networks. Many bitcoin holders simply use wrapped bitcoin on [Ethereum]({lang}/eth/) instead. And for anyone who just wants bitcoin itself, [Bitcoin]({lang}/btc/) is the obvious alternative.

## Main risks

- **Big swings:** STX lost more than 90% from its 2021 high by the end of 2022, and similar falls happened again after its 2024 record.
- **New supply:** new STX are created as mining rewards every day, which can add steady selling pressure.
- **Competition:** many newer Bitcoin layers fight for the same users and the same bitcoin.
- **Technology:** sBTC relies on a group of signers holding the bitcoin; a bug or failure there could hurt trust in the whole network.
- **Storage and regulation:** if an exchange fails or you lose your private key, the coins can be gone; crypto rules differ by country and can change quickly.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Stacks' price has moved in long waves: two big peaks, in 2021 and 2024, each followed by a deep fall. Buying a fixed amount regularly means you buy more when the price is low and less when it is high, without trying to guess the bottom — but it does not protect you from a long decline, as the five-year result above shows. Many people keep smaller coins like STX to a small part of their savings that they can afford to see fall sharply. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
