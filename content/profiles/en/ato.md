**Atmos Energy** is one of America's largest natural-gas-only utilities. Based in Dallas, it delivers gas for heating, hot water and cooking to about 3.4 million homes and businesses in more than 1,400 communities across eight mostly southern states, and it runs one of the largest gas pipeline and storage systems inside Texas. Because state regulators set its prices, its profits grow mainly by investing in pipes — not by selling more gas.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Fiscal 2026 (year to 30 September): earnings per share $8.49, up from $7.46; net income about $1.4 billion.
> - It invested $4.2 billion in fiscal 2026, more than 85% of it in safety and reliability.
> - The dividend for fiscal 2027 rises 9.0% to $4.36 a share — the 43rd yearly increase in a row.
> - Its five-year plan through fiscal 2031 calls for nearly $29 billion of investment, about $23 billion of it in Texas.

## How it makes money

Atmos is a regulated monopoly: in each town it is the only company that delivers gas through the pipes. Regulators in each state decide what it may charge, allowing it to recover its costs and earn a set return on the money it has invested in the network (its "rate base"). The cost of the gas itself is mostly passed on to customers, so Atmos does not earn more when gas prices rise. Its second business, **Atmos Pipeline–Texas**, moves gas across the state and stores it; most of its revenue comes from fixed delivery charges.

In fiscal 2026 Atmos earned $8.49 per diluted share, its 24th year in a row of higher earnings per share. Regulators approved new rates worth $395.4 million a year. For fiscal 2027 the company expects earnings of $9.05 to $9.25 per share and about $4.5 billion of investment. It added about 49,000 residential customers in the year, more than 37,000 of them in fast-growing Texas. At the end of September equity made up 60% of its capital and it had $3.8 billion of available liquidity.

## The stock's story

Atmos traces its roots to the Amarillo Gas Company, founded in Texas in 1906, and has used the Atmos name since 1988. It grew by buying other gas utilities across the South. Two events shaped it recently. In 2018 a gas explosion at a house in Dallas killed a 12-year-old girl, and the company speeded up the replacement of old pipes. In February 2021 Winter Storm Uri sent gas prices soaring; Atmos had to pay about $2 billion of extra gas costs, which Texas later allowed it to recover from customers over many years. Kevin Akers has been CEO since 2017.

## Competitors

Atmos has no direct rival for gas delivery in its towns. Investors compare it with other gas utilities such as ONE Gas, Southwest Gas, NiSource, Spire and CenterPoint Energy. Its real competition is electricity: heat pumps and electric stoves can replace gas in new homes.

## Main risks

- **Regulators:** if states allow lower returns or delay rate increases, profit growth slows.
- **Interest rates:** utilities borrow heavily, and higher rates raise costs and make dividends less attractive by comparison.
- **New shares:** much of the plan is funded with new stock, which spreads profits over more shares.
- **Safety:** a leak or explosion can cost lives, money and trust.
- **Electrification:** rules that limit gas in new buildings could slow customer growth.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Utilities like Atmos usually move less than the wider market, and the dividend has risen every year for decades — but its shares can still fall, especially when interest rates rise. Regular buying of a fixed amount spreads purchases over calm and stormy periods, and reinvested dividends add up over time. Many people hold a utility as the steadier part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
