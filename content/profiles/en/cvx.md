**Chevron** is one of the world's largest oil and gas companies and the second biggest in the US after ExxonMobil. It pumps oil and gas from Texas and New Mexico (the Permian Basin), the US Gulf of America, Kazakhstan, Australia, Guyana and other countries, refines it into petrol, diesel and jet fuel, and sells it at Chevron and Texaco stations. Its profits rise and fall with the oil price — but it has raised its dividend every year for almost four decades.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Profit: $12.3 billion in 2025 ($6.63 per share), down from $17.7 billion in 2024 as oil prices fell; revenue $184.4 billion.
> - Production hit a record 3.72 million barrels of oil equivalent a day in 2025 (+12%), helped by the Hess takeover.
> - In the July–September 2026 quarter, with Brent oil averaging $97, profit jumped to $9.9 billion from $3.5 billion a year earlier.
> - In 2026 Chevron raised its quarterly dividend by 4% to $1.78 — its 39th yearly increase in a row.

## How it makes money

Chevron has two main businesses. **Upstream** finds and produces crude oil and natural gas, including liquefied natural gas (LNG) from Australia; it earns most of the profit when oil is expensive. **Downstream** refines crude into fuels, lubricants and chemicals and sells them; it does better when refining margins are wide. Because the oil price drives both businesses, Chevron's results swing far more from year to year than those of most big companies. The company returns much of its cash to owners: in 2025 it paid $12.8 billion in dividends and bought back about $12.1 billion of its own shares.

In the third quarter of 2026 Chevron earned $9.9 billion, or $5.06 per share (adjusted: $10.4 billion, $5.28 per share), against $3.5 billion a year earlier. Sales were $65.2 billion. Oil and gas production was 3.98 million barrels of oil equivalent a day, slightly lower than a year before because of the conflict in the Middle East and contract effects of higher prices; US output stayed above 2 million barrels a day. Cash flow from operations was $16.0 billion and free cash flow $12.0 billion; the company spent $2.5 billion on buybacks and $3.5 billion on dividends and cut its debt by $3.5 billion in the quarter.

For the full year 2025 cash flow from operations was $33.9 billion and free cash flow $16.6 billion. Chevron cut $1.5 billion of costs in 2025 and aims for $3–4 billion of structural savings by the end of 2026.

## The stock's story

Chevron's roots go back to an oil company founded in California in 1879, which later became Standard Oil of California after the break-up of Rockefeller's Standard Oil. It took the name Chevron in 1984 after buying Gulf Oil, then merged with Texaco in 2001, bought Unocal in 2005, Noble Energy in 2020 and PDC Energy in 2023. The biggest deal, Hess, was completed in July 2025 after an arbitration fight with ExxonMobil over Hess's stake in Guyana's huge oil fields. Mike Wirth has been CEO since 2018, and the company moved its headquarters from California to Houston, Texas.

The share price has followed oil: it fell hard in 2008, in 2014–2016 and in 2020, when the pandemic crushed fuel demand and oil prices collapsed, and it rose strongly in 2021–2022 when energy prices surged after Russia's invasion of Ukraine. In 2026 the conflict in the Middle East pushed oil prices up again and lifted Chevron's profits.

## Competitors

Its closest rivals are ExxonMobil, Shell, BP, TotalEnergies and ConocoPhillips, plus state-owned giants such as Saudi Aramco. In US shale it competes with independent producers like EOG Resources and Diamondback Energy. In the long run, electric cars and renewable energy compete with its products.

## Main risks

- **Oil prices:** when oil falls, profit and cash flow fall fast — 2025 profit was about 30% lower than 2024's.
- **Geopolitics:** big assets in Kazakhstan, Venezuela, Israel's gas fields and the Middle East region are exposed to wars, sanctions and political decisions.
- **Big projects and deals:** cost overruns at projects like Tengiz in Kazakhstan, or overpaying for acquisitions, can destroy value.
- **Energy transition:** if demand for oil peaks sooner than expected, the value of reserves could shrink; climate rules and lawsuits add costs.
- **Dividend pressure:** buybacks are the first thing to be cut in a downturn, and a long slump would strain the dividend too.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Chevron is a cyclical stock: it can rise or fall by a third or more within a couple of years as oil moves. Buying a fixed amount regularly helps here, because you automatically buy more shares when oil and the share price are low. The dividend has grown for 39 years in a row and, if reinvested, adds noticeably to the long-term result. Many people hold an oil company as one part of a wider portfolio rather than on its own. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
