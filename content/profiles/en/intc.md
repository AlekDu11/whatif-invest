**Intel** makes the processors inside a huge share of the world's PCs and servers, and unlike most chip companies it also builds chips in its own factories. After its worst year ever in 2024, it staged a sharp comeback in 2025–2026: revenue rose 21% to $16.5 billion in Q3 2026, and in April 2026 the stock finally beat its record from August 2000.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Q3 2026 revenue: $16.5 billion, +21% a year; data centre and AI chips grew 78% to $7.3 billion.
> - The stock lost about 61% in 2024, then gained 84% in 2025 and broke its 2000 closing record of $74.88 in 2026.
> - The US government owns about 9.9% after an $8.9 billion investment in August 2025; Nvidia put in $5 billion and SoftBank $2 billion.
> - Q4 2026 outlook is weaker: revenue $15–16 billion and a gross margin of about 37%, down from 43%.

## How it makes money

Intel has two sides. **Intel Products** designs and sells chips: the client group (laptop and desktop processors such as Core) brought $8.1 billion in Q3, down 5%, while **Data Center and AI** (Xeon server processors and related chips) jumped 78% to $7.3 billion. In October 2026 CEO Lip-Bu Tan said the top task was making enough Xeon chips to meet demand.

The other side is **Intel Foundry**, the factory business, which made $6.7 billion in Q3, up 58%. Most of it is Intel building its own chips, so it largely cancels out in group revenue; the goal is to win outside customers.

In the quarter to 26 September 2026 Intel earned $16.5 billion in revenue with a 43.0% gross margin. Net profit was $3.3 billion ($0.60 per share), down 18% from a year earlier; on the adjusted basis Intel prefers, earnings per share more than doubled to $0.52. It completed a $23 billion share sale, ending the quarter with $21.8 billion in cash plus $32.2 billion in short-term investments, against $50.1 billion of debt. For Q4 it expects $15–16 billion of revenue and GAAP earnings of just $0.10 a share. It pays no dividend.

## The stock's story

At the height of the dot-com boom Intel was the world's second most valuable company, and its shares closed at a record $74.88 on 31 August 2000. Then came a wait of more than 25 years. In 2024 the stock dropped about 61%, its worst year since listing in 1971: Intel cut 15,000 jobs and the board pushed out CEO Pat Gelsinger on 1 December 2024.

The turn came in 2025: a new CEO, Lip-Bu Tan, plus cash from the US government, Nvidia and SoftBank. The stock gained 84% that year and passed its 2000 record in April 2026 as AI demand lifted server sales. In the twelve months to mid-November 2026 it traded between $32.89 and $142.35.

## Competitors

In processors Intel competes with AMD and with Arm-based chips from Apple, Qualcomm and the cloud giants. In AI accelerators the leader is Nvidia. In contract manufacturing its rivals are TSMC, the clear leader, and Samsung.

## Main risks

- **Margins are falling again:** the Q4 outlook cuts the gross margin from 43% to about 37%, and Intel expects PC demand to stay weak at least through the first half of 2027.
- **Foundry still has to prove itself:** its main customer is still Intel itself, and the strategy depends on winning big outside clients.
- **More shares, more debt:** the $23 billion share sale spread profits over more shares, and debt stands at about $50 billion.
- **Politics:** with the US government as a top shareholder, decisions may follow industrial policy, not only shareholders' interests.
- **The stock ran fast:** from about $33 to over $140 within a year, which leaves room for sharp pullbacks.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Intel is a reminder that even a market leader can disappoint for decades: a person who bought at the 2000 peak waited about 26 years just to get back to that price. Buying regularly through the bad years meant also buying at much lower prices. The flip side: a single company can stay down for a very long time, so many people hold it as one part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
