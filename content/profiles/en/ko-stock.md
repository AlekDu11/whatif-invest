**Coca-Cola** sells far more than Coke: Sprite, Fanta, Coca-Cola Zero Sugar, smartwater, Powerade, Minute Maid, fairlife milk, Costa coffee and about 200 other brands in more than 200 countries. Most of the bottles are filled not by Coca-Cola itself but by independent bottlers — the company mainly sells them concentrate and runs the brands and the marketing, which is why its profit margins are so high. Founded in Atlanta in 1886, it is one of the best-known dividend stocks in the world.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: $47.9 billion in 2025, +2%; organic revenue (without currency and deals) +5%; earnings per share $3.04.
> - In the July–September 2026 quarter revenue rose 7% to $13.4 billion and unit case volume grew 5%, with Coca-Cola Zero Sugar up 16%.
> - Coca-Cola had raised its dividend for 63 years in a row by early 2026 and paid $8.8 billion of dividends in 2025.
> - Henrique Braun replaced James Quincey as CEO on 31 March 2026; Quincey became executive chairman.

## How it makes money

Coca-Cola earns in two ways. The core is the "concentrate" business: it makes the syrups and bases, owns the brands, pays for advertising and sells to bottling partners such as Coca-Cola HBC, Coca-Cola FEMSA or Coca-Cola Europacific Partners, which produce, bottle and deliver the drinks to shops. This side needs few factories and earns a high margin. The second part is its own bottling operations (in India, parts of Africa and elsewhere): they bring a lot of revenue but much thinner margins, so the company tends to sell them to partners over time — in 2026 it was selling its African bottler. By drink type, sparkling soft drinks are the biggest group, followed by water, sports drinks, coffee and tea, then juice, dairy and plant-based drinks.

In the third quarter of 2026 (to 2 October) net revenue grew 7% to $13.4 billion and organic revenue also grew 7%. Volume rose 5%, prices and mix added 1%, and the operating margin was 33.4% against 32.0% a year earlier. Earnings per share were $0.93 (comparable EPS $0.91, +11%). Latin America was the fastest-growing region, with net revenue up 25%. In the first nine months of 2026 the company generated $11.4 billion of free cash flow. It then raised its 2026 forecast to at least 5% organic revenue growth, at least 11% comparable EPS growth and at least $13.0 billion of free cash flow.

For the full year 2025 operating margin was 28.7% (comparable 31.2%). Free cash flow was only $5.3 billion because of a one-off $6.1 billion payment linked to the purchase of fairlife; without it, it was $11.4 billion.

## The stock's story

The drink was invented by pharmacist John Pemberton in Atlanta in 1886. In 1988 Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway began buying the shares and became one of the largest holders — a stake it has kept for decades. In the late 1990s the stock was priced for fast growth; it peaked in 1998 and then took well over a decade to climb back as sales of sugary sodas slowed. Since then Coca-Cola has spread into coffee (it bought Costa in 2019), sports drinks (BodyArmor in 2021) and milk (full control of fairlife in 2020). Unit case volume was flat in 2025, and in 2026 the business picked up speed again. In March 2026 longtime COO Henrique Braun, who joined the company in 1996, took over as CEO.

## Competitors

The main rival is PepsiCo, which also owns snacks like Lay's and Doritos. Keurig Dr Pepper, Monster Beverage (in which Coca-Cola holds a stake), Red Bull, Nestlé, Danone and many local brands compete in water, energy, coffee and juice. In many countries supermarket own-brand drinks and new "healthier" soda brands also take shelf space.

## Main risks

- **Health and sugar:** sugar taxes, labelling rules and new weight-loss drugs can reduce how many sugary drinks people buy.
- **Currencies:** most sales are outside the US, so a strong dollar reduces reported results.
- **Slow growth:** it is a mature company; a high share price only makes sense if it keeps growing a few percent a year.
- **Brand write-downs:** in late 2025 it wrote down the BodyArmor trademark by $960 million — bought brands do not always work.
- **Taxes and lawsuits:** a long-running dispute with the US tax authority (IRS) over profits earned abroad could cost billions.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Coca-Cola is a classic "defensive" stock: people buy drinks in good years and bad, and the company pays a rising dividend. That does not mean the price cannot fall — after its 1998 peak it took more than ten years to recover. Buying a fixed amount regularly spreads your purchases over good and bad prices, and reinvesting the dividends makes a big part of the long-term return. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
