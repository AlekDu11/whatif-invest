**Visa** is the world's largest card payment network. When you tap a Visa card or phone, its VisaNet system checks the payment and moves it between your bank and the shop's bank in a fraction of a second. Visa does not lend money or issue cards itself — banks do — it earns a small fee on each payment. Based in San Francisco, it had $45.9 billion of revenue and about $23.5 billion of profit in the fiscal year to 30 September 2026.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Fiscal 2026 (to 30 September 2026): net revenue $45.9 billion, up 15%, and net income of about $23.5 billion — roughly half of every dollar of revenue is profit.
> - It processed 281.3 billion transactions in fiscal 2026, up 9%; cross-border volume grew 13%.
> - In the July–September 2026 quarter revenue was $12.1 billion (+13%) on about $4.1 trillion of payments volume (+9%).
> - The quarterly dividend was raised 16% to $0.78 per share, paid on 1 December 2026.

## How it makes money

Visa sits in the middle between the bank that gave you the card and the bank of the shop that accepts it. It charges both sides small fees: service fees based on how much money moves over its cards, data-processing fees for each transaction it handles, and international fees when you pay abroad or at a foreign online shop — the most profitable part. On top come extra services such as fraud checks and Visa Direct, which sends money between people and businesses ($1.5 trillion in fiscal 2026, +23%). Visa pays part of its gross revenue back to big banks and merchants as incentives to keep their cards. Because it never lends, it carries no credit risk: if a cardholder does not pay, the bank loses, not Visa.

## The stock's story

Visa began in 1958 as BankAmericard, a credit card that Bank of America tested in Fresno, California. Other banks joined, took over the network in the 1970s and renamed it Visa in 1976. The company listed on the New York Stock Exchange on 19 March 2008 with an offering of nearly $18 billion — then the largest US IPO — in the middle of the financial crisis. In 2016 it bought Visa Europe and reunited the brand. Contactless cards and phone payments sped up the move away from cash, while the 2020 pandemic briefly crushed cross-border travel spending. In September 2024 the US Justice Department sued Visa, accusing it of monopolising debit payments; the case is still running. Ryan McInerney has been CEO since February 2023.

## Competitors

Its closest rival is [Mastercard]({lang}/ma/), which works the same way, followed by [American Express]({lang}/axp/), which issues its own cards. China UnionPay dominates in China, and national instant-payment systems — UPI in India, Pix in Brazil — let people pay straight from their bank accounts. Payment companies such as [PayPal]({lang}/pypl/), [Block]({lang}/xyz/) and [Adyen]({lang}/adyen/) and, more recently, stablecoins also compete for parts of the payment chain.

## Main risks

- **Rules on fees:** regulators in the US, Europe and elsewhere can cap the fees charged on card payments, and US lawmakers have proposed opening card routing to rival networks.
- **Lawsuits:** the US debit case and long-running merchant claims cost money every quarter ($474 million set aside in the last one).
- **Bank-to-bank payments:** systems like UPI and Pix, and possibly stablecoins, could take everyday payments away from cards.
- **Economy and travel:** fees follow spending; a recession or a drop in travel hits volumes, and cross-border fees most of all.
- **High price tag:** the shares usually trade at a high multiple of profits, so disappointing growth can mean a sharp fall.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Visa is one of the steadiest growers among large companies, but its shares still lost more than a third of their value in 2008 and again in early 2020 before recovering. Buying a fixed amount on a schedule means you pick up more shares in those dips and fewer when the price is high, without guessing the right moment. A single stock is usually held as one part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
