**Graham Holdings** is the company that used to own The Washington Post. After it sold the newspaper to Jeff Bezos in 2013, it kept the rest and turned into a small conglomerate: the education company Kaplan, seven local TV stations, home health and hospice care, car dealerships, manufacturers and a few media brands such as Slate and Foreign Policy. The Graham family still controls it, and it is often called a "mini-Berkshire".

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: $3.86 billion in the first nine months of 2026, +6% on the year; operating income $227.0 million, +21%.
> - Education (Kaplan) is still the biggest division, but its revenue fell 14% in the September 2026 quarter.
> - Television broadcasting earned $48.5 million of operating income in that quarter, up 81%, helped by election advertising.
> - In nine months of 2026 the company bought back 176,529 of its own shares for $198.6 million.

## How it makes money

Graham Holdings is a collection of different businesses under one roof. In the September 2026 quarter **Education** (Kaplan: test prep, higher-education services, language schools and international colleges) brought in $404.3 million of revenue. **Automotive** — car dealerships in the Washington, D.C. area — added $299.1 million, **Healthcare** (home health, hospice and other care services) $260.3 million, **Manufacturing** (companies such as Hoover Treated Wood, Dekko, Joyce/Dayton and Forney) $147.6 million, and **Television broadcasting** (Graham Media Group, with stations in Houston, Detroit, Orlando, San Antonio, Jacksonville and Roanoke) $125.3 million. "Other businesses", including Slate and Foreign Policy, made $89.4 million but lost money.

The TV stations are small in revenue but the most profitable part: they earned more operating income in that quarter than Kaplan. The company also holds a large portfolio of stocks and other investments, so its reported net income can swing with the stock market.

In the September 2026 quarter revenue was $1.33 billion, up 4% from $1.28 billion a year earlier, and operating income rose 27% to $85.5 million. Net income fell to $75.1 million, or $17.60 per share, from $122.9 million a year earlier, mainly because the company's stock investments lost $15.0 million instead of gaining $84.8 million. For the first nine months net income was $385.3 million, lifted by a one-off pension gain.

At the end of September 2026 the company had $1.25 billion in cash, marketable securities and other investments, against $853.4 million of borrowings. It had 4,185,665 shares outstanding — very few, which is why one share costs well over a thousand dollars.

## The stock's story

The Graham family bought The Washington Post in 1933, and the company went public in 1971, shortly before the Post's Watergate reporting made it famous. Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway bought a big stake in 1973, and Buffett sat on the board for years. From the 1980s the company bought Kaplan, which grew into its largest business. In 2013 it sold the Post to Jeff Bezos for $250 million and renamed itself Graham Holdings; Tim O'Shaughnessy, Don Graham's son-in-law, became chief executive in 2015. In October 2026 Kaplan completed the sale of Dublin Business School for $127.5 million.

## Competitors

Each division has its own rivals. Kaplan competes with other test-prep and education companies such as [Pearson](/en/pson/); the TV stations compete with local broadcasters like [Nexstar Media](/en/nxst/) and with streaming and online ads; the dealerships compete with big chains such as [AutoNation](/en/an/), [Lithia Motors](/en/lad/) and [Penske Automotive](/en/pag/). As a family-controlled holding company, Graham is often compared with [Berkshire Hathaway](/en/brk-b/), although it is much smaller.

## Main risks

- **Kaplan's international business:** it depends on foreign students, and its revenue fell 22% in the September 2026 quarter; tighter student-visa rules in countries like the UK and Australia can hurt it further.
- **Local TV:** audiences are moving to streaming, and advertising swings between election and non-election years.
- **Many unrelated businesses:** a conglomerate is harder to analyse, and investors sometimes value the whole at less than the sum of its parts.
- **Market swings in its investments:** gains and losses on the stock portfolio make reported profit jump around.
- **Few shares, low trading:** with about 4.2 million shares and family control, the stock trades thinly and outside shareholders have little say.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Graham Holdings is a mix of steady, cash-producing businesses and some that are shrinking, so the share price can stay flat for years and then move sharply. Its high price per share also means that buying a small fixed amount regularly may require a broker that sells fractional shares.
