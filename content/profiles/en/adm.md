**Archer Daniels Midland (ADM)** buys crops from farmers — soybeans, corn, wheat, canola — stores and ships them around the world, and turns them into cooking oil, animal feed, sweeteners, starch, ethanol and food ingredients. You rarely see its name in a shop, but its products sit inside bread, soft drinks, pet food and fuel tanks. Founded in Minneapolis in 1902, it is based in Chicago and had 41,496 employees at the end of 2025.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: $80.3 billion in 2025, down 6% on 2024; adjusted earnings per share fell 28% to $3.43.
> - In Q3 2026 net earnings jumped to $821 million from $108 million, helped by much better soybean crushing margins.
> - ADM raised its 2026 adjusted EPS outlook to about $5.50–5.80, up from $3.60–4.25 in February.
> - It raised its dividend in 2026 for the 53rd year in a row, to 52 cents a quarter.

## How it makes money

ADM earns a small margin on enormous volumes, in three segments. **Ag Services and Oilseeds** buys, stores, transports and trades crops and crushes soybeans and other oilseeds into meal for animal feed and oil for food and biodiesel. It was the biggest profit engine in 2025, with $1.61 billion of segment operating profit. **Carbohydrate Solutions** mills corn and wheat into sweeteners, starches, flour and ethanol ($1.21 billion). **Nutrition** sells flavours, plant proteins, probiotics and animal feed ingredients ($417 million). Soybeans alone were 17% of 2025 revenue, corn 13% and soybean meal 11%. ADM also owns 22.5% of Wilmar International, a Singapore-listed palm oil and food giant, and books its share of Wilmar's profit.

Because ADM sits between farmers and food and fuel makers, its profit depends less on crop prices than on the gap between what crops cost and what their products sell for — the "crush margin" for soybeans, for example. Government biofuel rules matter a lot, because they set how much vegetable oil and ethanol end up in fuel.

In the quarter to 30 September 2026 revenue was $22.3 billion, up from $20.4 billion a year earlier. Net earnings were $821 million, or $1.70 per share, against $108 million a year before, when the quarter carried $261 million of impairment and restructuring charges. Adjusted EPS nearly doubled to $1.77. Total segment operating profit rose 64% to $1.38 billion: crushing earned $311 million after just $13 million a year earlier, as the US biofuel quotas finalised in March 2026 lifted North American margins. Ethanol also improved, while the core grain-trading business and human nutrition earned a little less.

For nine months, operating cash flow was $1.79 billion, ADM paid $764 million in dividends and bought back $358 million of shares (it bought none in 2025). On 30 September 2026 it had $711 million of cash against $1.16 billion of short-term and $7.58 billion of long-term debt. Its 2026 outlook is adjusted EPS of about $5.50–5.80 and capital spending of $1.2–1.3 billion.

## The stock's story

ADM began in 1902 when George Archer and John Daniels started crushing linseed in Minneapolis, and it grew into one of the world's largest crop traders and processors. Like most commodity businesses, its share price has moved in cycles: on prices adjusted for dividends it fell about 37% in 2008 and 28% in 2015, then rose about 37% in 2021 and 40% in 2022, when the war in Ukraine pushed crop prices and processing margins up. The record monthly close was {{ath:adm}} ({{athd:adm}}).

Then margins fell back and trust took a hit. In January 2024 ADM disclosed an internal investigation into its Nutrition segment, withdrew that segment's outlook and put its finance chief on leave; the shares fell 24% the next trading day. The SEC later alleged that the former CFO shifted profit from other segments into Nutrition through intersegment pricing; ADM restated segment figures (consolidated earnings were not affected) and in January 2026 agreed to pay $40 million to settle, without admitting or denying wrongdoing. On adjusted prices the stock lost about 20% in 2023 and 28% in 2024, before recovering in 2025 and 2026 as crush margins improved.

## Competitors

ADM is one of the "ABCD" companies that dominate world grain trading, together with Bunge, Cargill and Louis Dreyfus (the last two are privately owned). In corn sweeteners and starches it competes with Ingredion, Tate & Lyle and Cargill; in flavours and nutrition ingredients with IFF, Kerry and Givaudan; in ethanol with Valero, POET and Green Plains.

## Main risks

- **Margins it does not control:** profit swings with crush and ethanol margins, harvests, weather and trade flows — 2025 adjusted EPS fell 28% before 2026 rebounded.
- **Government policy:** US and Brazilian biofuel rules drive a big part of crushing profit; a delay in Brazil's policy already pressured South American margins in Q3 2026.
- **Trade wars:** tariffs and disputes, especially between the US and China over soybeans, can reshape where crops go.
- **Governance:** the 2024 accounting probe was settled with the SEC, but the former CFO said in January 2026 that he would fight the SEC's fraud charges, and trust is slow to rebuild.
- **Thin margins, big volumes:** on $80 billion of revenue, a small change in costs or prices moves profit a lot.

## What it means if you buy regularly

ADM is a long-established, dividend-paying company, but its stock is cyclical: years of 30–40% gains have been followed by years of 20–30% falls. Buying a fixed amount regularly means you buy more shares in the weak years and fewer in the strong ones, without trying to guess where crop margins go next; reinvested dividends add to the result over long periods. Many people hold one company only as part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
