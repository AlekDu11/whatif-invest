**Microsoft** is the company behind Windows, Office (now Microsoft 365), Teams, LinkedIn and Xbox — but most of its money now comes from Azure, its cloud: rented computing power that businesses use to run websites, store data and, more and more, run AI. If your company's email, files or AI assistant live "in the cloud", there is a good chance part of that bill goes to Microsoft.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: $92.3 billion in the quarter to 30 September 2026, +19% on the year; operating income $45.9 billion.
> - Azure grew 48%; the whole Microsoft Cloud brought in $63.9 billion, +30%.
> - Contracted future cloud revenue (commercial remaining performance obligation) reached $677 billion, +73%.
> - The stock fell more than 33% from its July 2025 peak to June 2026, then rebounded close to its highs by November.

## How it makes money

Since July 2026 Microsoft reports two segments. **Agents and Infra** — $76.8 billion last quarter, +25% — holds Azure, Microsoft 365 for businesses, server software, Dynamics, LinkedIn and other business tools. **Devices and Consumer** — $15.5 billion, −3% — covers Windows sold with new PCs, Microsoft's own devices, Xbox and Bing search advertising. In other words, about five of every six dollars now come from businesses paying for cloud and software subscriptions. Microsoft also says Azure alone made about $100 billion in fiscal 2026, up from $75 billion a year earlier.

## The numbers right now

In the first quarter of fiscal 2027 (July–September 2026) Microsoft earned $92.3 billion in revenue and $36.6 billion in net income, or $4.92 per share (+32%). The prior-year quarter included a $3.1 billion loss tied to its OpenAI investment, so without those effects earnings per share rose 18% to $4.89. Microsoft 365 commercial cloud grew 17%, search and advertising excluding traffic acquisition costs grew 12%, while Xbox content fell 2% and Windows OEM and devices fell 22%. The company returned $10.2 billion to shareholders through dividends and buybacks in the quarter.

## The stock's story

Microsoft's share price peaked at about $555 intraday on 31 July 2025. Then investors started to worry about how much it was spending on AI data centres: capital spending hit a record $37.5 billion in the last quarter of 2025, and about 45% of the cloud order book was tied to a single customer, OpenAI. On 29 January 2026 the stock dropped about 12% in a day, wiping out roughly $400 billion of value, and by June 2026 it traded near $356–367 — more than a third below the peak. Strong results in July 2026 turned the mood: the shares jumped about 18% in a week, and after the October results they were back around $545, near their 52-week high.

## Competitors

In the cloud Microsoft competes with Amazon Web Services and Google Cloud, plus Oracle and specialised AI clouds. In office software and AI assistants it faces Google Workspace and Gemini, and AI tools from OpenAI and Anthropic. Xbox competes with Sony PlayStation and Nintendo; Bing competes with Google Search. Its advantages are millions of business customers already on its software and the ability to sell them everything in one contract.

## Main risks

- **The AI spending bill:** Microsoft is pouring tens of billions of dollars a quarter into data centres and chips; if AI demand cools, those costs stay.
- **Customer concentration:** a large share of the cloud order book depends on one customer, OpenAI.
- **Capacity limits:** shortages of chips and power have already slowed Azure growth once, in late 2025.
- **Competition and pricing:** Amazon and Google fight hard for the same AI customers, which can squeeze margins.
- **Regulation:** as one of the biggest software and cloud suppliers, Microsoft is a constant target for competition authorities in the US and Europe.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Microsoft shows that even a very profitable giant can fall by a third in less than a year when investors change their minds. Regular buying meant purchasing more shares during that drop and fewer near the highs, but nobody knew in advance how long the fall would last. Many people hold a single stock only as part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
