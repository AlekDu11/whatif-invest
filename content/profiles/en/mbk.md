**mBank** is one of Poland's biggest banks and the one many Poles know from their phone: it started as an internet-only bank in 2000 and by mid-2026 served more than 6 million clients with over PLN 300 billion (about $75 billion) in assets. After years of losses from old Swiss-franc mortgages, it posted record profits in 2025 and 2026.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Net profit: a record PLN 1.085 billion in Q3 2026, +29.6% a year; PLN 3.1 billion in the first nine months (+23.8%).
> - Swiss-franc mortgage costs fell 83% to PLN 78 million in the quarter, after more than PLN 3.1 billion in 2022 alone.
> - Lending grew fast in Q3 2026: gross loans PLN 164.4 billion (+17.7%), deposits PLN 254.7 billion (+19%).
> - It lost money in 2021 and 2022 because of franc loans, then earned a record PLN 3.54 billion in 2025.

## How it makes money

Like any bank, mBank mostly earns the difference between what it charges on loans and what it pays on deposits. Its net interest margin was 3.57% in Q3 2026. **Retail banking** is the larger half: PLN 91.5 billion of loans, mostly mortgages — mBank is one of Poland's top three mortgage lenders, with PLN 16.6 billion of new home loans in January–September 2026 and a 9.7% market share. **Corporate banking** adds PLN 66.5 billion of loans to companies, plus payments, currency and trading services. It also earns on cards and brokerage fees, and in the Czech Republic and Slovakia. Costs were a lean 31.6% of income (normalised) in nine months.

In the first nine months of 2026 mBank booked a record PLN 9.8 billion of revenue and PLN 3.098 billion of net profit. Its return on tangible equity was 21.3% — it earned about 21 zł a year for every 100 zł of shareholders' capital. The total capital ratio was 15.3% at the end of September 2026. That was despite Poland raising banks' income tax from 19% to 30% in 2026. In July 2026 CEO Cezary Kocik said mBank planned to pay a dividend from 2026 profit — 30% of it — the first in a long time; it paid no dividend for 2025.

## The stock's story

Founded in 1986 as BRE Bank, it came under Germany's Commerzbank in 2003 and took the mBank name in 2013. Between 2002 and 2014 it granted more than 70,000 mortgages linked to the Swiss franc. When the franc soared, borrowers sued, courts often sided with them, and mBank set aside billions: it lost money in 2021 and 2022 (PLN 703 million in 2022). In dollar terms the monthly close fell about 86% from its October 2007 peak to October 2020. Settlements with borrowers — more than 34,000 by mid-2026, with about 4,300 court cases still open then — and fast loan growth turned it around: 2025 brought a record PLN 3.54 billion profit, and by November 2026 the share price in dollars was about 14 times its October 2020 low.

## Competitors

mBank competes with PKO Bank Polski, Bank Pekao, Santander Bank Polska, ING Bank Śląski, Millennium, Alior and apps such as Revolut. Its strengths are a strong app, young digital clients and low costs; its weak spot was a bigger franc-mortgage book than most peers.

## Main risks

- **Franc-loan tail:** costs are much lower, but thousands of cases are still in court, and there are also 1,399 suits over the "free credit" sanction (borrowers claiming loans should be interest-free over paperwork errors).
- **Interest rates:** when the Polish central bank cuts rates, the margin on loans usually shrinks.
- **Taxes and politics:** Polish governments have repeatedly put extra burdens on banks — the 2022 "credit holidays" cost mBank about PLN 1.3 billion, and the tax rate jumped to 30% in 2026.
- **Owner change:** Commerzbank owns 69% of mBank, and UniCredit is moving towards control of Commerzbank; a new owner could change plans for mBank, its dividends or a sale.

## What it means if you buy regularly

mBank shows how long a bank can stay down: in dollar terms it took about 13 years to fall from the 2007 peak to the 2020 low before the recovery began. Buying through the bad years meant cheap shares that later rose, but nobody knew in advance the franc-loan problem would ease. A single bank is tied to one country's economy and politics, so many hold it only as a small part of a portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
