**GE Aerospace** builds jet engines for airliners, fighter jets and helicopters — including the LEAP, which it makes with France's Safran in their CFM joint venture and which powers the Boeing 737 MAX and many Airbus A320neo jets. Most of its money comes later, from decades of spare parts and repairs for about 50,000 commercial and 30,000 military engines in service. It is what remained of the old General Electric after it split into three companies.

> **Key takeaways**
> - In 2025 adjusted revenue was $42.3 billion, operating profit $9.1 billion (a 21.4% margin) and adjusted EPS $6.37.
> - In the September 2026 quarter orders rose 49% to $19.0 billion and adjusted EPS rose 36% to $2.25.
> - In October 2026 it raised its 2026 outlook: adjusted revenue up about 19–20%, adjusted EPS $8.00–8.10, free cash flow $9.1–9.4 billion.
> - Larry Culp has run the company since October 2018; GE Aerospace has stood alone since April 2024, after GE HealthCare and GE Vernova were spun off.

## How it makes money

There are two businesses. **Commercial Engines & Services** sells engines for passenger and cargo planes and then services them; in the September 2026 quarter it had revenue of $10.3 billion and operating profit of $2.9 billion. **Defense & Propulsion Technologies** makes engines for military jets and helicopters plus other aircraft parts: $3.5 billion of revenue and $475 million of profit in that quarter. New engines are often sold at thin margins; the real profit comes from shop visits and parts over an engine's 30-year life. The company says its commercial services backlog is more than $170 billion.

In the quarter to 30 September 2026 total revenue was $14.1 billion, up 16%, and adjusted revenue $13.3 billion, up 18%. Operating profit rose 30% to $3.0 billion, a 22.5% margin. In the same quarter GE Aerospace agreed to buy Consolidated Precision Products, a maker of engine castings, to ease a supply bottleneck.

## The stock's story

General Electric was founded in 1892 and built America's first jet engine in the 1940s. Around 2000 it was one of the most valuable companies in the world. Its finance arm, GE Capital, nearly sank it in the 2008 crisis, when Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway invested $3 billion. A collapse in the power business followed in 2017–2018: GE cut its dividend to a cent a share and was removed from the Dow Jones index in June 2018. Larry Culp took over in October 2018, cut debt and broke the group up — GE HealthCare was spun off in January 2023 and GE Vernova in April 2024, leaving GE Aerospace. As air travel recovered after the pandemic and demand for engine repairs grew, the shares rose many times over from their 2020 lows.

## Competitors

In airliner engines GE competes with Pratt & Whitney (part of RTX), whose GTF engine rivals the LEAP on the A320neo, and with Rolls-Royce in wide-body jets. Safran is both a partner (CFM) and a rival in other parts. In military engines Pratt & Whitney is again the main rival. Independent repair shops compete for service work.

## Main risks

- **Aircraft makers:** engine sales depend on how fast Boeing and Airbus can build planes, and both have had production problems.
- **Supply chain:** shortages of castings, forgings and other parts limit how many engines GE can deliver.
- **Engine durability:** if engines need repairs sooner than planned, costs and customer complaints rise.
- **Air travel shocks:** a pandemic, war or recession cuts flying hours, and the services business shrinks with them, as in 2020.
- **High expectations:** after a long rally a lot of growth is already in the share price.

## What it means if you buy regularly

GE Aerospace is a cyclical stock: it can fall hard when people stop flying, as it did in 2020. Buying a fixed amount on a schedule means you buy more shares when the price drops and fewer when it is high, without trying to guess the next downturn. Many people hold one company only as part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
