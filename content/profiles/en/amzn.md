**Amazon** is the online store most people know, but that is only half the story. It also rents computing power to other companies through Amazon Web Services (AWS), sells ads on its shop pages, runs Prime subscriptions with video and music, and makes devices like Kindle, Echo and Ring. Founded by Jeff Bezos in 1994 in Seattle, it has been on the Nasdaq since 1997; Andy Jassy has been CEO since 2021.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Sales: $201.9 billion in Q3 2026, +12% a year; AWS alone grew 39% to $46.0 billion.
> - AWS brought in $17.8 billion of the $28.1 billion operating profit — about 63% — on less than a quarter of sales.
> - Amazon expects about $220 billion of capital spending in 2026, mostly AI data centres; free cash flow over the last 12 months was minus $42.4 billion.
> - Operating margin hit 13.9% in Q3 2026, the highest Amazon has ever reported.

## How it makes money

The shop is huge but thin-margined. North America brought in $112.9 billion in Q3 2026 (+6%) and International $43.1 billion (+5%). Two quieter businesses matter more for profit: advertising — brands paying to appear at the top of search results — made $19.6 billion (+11%), and subscriptions such as Prime made $14.0 billion (+11%). AWS rents servers, storage, databases and AI tools to companies of every size; it is now growing faster than at any time in recent years as businesses train and run AI models. Amazon also builds its own AI chips (Trainium) and a satellite internet service, Amazon Leo, which it says should start initial service by the end of 2026.

## The numbers right now

In the quarter to 30 September 2026 Amazon earned $28.1 billion of operating income, up from $17.4 billion a year earlier. Net income was $20.7 billion, 2% lower, but only because the year-earlier quarter included $9.5 billion of gains on its stake in Anthropic. Operating cash flow over 12 months rose 29% to $168.3 billion, yet spending on property and equipment jumped 82% to $210.7 billion, so free cash flow turned negative. Long-term debt climbed to $173.1 billion from $65.6 billion at the end of 2025; cash and marketable securities were about $114 billion. For Q4 2026 Amazon guides to $240–247 billion of sales and $28.5–33.5 billion of operating income.

## The stock's story

Amazon is a classic survivor. In the dot-com bust of 2000–2001 its shares lost more than 90% of their value; investors doubted an online bookstore could ever make money. It then grew for two decades, and AWS turned it into a profit machine. In 2022 the stock roughly halved as pandemic shopping faded and costs ballooned. In February 2026 it dropped about 11% after hours when Amazon announced around $200 billion of 2026 spending; it later recovered and set new records in 2026 as AWS growth sped up. Since October 2025 the company has also cut well over 30,000 corporate and retail jobs.

## Competitors

In shopping Amazon fights Walmart, Temu, Shein, Alibaba and, in Latin America, MercadoLibre. In the cloud its rivals are Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud, and in online ads Google and Meta. Its advantages are a giant delivery network, Prime loyalty and the largest share of the cloud market.

## Main risks

- **The AI bill:** about $220 billion of capital spending in a single year has to pay off; if AI demand cools, those data centres earn less and free cash flow stays negative longer.
- **More debt:** long-term debt almost tripled in nine months, and interest expense rose to $1.7 billion a quarter from $0.5 billion.
- **Regulation:** Amazon faces antitrust and consumer-protection cases in the US and Europe over how it treats sellers and shoppers.
- **Thin retail margins:** the shop grows only 5–6% a year in its main regions and is exposed to tariffs, wages and price wars with Temu and Walmart.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Amazon has rewarded patient owners, but the path included a 90%+ crash and a 50% fall. Buying a fixed sum each month spreads your entry across good and bad years, and a company this large moves less wildly than smaller tech names — though it can still drop sharply on news like a spending surprise. Many people hold it as one part of a wider portfolio rather than on its own. Past results don't guarantee future ones.
