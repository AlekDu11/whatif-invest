**Vale** digs iron ore out of the ground in Brazil and ships it around the world — mostly to China, where it is turned into steel for buildings, cars and machines. It is one of the world's biggest iron ore miners alongside Rio Tinto and BHP, and it also mines copper and nickel through its unit Vale Base Metals. The shares trade in São Paulo as VALE3 and in New York as VALE.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: $10.6 billion in July–September 2026, +2% on the year; proforma EBITDA $3.5 billion (−20%); net income $1.26 billion (−53%).
> - Vale sold 86.3 million tonnes of iron ore and pellets in the quarter, but its iron ore fines fetched an average $85.1 a tonne, 10% less than a year earlier.
> - Copper and nickel earned $1.1 billion of EBITDA, +61% on the year — about a third of the group's total.
> - Expanded net debt was $16.4 billion at the end of September 2026; $1.7 billion went to shareholders as dividends and interest on capital during the quarter.

## How it makes money

Most of Vale's profit comes from iron ore. It sells "fines" (crushed ore) from its giant mines in Carajás in the Amazon state of Pará and in Minas Gerais, plus pellets — small balls of ore that steelmakers pay extra for because they pollute less. The price is set by world markets and depends above all on how much steel China makes. Vale's advantage is cost: in July–September 2026 its cash cost was about $23 a tonne for fines, so it stays profitable even when prices drop. The second business, Vale Base Metals, mines copper in Carajás and nickel in Canada and Indonesia; copper prices were strong in 2026, which is why this unit grew fast.

In July–September 2026 Vale produced 88.4 million tonnes of iron ore, 6% less than a year earlier: output at the older Serra Norte mines fell 31%, while the newer Serra Sul (S11D) had a record quarter of 23.8 million tonnes. Vale said it expected 2026 iron ore output at the low end of its 335–345 million tonne range. Copper output was 85.5 thousand tonnes and nickel 49.7 thousand tonnes; for 2026 it guided to the upper half of 360–380 thousand tonnes of copper and 185–200 thousand tonnes of nickel. Free cash flow was $2.2 billion in the quarter, and net debt was 0.8 times a year of EBITDA. In October 2026 Vale Base Metals sold its Thompson nickel mine in Canada, keeping an 18.9% stake.

## The stock's story

Vale was created by the Brazilian government in 1942 as Companhia Vale do Rio Doce and was privatised in 1997. In 2006 it bought the Canadian nickel miner Inco, and the shares soared in the 2000s as China's construction boom pushed iron ore prices up. When that boom faded, iron ore fell below $40 a tonne in late 2015 and the shares lost most of their value. Two dam disasters shaped the company: in November 2015 the Fundão tailings dam of Samarco, Vale's joint venture with BHP, burst near Mariana, killing 19 people; in January 2019 Vale's own dam at Brumadinho collapsed, killing 270 people, and the shares fell about a quarter in one day. In October 2024 Vale, BHP and Samarco signed a R$170 billion settlement with Brazilian authorities for Mariana; by 30 September 2026 R$83.8 billion had been paid out. Gustavo Pimenta has been chief executive since late 2024.

## Competitors

In iron ore Vale competes with Australia's Rio Tinto, BHP and Fortescue, and from late 2025 with the new Simandou mine in Guinea. In copper and nickel its rivals include Freeport-McMoRan, Glencore, Anglo American and Indonesian nickel producers backed by Chinese companies.

## Main risks

- **China:** most of Vale's ore goes to Chinese steel mills; a weak Chinese property market means lower iron ore prices.
- **Price swings:** a fall of $10 a tonne in iron ore wipes out billions of dollars of yearly profit.
- **Dam safety and legal costs:** Vale still carried about $3.8 billion of provisions for Brumadinho and Samarco at the end of September 2026.
- **New supply:** Simandou and other new mines could push iron ore prices lower.
- **Brazil:** politics, taxes and the real's exchange rate affect profits and dividends.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Vale is a low-cost miner that pays big dividends in good years, but its shares follow iron ore prices, which rise and fall in long cycles. Buying a fixed amount regularly spreads your purchases across those cycles, so you buy more shares when prices are low. Dividends from miners can be cut sharply when prices fall. Many people hold one commodity stock only as part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
