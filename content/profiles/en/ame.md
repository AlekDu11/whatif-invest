**Ametek** makes the precise instruments and small motors you never see but that keep things running: sensors in aircraft engines, analysers in chemical plants and labs, power-quality monitors for utilities, test equipment for chip makers and medical devices. Founded in 1930 and based in Berwyn, Pennsylvania, it has traded on the New York Stock Exchange since 1984 and has grown largely by buying niche market leaders.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Record sales of $2.22 billion in Q3 2026, +17% a year (+10% organic, without acquisitions).
> - Orders jumped 35% to $2.65 billion and the backlog hit a record $4.54 billion.
> - Ametek paid $5.0 billion in cash for Indicor Instrumentation in August 2026; total debt rose to about $6.7 billion.
> - 2026 adjusted earnings guidance was raised to $8.53–8.57 a share, about 15% above 2025.

## How it makes money

Ametek has two groups. Electronic Instruments (EIG), about two-thirds of sales, makes measuring and testing gear for process industries, aerospace, power, labs, semiconductors and medical equipment — $1.44 billion in Q3 2026 (+16%). Electromechanical (EMG) makes precision motors, motion-control parts, specialty metals and interconnects — $778 million (+20%). Its products are usually a small part of a customer's total cost but essential to the operation, and replacement parts, consumables and service bring repeat revenue. The strategy is simple: buy leaders in narrow niches, plug them into Ametek's global platform and raise their margins.

## The numbers right now

In the quarter to 30 September 2026 Ametek earned an adjusted operating margin of 26.8% and adjusted earnings of $2.21 a share (+17%). Free cash flow was $527 million, 124% of net income. Over the last 12 months revenue was about $8.19 billion with a profit margin near 20%. For Q4 2026 the company expects sales to grow in the low 20% range, helped by Indicor, which should add about $350 million to 2026 sales. The market values Ametek at about $54 billion — roughly 33 times its yearly profit, a premium price for an industrial company.

## The stock's story

Ametek is known as a steady compounder rather than a rocket, but it is not immune to downturns: the stock fell about 35% in 2008 during the financial crisis and slipped about 4% in 2022. In 2026 it started the year near $200, reached a record of $261.16 on 13 August and closed at $248.58 on 3 November, up about 22% for the year. Over the past 52 weeks it traded between about $189 and $261. David A. Zapico is chairman and CEO.

## Competitors

Ametek competes business by business with other industrial technology groups: Roper Technologies, Fortive, Keysight, Emerson, Thermo Fisher in some lab instruments, and many specialised private firms. Its edge is depth in small niches where few rivals bother to compete and customers rarely switch suppliers.

## Main risks

- **More debt:** borrowing for Indicor lifted debt to about 2.3 times EBITDA (a measure of operating profit), higher than Ametek usually carries, and interest costs rise with it.
- **Integration:** the promised Indicor savings of 10–12% of its sales still have to be delivered.
- **Industrial cycle:** a slowdown in factories, energy, chips or aerospace would cut orders quickly.
- **Valuation:** at about 33 times earnings, a profit disappointment could hit the share price hard.
- **Acquisition appetite:** further large deals could delay paying down debt.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Ametek pays a small dividend ($1.36 a share a year, well under 1% of the price), so most of the result for a regular buyer comes from the share price. Its history shows long steady growth with occasional sharp drops, and buying every month means some purchases land near highs and some near lows. As a single stock, it is usually held as one part of a broader portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
