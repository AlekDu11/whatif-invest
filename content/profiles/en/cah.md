**Cardinal Health** is one of the three big companies that move medicines from drug makers to American pharmacies, hospitals and clinics. It buys the drugs, stores them in huge warehouses and delivers them every day, keeping only about one cent of each dollar of sales as profit. It also makes and distributes medical supplies such as gloves and syringes, and runs smaller, more profitable businesses in nuclear medicine and home healthcare.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: $254.2 billion in fiscal 2026 (to 30 June), +14%; GAAP earnings $7.23 per share, $11.26 on the company's adjusted basis.
> - Distribution of medicines brought in $234.8 billion of revenue, about 92% of the total, but a profit of only $2.78 billion.
> - In the September 2026 quarter revenue rose 5% to $67.5 billion, and the company raised its fiscal 2027 adjusted earnings forecast to $12.80–13.00 per share.
> - It paid $491 million in dividends and $1.36 billion for buybacks in fiscal 2026.

## How it makes money

The **Pharmaceutical and Specialty Solutions** segment buys branded, generic and specialty drugs and delivers them to pharmacies such as CVS, hospitals and doctors' offices; in fiscal 2026 it had revenue of $234.8 billion and a segment profit of $2.78 billion. Specialty drugs for cancer, eye and gut diseases and its own networks of specialist doctors' practices are the fastest-growing part. **Global Medical Products and Distribution** ($12.7 billion) sells Cardinal-brand gloves, gowns, syringes and other supplies to hospitals. The "Other" businesses ($6.8 billion) — nuclear medicine for scans, at-home supplies such as diabetes equipment, and shipping services — are small but earned $707 million, with far higher margins than drug distribution.

In the September 2026 quarter (the first of fiscal 2027) revenue was $67.5 billion, up 5% from a year earlier. GAAP earnings rose 27% to $2.38 per share and adjusted earnings 28% to $3.26. Pharmaceutical profit grew 17% to $783 million, while medical products profit fell 28% to $33 million. Operating cash flow was $1.40 billion, and the company completed a $500 million buyback. In fiscal 2026 net cash from operations was $5.17 billion.

## The stock's story

Robert Walter founded the company in 1971 in Ohio as a food distributor; it moved into drugs soon after and grew through dozens of acquisitions. In 2017 it bought Medtronic's patient-care supplies business for about $6.1 billion, a deal that later disappointed and led to large write-downs. In the opioid crisis, Cardinal Health, McKesson and AmerisourceBergen (now Cencora) agreed in 2021 to a national settlement of about $21 billion over 18 years. Jason Hollar became CEO in 2022 and refocused the company on specialty drugs, buying networks of cancer, gut-disease and urology practices in 2024–2025, including GI Alliance and Solaris Health. Profit grew strongly and the stock rose to records in 2025 and 2026.

## Competitors

McKesson and Cencora are its two direct rivals; together the three handle most US drug distribution, and they compete hard for the biggest customers. In medical supplies Cardinal faces Medline, Owens & Minor and cheaper imports. In specialty practices and home care it meets McKesson again, plus insurers such as UnitedHealth's Optum.

## Main risks

- **Very thin margins:** it keeps about 1% of revenue as profit, so a small rise in costs or a lost price fight matters a lot.
- **Big customers:** a few pharmacy chains and buying groups bring a large share of sales; it relies on renewals, such as its CVS contracts.
- **Drug pricing policy:** changes to how US drug prices are set or paid could squeeze distributors.
- **Medical products:** this segment has struggled for years with costs, tariffs and foreign exchange.
- **Legal costs:** opioid payments continue for years, and new lawsuits are possible.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Cardinal Health is a steady, cash-producing business, but its stock has also had long bad stretches when one acquisition or a legal problem went wrong. Buying a fixed amount on a schedule spreads your entry price over good and bad years without guessing the timing. Many people hold one company only as part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
