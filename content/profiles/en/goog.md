**Alphabet** is the parent company of Google. It owns Google Search, YouTube, Android, Chrome, Gmail, Maps and the Gemini AI assistant, and it rents computing power and AI models to businesses through Google Cloud. Most of what you use from Google is free — you "pay" by seeing ads, and advertisers pay Alphabet.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: $127.6 billion in Q3 2026, +25% on the year — a run rate of about half a trillion dollars a year.
> - Google Cloud more than doubled: $30.7 billion, +103%, with $11.1 billion of operating income.
> - Capital spending was $51.4 billion in one quarter, more than cash from operations ($42.3 billion), and management expects it to rise "significantly" in 2027.
> - The stock traded above $400 in spring 2026 but fell more than 10% after the Q3 results.

## How it makes money

Ads are still the core. In Q3 2026 Google Search and related products earned $63.4 billion, YouTube ads $11.2 billion and the Google Network (ads Google places on other websites and apps) $7.5 billion. Subscriptions, platforms and devices — YouTube Premium and Music, Google One storage, the Play store and Pixel phones — added $14.3 billion. Google Cloud, which sells AI chips, Gemini models and data tools to companies, brought in $30.7 billion. Other Bets, such as the self-driving unit Waymo, are still tiny at $372 million of revenue.

## The numbers right now

In the quarter to 30 September 2026 operating income was $40.6 billion, up 30%, with an operating margin of about 32%. Net income fell to $26.5 billion, mainly because of an $8.7 billion loss on equity investments, which cut earnings per share to $2.12. The Gemini app passed one billion users, and YouTube Music and Premium passed 150 million subscribers including trials.

The AI build-out is changing how Alphabet uses cash. It spent $51.4 billion on equipment and data centres in the quarter, so free cash flow was negative at −$9.1 billion. It bought back no shares, issued $12.4 billion of new stock and raised $28.7 billion in bonds, while still paying a quarterly dividend of $0.22 per share. It ended September with $265.1 billion in cash and marketable securities.

## The stock's story

Our price history for Alphabet is split-adjusted. In September 2025 Judge Amit Mehta ruled on remedies in the US search monopoly case: Google could keep Chrome but must share some search data with rivals and may not sign exclusive search deals. Investors were relieved, and the stock rose about 57% over the next four months, taking Alphabet past $4 trillion in market value in January 2026. The shares climbed above $400 in spring 2026, then sold off after the Q3 results in October, when Search and YouTube growth disappointed, falling more than 10% in after-hours trading to about $290.

## Competitors

In search and AI assistants Alphabet competes with OpenAI's ChatGPT, Microsoft's Bing and Copilot, and Perplexity. In ads its rivals are Meta, Amazon and TikTok. Google Cloud competes with Amazon Web Services and Microsoft Azure. YouTube competes with TikTok, Netflix and other streaming services. Its advantages are billions of users, its own AI chips and models, and one of the strongest balance sheets in the world.

## Main risks

- **AI could change search:** if people ask chatbots instead of searching, the ads that pay for Google could shrink; Q3 Search growth of 12% already worried investors.
- **Spending without a guarantee:** capital spending now exceeds operating cash flow, and the company is issuing shares and debt to fund it.
- **Antitrust:** the search remedies limit Google's deals, Google plans to appeal, and the case could run into 2027 or later.
- **Investment swings:** gains and losses on stakes in other companies make reported profit jump around from quarter to quarter.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Alphabet moved from a record to a sharp drop within a few months in 2026, which is normal for a large tech stock. Buying a fixed amount regularly spreads your purchases across those highs and lows, but it cannot protect you if the business itself weakens. Many people hold a single stock only as part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
