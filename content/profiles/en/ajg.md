**Arthur J. Gallagher & Co.** is one of the world's largest insurance brokers. It does not carry insurance risk itself: it helps businesses and people find and buy insurance and other benefits, and gets paid a commission or a fee for it. A second business, Gallagher Bassett, handles insurance claims for companies and insurers. Based near Chicago, it works with clients in about 130 countries.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: $13.78 billion in 2025, up 21%; net earnings $1.50 billion, or $5.74 per diluted share.
> - In 2025 Gallagher completed 33 mergers, the biggest being AssuredPartners, bought in August 2025 for about $13.8 billion.
> - In the September 2026 quarter revenue was $3.93 billion, +18%, with organic growth of 5%.
> - The company paid a dividend of $0.70 per share for the September 2026 quarter, up from $0.65.

## How it makes money

Gallagher has two main segments. **Brokerage** — about 88% of revenue in 2025 ($12.19 billion) — places property, liability, workers' compensation, health and employee-benefit cover for businesses, from small firms to large corporations, and also works as a reinsurance broker for insurers. It earns commissions from insurers (a share of each premium) and fees from clients, plus extra "supplemental" and "contingent" commissions that insurers pay for volume and profitable business. **Risk Management** ($1.59 billion in 2025), mainly Gallagher Bassett, handles claims for companies that insure themselves and for insurance companies, and is paid fees.

Because brokers are paid a percentage of premiums, Gallagher benefits when insurance prices rise and when clients' payrolls and property values grow. It also grows by buying hundreds of smaller local brokers over the years.

## The stock's story

Arthur James Gallagher started the brokerage in Chicago in 1927. It stayed a family business for decades, went public in the 1980s and is still led by a member of the family: J. Patrick Gallagher Jr., the founder's grandson, became chief executive in 1995. Over the following 30 years the company grew mostly by acquisitions, buying small brokers in the US, the UK, Australia and elsewhere; in 2021, for example, it bought Willis Re, the reinsurance brokerage of Willis Towers Watson. The shares rose steadily for most of that time and reached a high in 2025. They then fell back in 2025–2026 as organic growth slowed from 2025's pace and investors worried that insurance prices were softening.

## Competitors

Gallagher's biggest rivals are the other global brokers: [Marsh McLennan](/en/mrsh/), [Aon](/en/aon/) and [Willis Towers Watson](/en/wtw/). In the US it also competes with [Brown & Brown](/en/bro/), private brokers such as Hub International and Acrisure, and thousands of local agencies. In claims handling it competes with firms such as Sedgwick and Crawford.

## Main risks

- **Insurance prices:** commissions are a share of premiums; when insurance gets cheaper, growth slows even if Gallagher wins clients.
- **Acquisitions:** growth depends heavily on buying brokers; overpaying, or failing to integrate a deal as big as AssuredPartners, could hurt profits.
- **Debt:** big purchases are partly financed with borrowing, which adds interest costs.
- **Competition and technology:** online platforms and new software could squeeze commissions on simpler policies.
- **Economy:** a recession reduces payrolls, revenues and property values that premiums are based on.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Gallagher is a fairly steady business — companies have to buy insurance in good years and bad — and its stock has moved less than the overall market for long stretches. Still, it can fall for a year or more when insurance prices soften, as in 2025–2026. Buying a fixed sum regularly spreads your entry points over time instead of trying to time the market, and the quarterly dividend adds a small income. Many people hold one company only as part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
