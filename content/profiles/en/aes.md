**AES** builds and runs power plants and electricity networks: solar and wind farms, giant batteries, gas plants and two regulated utilities in Indiana and Ohio that keep the lights on for about 1.1 million customers. Much of its newest power goes to tech companies feeding data centres. In March 2026 AES agreed to be bought by a group of infrastructure investors, so its days as a listed stock are almost over.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Buyout price: $15.00 in cash per share; the deal values AES at about $33.4 billion including debt.
> - Final regulatory approval (FERC) came on 17 November 2026; closing is expected on or about 2 December 2026.
> - Q3 2026 revenue: about $3.87 billion, up roughly 16% a year.
> - The stock never got back to its October 2000 peak of $70.63 — it fell 98.7% by October 2002.

## How it makes money

AES earns money in two main ways. First, it sells electricity from its own plants under long-term contracts (PPAs — power purchase agreements) to utilities, factories and, more and more, big tech firms: by early 2026 it had 8.2 GW of signed data-centre deals and 11.1 GW of renewable projects in its contracted backlog. Second, its regulated utilities, AES Indiana and AES Ohio, charge households and businesses tariffs approved by state regulators. More than half of its new solar projects come with battery storage, so the power can be sold after sunset too.

## The numbers right now

In the quarter to 30 September 2026 AES reported revenue of about $3.87 billion, up around 16% year on year, and net income of $553 million, or $0.76 per share. Over the first nine months of 2026 revenue reached about $10.5 billion, up 15%. The business carries a lot of debt, most of it tied to individual projects rather than the parent company — typical for builders of power plants, but it makes AES sensitive to interest rates.

The buyers are a consortium led by Global Infrastructure Partners (part of BlackRock) and EQT's infrastructure fund, joined by CalPERS and the Qatar Investment Authority. The $15 price was a 40.3% premium to the average price before takeover rumours surfaced in July 2025. Shareholders and the other regulators had already said yes; FERC was the last approval. AES has also declared a final "stub" dividend of $0.06369 per share for shareholders of record on the last trading day (expected 1 December 2026), payable on 7 December.

## The stock's story

AES was a star of the late-1990s power boom and set a record of $70.63 on 2 October 2000. In the 2001–2002 energy-sector bust it lost 98.7% and bottomed in October 2002. It recovered only partly over the following two decades. After a peak in 2022, the price more than halved by the end of 2025 as higher interest rates made its debt-funded build-out costlier — which is what made AES cheap enough to attract a takeover.

## Competitors

In renewables AES competes with NextEra Energy, Brookfield Renewable and other large developers for land, grid connections and tech-company contracts. Its Indiana and Ohio utilities are regulated local monopolies, so there the real counterweight is the state regulator that sets tariffs.

## Main risks

- **Deal timing:** closing on about 2 December is expected but not guaranteed; if a closing condition fails, the price would no longer be anchored to $15.
- **Capped upside:** once the deal closes, holders get exactly $15 per share in cash — any future growth goes to the private owners.
- **Debt and rates:** heavy project debt makes the business sensitive to borrowing costs.
- **Contract and project risk:** data-centre demand drives growth, but delays in building plants or a slowdown in tech spending would hurt.

## What it means if you buy regularly

AES is about to stop trading: after closing, shares turn into $15 in cash and leave the New York Stock Exchange, so regular buying in it ends. Anyone who bought monthly over the past decade paid a mix of prices — some above $15, some below — which the line above shows. The broader lesson: a single stock can drop 98% and never fully come back, and a buyout fixes the final price for everyone. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
