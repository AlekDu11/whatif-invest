**Aflac** sells "supplemental" insurance: policies that pay cash straight to you when you get cancer, have an accident or go to hospital, on top of whatever your main health cover pays. It does this in two countries — Japan and the United States — and Japan is where it sells cancer and medical cover to millions of families. The company is led by chairman and CEO Daniel P. Amos.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Adjusted earnings: $2.43 per share in Q3 2026, down 2.4% a year; GAAP net earnings fell to $0.61 per share.
> - Premiums split almost evenly: about $1.5 billion a quarter from Japan and $1.5 billion from the US.
> - Dividend raised for 44 years in a row; $1.1 billion returned to shareholders in Q3 alone.
> - The stock hit a record $129.55 in July 2026 — but in 2008–2009 it fell 83% and took until 2016 to recover.

## How it makes money

Aflac collects regular premiums and invests the money until claims are paid. It earns when claims and costs stay below premiums plus investment income. **Aflac Japan** sells cancer, medical, nursing-care and whole-life policies; in Q3 2026 its net earned premiums were ¥240 billion (about $1.5 billion). **Aflac U.S.** sells accident, disability, cancer, critical-illness, hospital, dental and vision cover, mostly through employers at the workplace; its premiums were also about $1.5 billion. Products are sold through agencies, banks and independent sales associates. Because half the business is in yen, the exchange rate moves reported dollar results.

## The numbers right now

In the quarter to 30 September 2026 total revenue fell 31% to $3.3 billion. That drop mostly reflects investment losses Aflac booked while repositioning its bond portfolio, not fewer customers — which is why net earnings fell to $303 million ($0.61 per share) from $1.6 billion a year earlier. Adjusted earnings, which strip such items out, were $2.43 per share, beating the analyst consensus of $1.80. The yen averaged 158.49 per dollar, 6.8% weaker than a year before, and cut $0.03 off adjusted EPS.

The two halves moved in opposite directions. In Japan, premiums slipped 2.0% in yen, sales fell 4.0% and the pretax margin dropped to 44.4% from 52.2% as claims rose. In the US, premiums grew 3.3%, sales rose 3.5% and the pretax margin improved to 27.7% from 21.7%. Aflac spent $804 million buying back shares and $305 million on dividends in the quarter, and declared a Q4 dividend of $0.61 per share.

## The stock's story

Aflac is a classic slow-and-steady stock, but it has had deep falls. During the 2008–2009 financial crisis, when investors feared for insurers' investment holdings, the price fell 83.2% from April 2008 to March 2009, and it took until April 2016 to regain that high. In 2019–2020 it fell 55.6% into the Covid crash, recovering by June 2021. Since then it has climbed to a record close of $129.55 on 28 July 2026.

## Competitors

In Japan Aflac competes with domestic life insurers such as Nippon Life, Dai-ichi Life and Japan Post Insurance, and with foreign players such as MetLife. In US workplace benefits its rivals include Unum (with its Colonial Life brand), MetLife and Guardian. Aflac's strengths are its brand, its long lead in Japanese cancer insurance and a large sales force.

## Main risks

- **The yen:** half of earnings come from Japan, so a weaker yen shrinks dollar results.
- **Japan is mature:** an ageing, shrinking population makes growth there hard; Japan sales fell 4.0% in Q3 2026.
- **Claims trends:** the Japan benefit ratio jumped to 49.2% from 39.3% in a year, showing how quickly margins can move.
- **Data security:** a 2025 US breach affected nearly 22.7 million people, and a June 2026 hack at Aflac Japan exposed data of about 4.38 million customers and agents.
- **Interest rates and investments:** results depend on a roughly $100 billion investment portfolio.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Aflac pays a growing dividend and tends to move less than tech stocks, but it is not risk-free: it once lost more than 80% and needed seven years to recover. Regular buying spreads purchases over good and bad years, and reinvested dividends add up over time — still, it is one company tied heavily to one country's currency. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
