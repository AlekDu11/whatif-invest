**Mastercard** is the world's second-largest card payment network after Visa. Like Visa, it does not issue cards or lend money: banks do that, and Mastercard runs the network that connects your bank with the shop's bank, earning a small fee on each payment — plus a fast-growing business selling fraud protection, data and cybersecurity services. Based in Purchase, New York, it had $32.8 billion of revenue and $15.0 billion of profit in 2025.

> **Key takeaways**
> - 2025: net revenue $32.8 billion and net income $15.0 billion.
> - July–September 2026 quarter: revenue $9.8 billion (+14%), net income $4.6 billion (+18%), earnings per share $5.28 (+22%).
> - About $3.0 trillion was spent on Mastercard cards in that quarter (+7.5% in local currencies); cross-border volume grew 11% and 49.3 billion transactions passed through its network.
> - Value-added services grew 19% to about $4.1 billion — around 41% of revenue.

## How it makes money

Mastercard has two engines. The payment network earns fees based on the money spent with its cards, on each transaction it switches and, most profitably, on cross-border payments; in the September 2026 quarter this brought in $5.7 billion (+11%) after the rebates and incentives it pays banks and big merchants to win their cards ($6.4 billion, +18%). The second engine, value-added services and solutions ($4.1 billion, +19%), sells fraud prevention, data analytics, cybersecurity, loyalty programmes and open-banking tools to banks, shops and governments. Because it never lends, credit losses fall on the banks, not on Mastercard.

## The stock's story

Mastercard started in 1966, when a group of California banks formed the Interbank Card Association to compete with Bank of America's BankAmericard (today's Visa). Its card was called Master Charge from 1969 and was renamed MasterCard in 1979. In 2002 it merged with Europe's Europay. It listed on the New York Stock Exchange on 25 May 2006 at $39 a share, then split its shares 10-for-1 in 2014. Over the years it has fought many cases over card fees, including a €570 million EU fine in 2019. In 2024 it bought the threat-intelligence firm Recorded Future to grow its security business. Michael Miebach has been CEO since January 2021, after Ajay Banga left to lead the World Bank.

## Competitors

Its main rival is [Visa]({lang}/v/), which is bigger and works the same way, followed by [American Express]({lang}/axp/), which issues its own cards. China UnionPay leads in China, and instant bank-to-bank systems such as UPI in India and Pix in Brazil take a growing share of everyday payments. Payment firms like [PayPal]({lang}/pypl/), [Block]({lang}/xyz/) and [Adyen]({lang}/adyen/) and stablecoins compete for parts of the chain — which is why Mastercard is buying into stablecoin infrastructure itself.

## Main risks

- **Rules on fees:** regulators can cap card fees, and US lawmakers have proposed letting merchants route card payments over rival networks.
- **Lawsuits:** merchant claims in the US and UK, including large ones from Block and Intuit, can lead to settlements and provisions.
- **Bank-to-bank payments:** UPI, Pix and similar systems, and possibly stablecoins, could replace cards for everyday spending.
- **Economy and travel:** fees follow spending; a recession or a drop in travel hits volumes, cross-border fees most of all.
- **High price tag:** the shares usually trade at a high multiple of profits, so slower growth can mean a sharp fall.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Mastercard has grown its profits for most of the years since its 2006 listing, but its shares still fell by more than a third in 2008 and again in early 2020. Buying a fixed amount on a schedule spreads your entry price over those swings, so you buy more shares when fear pushes the price down. A single stock is usually held as one part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
