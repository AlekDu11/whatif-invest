**Ares Management** invests other people's money — mostly from pension funds, insurers, sovereign funds and, more and more, wealthy individuals — and charges a fee for it. Its speciality is private credit: lending directly to mid-sized companies instead of going through a bank or the bond market. Founded in Los Angeles in 1997, it managed $690.6 billion of assets at the end of September 2026.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Assets under management: $690.6 billion on 30 September 2026, +16% on a year earlier; about 65% of it is credit.
> - Fee related earnings (profit from steady management fees): $1.78 billion in 2025 and $524.2 million in Q3 2026, +11% on the year.
> - In 2025 Ares raised a record $113.2 billion of new money from clients and invested $145.8 billion.
> - Ares paid a dividend of $1.35 per share each quarter in 2026, declared again with the Q3 results.

## How it makes money

Ares does not lend or invest mainly with its own cash. Clients commit money to its funds, and Ares earns a yearly **management fee** on the part of that money that is invested or committed — its "fee-paying assets", $416.4 billion at the end of September 2026. Those fees ($3.86 billion in 2025) are the core of the business and are fairly predictable, because much of the money is locked up for years. About $233.9 billion sits in "perpetual capital" — vehicles with no fixed end date, such as listed lending funds and insurance accounts.

On top of that Ares can earn **performance fees** (a share of the profit, often called carried interest) when funds beat a target return. These are bigger in good years and can shrink in bad ones.

The money is spread over four main groups. Credit is by far the largest at $446.2 billion: direct loans to companies, liquid credit and asset-backed lending. Real Assets (real estate and infrastructure, including data centres and warehouses) had $161.6 billion, Secondaries (buying stakes in other funds from investors who want to sell) $46.3 billion and Private Equity $24.5 billion. Ares also manages Ares Capital (ARCC), one of the largest listed business development companies — funds that lend to mid-sized US firms and are traded on the stock exchange. In 2025 Ares had over 4,250 employees in more than 55 offices in over 25 countries.

In the quarter to 30 September 2026 management fees rose 10% to $1.07 billion and fee related earnings rose 11% to $524.2 million, with a margin of 42.5% (41.4% a year earlier). After-tax realized income, the profit measure Ares itself uses for dividends, was $1.26 per share against $1.19. Profit under standard accounting (GAAP) was much smaller — $85.3 million, or $0.21 per share — because it includes non-cash items and changes in the value of investments.

Clients committed $31.0 billion of new money in the quarter, and Ares invested $34.8 billion. It also had $173.8 billion of "dry powder" — money promised by clients but not yet invested — and $117.1 billion of assets that will start paying fees once invested.

## The stock's story

Ares listed on the New York Stock Exchange in May 2014. For years it was the smaller cousin of Blackstone, KKR and Apollo, but private credit grew fast after banks pulled back from lending to mid-sized companies, and Ares grew with it. On 1 March 2025 it closed the purchase of GCP International, the international arm of GLP Capital Partners, adding logistics real estate and data centres. On 11 December 2025 it joined the S&P 500, replacing Kellanova.

Then came a sharp reversal. In the first three months of 2026 the shares fell 31.6%: investors feared that AI would hurt the software companies Ares lends to, and that clients would pull money from its funds. Ares answered that software is a small part of its portfolio and that its loans sit first in line for repayment. The record month-end close on WhatIfStack is {{ath:ares}} ({{athd:ares}}). In October 2026 Ares Capital agreed to buy MidCap Financial Investment Corporation for an enterprise value of about $2.6 billion.

## Competitors

Its closest rivals are the other big alternative asset managers: Blackstone, Apollo, KKR, Blue Owl, Carlyle, Brookfield and TPG. In private credit it also competes with banks, Golub Capital and HPS (now part of BlackRock). BlackRock itself is pushing hard into private markets.

## Main risks

- **Credit losses:** if a recession or AI disruption makes many borrowers fail, fund returns fall, performance fees shrink and clients may raise less money.
- **Outflows from wealthy clients:** funds for individual investors allow regular withdrawals; a wave of redemption requests can slow growth.
- **Sentiment swings:** as Q1 2026 showed, fear about private credit can cut the share price by a third even while fees keep rising.
- **Competition for deals:** more money chasing the same loans can push down the interest Ares earns.
- **Accounting gap:** GAAP profit is far below the company's own measures, which makes the stock harder to judge for beginners.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Ares grew fees steadily for years, but its share price has swung hard — down 31.6% in a single quarter in 2026. Buying a fixed amount regularly means you buy more shares when the price falls and fewer when it rises, without trying to guess the bottom. The dividend is paid every quarter and can be reinvested. One company is usually held as part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
