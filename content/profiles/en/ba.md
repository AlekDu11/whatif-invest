**Boeing** builds passenger jets — the 737, 767, 777 and 787 — plus fighter jets, military helicopters, satellites and space systems, and it earns steady money servicing planes after they are sold. With Airbus it is one of only two makers of big airliners in the world. After the 737 MAX crashes and a 2024 safety scare, the company is rebuilding production under CEO Kelly Ortberg.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: $24.2 billion in the September 2026 quarter, +4%; net loss $116 million, against a $5.3 billion loss a year earlier.
> - Order backlog of $745 billion at the end of September 2026, including more than 6,300 airliners worth $612 billion.
> - 475 airliners delivered in the first nine months of 2026, up from 440 a year earlier.
> - The 737-10 was certified in October 2026; the bigger 777X is due for first delivery in 2027.

## How it makes money

Boeing has three businesses. Commercial Airplanes sells jets to airlines and leasing firms; in the September 2026 quarter it had revenue of $10.7 billion but still lost $697 million on operations. Defense, Space & Security builds military aircraft, weapons and satellites for the US and allies: revenue $7.9 billion, up 15%. Global Services sells spare parts, maintenance, training and upgrades for planes already flying: revenue $5.6 billion and operating profit of $1.0 billion — today the most profitable part of the company. Airlines pay most of a jet's price when it is delivered, so the number of deliveries drives Boeing's cash.

In the first nine months of 2026 Boeing had revenue of $71.0 billion, up 8%, and a net loss of $551 million — much smaller than the $6.0 billion loss a year earlier. In the third quarter it delivered 161 airliners (124 of them 737s and 22 787s), generated $1.5 billion of operating cash flow and $0.5 billion of free cash flow, after about $0.7 billion of US Justice Department settlement payments. At the end of September it held $20.7 billion in cash and securities against $45.9 billion of debt. Boeing has paid no dividend since 2020.

## The stock's story

William Boeing founded the company in Seattle in 1916. The 707 opened the jet age in 1958 and the 747 "jumbo" followed in 1970; in 1997 Boeing merged with rival McDonnell Douglas. Its worst crisis began with two 737 MAX crashes in October 2018 and March 2019 that killed 346 people; the plane was grounded worldwide for about 20 months. In January 2024 a door panel blew off an Alaska Airlines 737 MAX 9 in flight, and US regulators capped 737 production. Kelly Ortberg became CEO in August 2024, a seven-week machinists' strike followed, and Boeing raised more than $20 billion of new shares to repair its balance sheet. In December 2025 it completed the purchase of Spirit AeroSystems, the main maker of 737 fuselages, to bring quality control back in-house. In 2026 the US Navy picked Boeing to build its next carrier fighter, the F/A-XX.

## Competitors

In airliners Boeing's real rival is [Airbus](/en/air/), which has delivered more jets than Boeing every year since 2019; China's COMAC is a small newcomer with the C919. In defense Boeing competes with [Lockheed Martin](/en/lmt/), [Northrop Grumman](/en/noc/), [General Dynamics](/en/gd/) and [RTX](/en/rtx/). [GE Aerospace](/en/ge/) is a key partner: it co-makes the engines on every 737 MAX.

## Main risks

- **Quality and regulators:** another safety incident could halt production again; raising the 737 rate needs approval from the US Federal Aviation Administration.
- **Debt:** $45.9 billion of debt limits room for mistakes, and the company still loses money on commercial jets.
- **Delays:** the 777X was first meant to fly with airlines around 2020; any new slip costs billions.
- **Fixed-price defense contracts:** several military programmes have already caused large losses.
- **The airline cycle:** recessions, pandemics, trade disputes and Chinese orders can change demand fast.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Boeing shows that a famous company can spend years below its old record: big problems at one product line can weigh on the stock for a long time. Buying a fixed amount regularly means you buy more shares when the price is low and fewer when it is high, without trying to guess when the recovery comes. Many people hold one company only as part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
