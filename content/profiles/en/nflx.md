**Netflix** is the world's biggest paid streaming service: you pay a monthly fee and watch series, films, documentaries, live sports and even games on any screen. It started in 1997 in California renting DVDs by post, switched to streaming in 2007 and now makes its own shows in dozens of languages. It has been on the Nasdaq since 2002 and is run by co-CEOs Ted Sarandos and Greg Peters.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: $12.9 billion in Q3 2026, +12% a year; operating margin 34.6%, up from 28.2%.
> - Netflix expects $51.1 billion of revenue in 2026 (+13%) and about $12.0 billion of free cash flow.
> - It walked away from a deal to buy Warner Bros. in February 2026 and collected a $2.8 billion break-up fee.
> - In 2022 the stock fell about 75% from its late-2021 high; it then rallied to a new month-end high in June 2025 and slid through 2026, by our price data.

## How it makes money

Almost all revenue comes from subscriptions. Netflix raises prices every so often and says members took the 2026 increases about as expected. The cheaper plan with ads is a growing second leg: Netflix now expects about $2.7 billion of ad revenue in 2026, down from about $3 billion it hoped for earlier, and plans to launch the ad tier in 15 more countries from March 2027. Live events bring in new viewers and advertisers: in Q4 2026 it streams NFL games on Thanksgiving Eve and Christmas Day and the Tyson Fury vs Anthony Joshua fight. By region, the US and Canada gave $5.63 billion in Q3, Europe, the Middle East and Africa $4.11 billion, Latin America $1.60 billion (+17%) and Asia-Pacific $1.54 billion.

## The stock's story

Netflix has crashed hard before. In 2022 it reported losing subscribers for the first time in a decade, and by our data the stock fell about 75% from its late-2021 high to mid-2022. Password-sharing crackdowns, the ad plan and live sport then drove a long rally to the June 2025 peak. In December 2025 Netflix agreed to buy the Warner Bros. studio and other entertainment assets for an enterprise value of $82.7 billion; Paramount Skydance outbid it, and in February 2026 Netflix declined to raise its offer. The shares bounced, but slid through 2026 as investors worried about slower growth in viewing hours, a cut ad-revenue forecast and fierce competition — even as profits hit records.

## Competitors

Netflix's biggest rival for screen time is YouTube. Among paid services it faces Disney+, Amazon Prime Video, Apple TV, Comcast's Peacock and Paramount+, which will be joined by HBO Max once Paramount completes its Warner Bros. Discovery deal. TikTok and video games also compete for the same evenings. Netflix's edge: a global audience and a huge, steady content budget.

## Main risks

- **Viewing time:** Netflix itself acknowledges investor concern about view-hour growth; if people watch less, price rises get harder.
- **Ads behind plan:** the 2026 ad-revenue forecast was cut by about $0.3 billion because some ad tools rolled out slower than expected.
- **Bigger rivals:** a combined Paramount and Warner Bros. would own a deep film and TV library and could bid up content and sports rights.
- **Slower growth:** revenue growth of about 12% is healthy but slower than earlier years, and the stock's valuation reacts strongly to any miss.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Netflix shows that a growing, profitable business can still see its stock halve — twice in four years. Buying a fixed amount regularly means you buy more shares when the price is low, but there is no guarantee a fall reverses. Many people keep a single stock like this as a small part of a broader portfolio. Past results don't guarantee future ones.
