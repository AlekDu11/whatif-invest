**Inditex** is the Spanish company behind **Zara** — and also Pull&Bear, Massimo Dutti, Bershka, Stradivarius, Oysho, Zara Home and Lefties. It is the world's largest fashion retailer by sales. Its secret is speed: new designs reach its roughly 5,400 stores and websites in weeks, not months, so it sells more at full price and is left with fewer unsold clothes. Its shares trade in Madrid under the ticker ITX and are among the biggest in Spain's IBEX 35 index.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Sales: €39.9 billion in fiscal 2025 (to 31 January 2026), +3.2% (+7% at constant exchange rates); net profit €6.2 billion, +6%.
> - Zara (with Zara Home and Lefties) brought in €28.1 billion, about 70% of all sales; online sales were €10.7 billion.
> - In February–July 2026 sales grew 7.6% to €19.8 billion and net profit 6.8% to €2.98 billion.
> - The company had about €11 billion of net cash and paid a dividend of €1.75 per share for 2025.

## How it makes money

Inditex designs clothes in its headquarters in Arteixo near A Coruña, has a large part of them made close to home — in Spain, Portugal, Morocco and Turkey — and ships them through its own logistics hubs twice a week to stores in dozens of markets. Because it orders small batches and repeats what sells, it rarely needs big discounts. It owns most of its stores and runs one shared online platform for all brands. In fiscal 2025 Zara and its sister formats made €28.1 billion, Bershka €3.3 billion, Stradivarius €3.0 billion, Pull&Bear €2.6 billion, Massimo Dutti €2.0 billion and Oysho €0.96 billion. About half of sales came from Europe outside Spain, 16% from Spain, 18% from the Americas and 15% from Asia and the rest of the world.

In the first half of fiscal 2026 (1 February to 31 July 2026) sales were €19.76 billion, up 7.6% (9.2% at constant exchange rates), and net profit was €2.98 billion. Gross margin rose to 58.7%, and EBIT grew 7.6% to €3.84 billion. The group had 5,444 stores at the end of July. Sales in the first weeks of the autumn season (1 August to 7 September 2026) were 9% higher than a year earlier. For the full year 2025 gross margin was 58.3%, EBIT €8.0 billion and profit before tax margin 20.1% — far above most clothing retailers.

## The stock's story

Amancio Ortega and Rosalía Mera opened the first Zara shop in A Coruña in 1975. Inditex listed on the Madrid stock exchange in May 2001 and grew into a global chain over the next two decades, making Ortega one of the world's richest people; he still owns the majority of the shares. Pablo Isla chaired the company from 2011 until 2022, when Ortega's daughter Marta Ortega became chair and Óscar García Maceiras became CEO. In 2020 the pandemic closed most of its stores for weeks and the shares fell sharply; the company recovered by moving more sales online and closing smaller shops. Record profits in 2023–2025 pushed the stock to new highs — with sharp falls along the way whenever sales growth slowed.

## Competitors

Its closest rival is Sweden's H&M, which is far less profitable: its operating margin was 8.1% in 2025, against Inditex's 20.1%. Others are Japan's Fast Retailing (Uniqlo), Primark, Mango, Gap, and online players Shein and Temu, which sell very cheap clothes shipped from China. Sportswear brands such as Nike and Adidas compete for the same wallets.

## Main risks

- **Consumer spending:** clothes are easy to postpone when people feel poorer, so a weak economy in Europe or the US hits sales.
- **Cheap online rivals:** Shein and Temu compete hard on price with young shoppers.
- **Currencies:** a large part of sales is outside the euro area; the company expected currencies to cut 2026 sales by about 1%.
- **Weather and fashion misses:** a warm autumn or a collection that does not sell can hurt a whole season.
- **Geopolitics and trade:** stores in the Middle East were temporarily closed during the regional conflict, and tariffs can raise costs.
- **Valuation:** investors pay a high multiple for Inditex's quality, so slower growth can mean big share price drops.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Inditex is a very profitable company with no net debt and a generous dividend, but its shares still swing with fashion cycles and consumer moods. The stock trades in euros, so for someone saving in dollars or another currency the exchange rate adds or removes return. Buying a fixed amount regularly spreads purchases over good and bad seasons instead of betting on one moment. Many people hold one company only as part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
