**Procter & Gamble** makes the everyday things people buy again every few weeks: Tide and Ariel detergent, Pampers nappies, Gillette razors, Oral-B and Crest toothpaste, Head & Shoulders and Pantene shampoo, Charmin toilet paper, Always pads and Vicks cold remedies. Because people keep buying soap and nappies in good years and bad, P&G is one of the steadiest big companies in the US — and it has raised its dividend every year for 70 years.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Net sales: $87.0 billion in fiscal 2026 (to 30 June), +3% on the year; organic sales grew just 1%.
> - Net earnings were $16.1 billion; diluted earnings per share rose 2% to $6.62.
> - P&G returned over $15 billion in one year: $10.2 billion in dividends and $5.0 billion in buybacks.
> - Shailesh Jejurikar became CEO on 1 January 2026, taking over from Jon Moeller.

## How it makes money

P&G sells branded household and personal-care products in supermarkets, chemists and online shops around the world, mostly through big retailers such as Walmart. It earns its margin by charging more than store brands for products people trust. In fiscal 2026 Fabric & Home Care (Tide, Ariel, Dawn, Febreze) was the largest segment with $30.3 billion of sales, followed by Baby, Feminine & Family Care (Pampers, Always, Charmin, Bounty) with $20.4 billion, Beauty (Olay, Pantene, SK-II) with $16.0 billion, Health Care (Oral-B, Crest, Vicks) with $12.5 billion and Grooming (Gillette, Braun) with $6.9 billion. Beauty grew fastest, +7%.

In the July–September 2026 quarter, the first of fiscal 2027, net sales rose 4% to $23.3 billion and organic sales also grew 4%, helped by 2% higher volumes. Diluted earnings per share were $1.97, up 1%, but gross margin fell 1.6 points because raw materials got more expensive. For fiscal 2027 P&G expects all-in sales growth of 2–4% (including the supplements maker Thorne, owned from 1 October 2026) and core earnings per share of $6.89–7.11; it warned of about $1.4 billion of extra commodity costs after tax. It plans to pay over $10 billion in dividends and buy back about $5 billion of shares.

## The stock's story

William Procter, a candle maker, and James Gamble, a soap maker, started the company in Cincinnati in 1837; Ivory soap followed in 1879. P&G built many of the brands still on shelves today and in 2005 bought Gillette for about $57 billion. The stock is known as calm, but not always: on 7 March 2000 it fell about 31% in one day after a profit warning. From 2014 P&G sold or closed around 100 smaller brands to focus on the big ones, and in 2017 the activist investor Nelson Peltz won a board seat after a close proxy fight. In June 2025 the company announced a two-year restructuring with up to 7,000 office jobs cut, costing $1–1.6 billion. Jon Moeller led P&G from 2021 to 2025; Shailesh Jejurikar, its former chief operating officer, has run it since 2026.

## Competitors

In household and personal care P&G competes with Unilever, Colgate-Palmolive, Kimberly-Clark, Reckitt, Church & Dwight and Henkel; in beauty with L'Oréal and Estée Lauder; in razors with Edgewell (Schick) and cheap subscription brands. Its quietest but toughest rival is the store brand: Walmart, Costco, Aldi and others sell their own cheaper detergent, nappies and razors.

## Main risks

- **Slow growth:** organic sales grew only 1% in fiscal 2026; a company this big cannot grow much faster than the population and prices.
- **Costs:** oil-based plastics, pulp and chemicals are big costs; P&G expected $1.4 billion more in fiscal 2027.
- **Price-sensitive shoppers:** when money is tight, people trade down to store brands, and P&G cannot keep raising prices.
- **Powerful retailers:** a few chains sell a large share of its products and push hard on prices.
- **Currencies and tariffs:** about half of sales are outside the US, so a strong dollar and trade disputes cut reported results.

## What it means if you buy regularly

P&G is the kind of stock many people hold for dividends and stability: its products sell in every economy and the dividend has grown for 70 years in a row. But "steady" is not "risk-free" — the share price can stand still for years when growth is slow or the stock is expensive, and it has had sharp falls. Buying a fixed amount regularly spreads your entry price over time, and reinvesting the dividends adds up over many years. Many people hold one company only as part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
