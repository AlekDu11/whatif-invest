**Apple** makes the iPhone, Mac, iPad, Apple Watch and AirPods — and then earns money every month from the people who own them, through the App Store, iCloud, Apple Music, AppleCare and Apple Pay. Its edge is the "ecosystem": once your devices all work together, switching away is a hassle. In November 2026 its stock market value passed $5 trillion, making it the world's most valuable company.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: $478.8 billion in fiscal 2026 (to 26 September), +15% on the year; net income $132.2 billion.
> - The iPhone brought in $254.1 billion, about 53% of all sales; Services added $123.3 billion, about 26%.
> - Apple paid $86.4 billion for buybacks and $15.7 billion in dividends in one year.
> - John Ternus replaced Tim Cook as CEO on 1 September 2026 — the company's fourth CEO.

## How it makes money

Most of Apple's money still comes from selling devices. In fiscal 2026 the iPhone earned $254.1 billion, the Mac $37.9 billion, the iPad $27.9 billion and wearables, home and accessories (Watch, AirPods, Vision Pro and more) $35.6 billion. The second engine is Services: the App Store cut, iCloud storage, subscriptions, AppleCare, payments and advertising, including what Google pays to be the default search engine in Safari. Services earned $123.3 billion — smaller than the iPhone, but it repeats every month and does not depend on a yearly upgrade.

## The numbers right now

In the September quarter Apple had revenue of $114.5 billion, a record for that quarter and up 12% from a year earlier. The iPhone alone made $57.6 billion and Services $31.6 billion. Net income was $30.7 billion, or $2.10 per share; gross margin was 47.9%, about one point of which came from tariff refunds. For the full year net income rose to $132.2 billion from $112.0 billion, and operating cash flow was $145.1 billion.

At the end of September Apple held $36.3 billion in cash plus $100.6 billion in marketable securities against $80.1 billion of debt. It pays a quarterly dividend of $0.27 per share. Apple does not publish a formal revenue forecast in its earnings release.

## The stock's story

The iPhone launched in 2007 and made Apple one of the world's biggest companies. When Tim Cook took over in 2011, Apple was worth about $348 billion; by the time he handed the job to hardware chief John Ternus in 2026 (Cook stays as executive chairman), it was worth roughly $4 trillion and yearly revenue had quadrupled. In January 2026 Apple said it would use Google's AI technology to improve Siri. The shares then set a run of records in autumn 2026: the record close was $350.69 on 9 November 2026 (split-adjusted), and the market value topped $5 trillion.

## Competitors

In phones Apple fights Samsung, Xiaomi, Huawei and other Android makers, with Google's Android as the rival platform. Macs compete with Windows PCs from Lenovo, HP and Dell; services compete with Spotify, Netflix, Google and Amazon. In AI, Apple is behind Google, Microsoft and OpenAI — which is why it chose to work with Google for Siri.

## Main risks

- **One product dominates:** the iPhone is about half of revenue, so a weak upgrade cycle hits the whole company.
- **Regulation:** the EU and other regulators are forcing changes to the App Store and payments; the US search case limits exclusive deals like the one that sends Google payments to Apple.
- **China and tariffs:** Apple builds most devices in Asia and sells heavily in China, so trade disputes and local competition matter.
- **AI catch-up:** if rivals' AI features make people switch phones, Apple's ecosystem advantage weakens.
- **A high price:** after the 2026 rally a lot of good news is already in the share price, and a new CEO still has to prove himself.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Apple is a giant, profitable company, but that does not make the stock safe from big drops — even giants can lose a large part of their value in a bad year. Buying a fixed amount regularly means you buy more shares when the price is low and fewer when it is high, without trying to time the market. Many people hold one company only as part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
