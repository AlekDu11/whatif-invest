**Air Products** makes the gases industry can't work without: oxygen for steel mills and hospitals, nitrogen for food packaging and chip factories, hydrogen for oil refineries, helium for MRI scanners. It often builds a plant right next to a big customer and pipes the gas in under a long-term contract. Based in Pennsylvania, it is one of the world's largest industrial-gas companies.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Fiscal 2026 (to 30 September): sales of $13.0 billion, up 8%; adjusted EPS $13.45, up 12%.
> - Exiting clean-energy projects in Louisiana and Arizona cost about $3.2 billion in pre-tax charges, so GAAP EPS was only $2.39.
> - Capital spending was cut by $1.8 billion to $3.3 billion; guidance for fiscal 2027 is adjusted EPS of $14.15–14.50.
> - The stock set a record of $338.07 in February 2025 and has fallen more than 30% twice since late 2022.

## How it makes money

Air Products earns in three ways. **On-site** plants sit at a customer's site — a refinery, chemical plant or chip fab — and supply gas under multi-year contracts, often with energy costs passed through to the customer. **Merchant** sales deliver liquid gas by truck or in cylinders to thousands of smaller users, from hospitals to welders. A smaller **equipment** business designs and sells air-separation and gas-liquefaction kit. By region, fiscal 2026 sales were $5.68 billion in the Americas (about 44%), $3.46 billion in Asia (27%) and $3.24 billion in Europe (25%); it also earns income from joint ventures in the Middle East and India.

## The numbers right now

In the quarter to 30 September 2026, sales rose 12% to $3.6 billion, helped by 9% higher volumes — partly from a one-off asset sale linked to an early contract termination in the Americas, partly from new plants. Adjusted EPS was $3.61, up 6%, and the adjusted operating margin was 24.1%. For the full fiscal year, adjusted operating margin reached 24.4% and the company paid $1.6 billion in dividends.

The headline GAAP numbers look much weaker because of the clean-energy retreat. On 30 June 2026 the company announced it would exit a clean-energy complex under construction in Louisiana, a green-hydrogen plant in Casa Grande, Arizona, and other projects. Together with problems at two coal-gasification plants in China (one sold in October 2026, the other fully written down), these actions cost about $3.2 billion before tax. For fiscal 2027 management expects adjusted EPS of $14.15–14.50 (up 5–8%) and capital spending of $3.0–3.5 billion.

## The stock's story

For years Air Products was a steady compounder. The worst fall in its modern history came in 2008, when it lost 60.7% between June and November. From December 2022 to February 2024 it dropped 33.8% as investors grew uneasy about the billions it was pouring into large hydrogen projects. In early 2025 activist investor Mantle Ridge, holding a stake of about $1.3 billion, pushed for change: long-time CEO Seifi Ghasemi (in charge since 2014) left, and former Linde executive Eduardo Menezes took over on 7 February 2025. The stock hit its record close of $338.07 days earlier, then fell 31.7% by December 2025 before recovering; on 30 October 2026 it was 6.4% below the peak.

## Competitors

The industrial-gas market is dominated by a few giants: Linde (the largest), France's Air Liquide, Japan's Nippon Sanso and Germany's Messer. Because gas is expensive to transport, local plant networks and long contracts protect each player in its regions.

## Main risks

- **Project bets:** the $3.2 billion of exit charges show how costly a wrong capital decision can be; the one-third stake in the NEOM green-hydrogen venture in Saudi Arabia is still on the books.
- **Industrial cycle:** demand follows steel, chemicals and electronics output, which slows in recessions.
- **Energy costs:** much of the cost is electricity and natural gas; contracts pass a lot of it through, but not all.
- **Competition** from Linde and Air Liquide, both larger, keeps pressure on pricing and new contracts.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Air Products sells something factories buy every day, and much of its revenue comes from long contracts — that usually means steadier results than many industrial stocks. But the last few years showed that big strategic bets can knock the price down by a third even in a calm market. Regular buying spreads purchases across those swings and collects the dividend along the way. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
