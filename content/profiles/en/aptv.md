**Aptiv** builds the hidden technology inside modern cars: the sensors and computers behind driver-assistance features, the software that runs them, and the high-speed connectors that carry power and data around the vehicle. It sells to carmakers rather than to drivers, and more and more to factories, aircraft makers and telecom networks too. In April 2026 it spun off its wiring business as a separate company, Versigent, so today's Aptiv is smaller and more focused on electronics and software.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: $3.2 billion in the third quarter of 2026, above analysts' estimates; earnings per share of $0.94 under GAAP came in below them.
> - Sales to carmakers slipped about 1% on the year in that quarter — growth came from non-automotive customers.
> - On 1 April 2026 the wiring business started trading on the NYSE as Versigent (VGNT); Aptiv shareholders received its shares.
> - In 2025, before the split, the whole company had record revenue of about $20 billion, up 3%.

## How it makes money

Aptiv earns money in two broad ways. The first is "smart" electronics: radar and camera systems for features like automatic emergency braking and lane keeping, central computers that run a car's driver-assistance and infotainment, and software — including Wind River, a maker of operating systems for cars, planes, factories and telecom gear that Aptiv bought for $4.3 billion in December 2022. The second is engineered components: connectors, cable assemblies and other parts that move electricity and data reliably, sold to carmakers and to industrial, aerospace, defence and data-network customers.

Carmakers sign contracts years before a model reaches showrooms, so Aptiv's sales follow how many cars the world builds and how much electronics each car contains. Electric cars and cars with advanced driver assistance need more of both, which is the long-term bet behind the company.

In the September 2026 quarter, its second full quarter without the wiring business, Aptiv reported revenue of $3.2 billion, which beat analysts' expectations. Revenue from automotive customers fell about 1% from a year earlier, so the growth came from industrial, aerospace and telecom buyers. GAAP earnings per share were $0.94, below what analysts had expected. Three months earlier, the second-quarter results had also beaten forecasts, but a weaker outlook sent the shares lower.

## The stock's story

Aptiv's roots go back to Delphi, the parts maker that General Motors spun off in 1999. Delphi went bankrupt in 2005, came out of it in 2009 and listed its shares on the New York Stock Exchange in November 2011 as Delphi Automotive. In December 2017 it split in two: the engine and powertrain business became Delphi Technologies (later bought by BorgWarner), and the rest was renamed Aptiv. In 2020 Aptiv and Hyundai launched Motional, a robotaxi joint venture; in 2024 Aptiv cut its stake sharply in a deal with Hyundai and stepped back from self-driving taxis.

The stock rose strongly in the years after 2017, as investors bet on electric and connected cars, then fell hard when chip shortages, weak car production and slower electric-car sales hit the industry. In January 2026 Aptiv named its wiring company Versigent, the board approved the spin-off on 5 March 2026, and Versigent's shares began trading on 1 April 2026. Because shareholders received Versigent shares, Aptiv's own share price dropped on that day by roughly the value of the business that left.

## Competitors

In driver-assistance and car computers Aptiv competes with Bosch, Continental, Denso and Visteon, and with chip and software companies such as NVIDIA, Qualcomm and Mobileye that sell directly to carmakers. In connectors its main rivals are TE Connectivity and Amphenol. Some carmakers, especially Tesla and Chinese brands, write more of their own software and design their own electronics, which shrinks the market for suppliers.

## Main risks

- **The car cycle:** when the world builds fewer cars, Aptiv sells less, and it cannot easily cut costs fast enough.
- **Electric-car slowdown:** much of the growth story assumed fast EV adoption; slower sales in the US and Europe delay that.
- **China and tariffs:** Chinese carmakers favour local suppliers, and trade barriers raise costs for a company with factories around the world.
- **A smaller company:** after the spin-off Aptiv has to prove that its electronics and software can grow on their own.
- **Customer power:** a handful of large carmakers buy a big share of its output and push hard on prices every year.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Aptiv is a supplier in a cyclical industry, and its shares have swung widely over the past decade, so long stretches of losses are possible. Note that the spin-off changed what the stock represents: the record high above was set by the old, bigger company. Buying a fixed amount regularly spreads your purchases over good and bad years, without trying to time the car cycle. Many people hold a single company only as part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
