**Canton** is a blockchain built for Wall Street: banks and market infrastructure firms use it to move tokenized bonds, funds and cash while keeping each deal visible only to the parties involved. Canton Coin (CC) is the token that pays for using it.

## How it works

Canton was created by Digital Asset, a New York company founded in 2014, and uses its Daml programming language. Instead of one public ledger that everyone can read, each transaction is shared only with the firms in it. A shared layer called the Global Synchronizer puts encrypted messages in order without reading them. It is run by "Super Validators" — large operators that also vote on network rules. The network and CC went live in July 2024.

## What CC is for

CC works on a "burn-mint" model. Every transaction costs a fee, paid by burning CC. At the same time new CC is minted on a set schedule and handed to the Super Validators, validators and app builders who bring real activity to the network. There was no pre-mine and no allocation to venture investors: every coin in circulation was earned for running or using the network.

## Why it matters

Canton is one of the few blockchains where traditional finance does real business: Broadridge's repo platform, Goldman Sachs' digital asset platform and DTCC's tokenization service run on it. About 40 billion CC are in circulation, and it ranks around #25 by market value. Surprising fact: most activity on the network is private by design.

## Main risks

CC trades about 60% below its February 2026 high. Supply keeps growing: in November 2026 burns were about 80% of new minting, so the coin is still inflationary until usage grows faster. Banks using Canton does not automatically mean demand for CC. Rewards and votes are concentrated in a small group of Super Validators, and reward rules have already changed several times. Beware look-alike "CC" tokens on other chains. Buying a fixed amount regularly smooths the swings but does not remove the risk.
