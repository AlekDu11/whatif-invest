**Koç Holding** is Turkey's largest business group: one listed company that owns big stakes in many others — the oil refiner Tüpraş, the car makers Ford Otosan and Tofaş, the home-appliance maker Arçelik (Beko), Yapı Kredi bank and dozens more. Buying its shares (ticker KCHOL on Borsa Istanbul) is a bit like buying a slice of the Turkish economy, still controlled by the Koç family that started it almost a century ago.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: TL 2.82 trillion in January–September 2026, +11% on the year after adjusting for inflation.
> - Net income for shareholders was TL 56.4 billion in nine months, about three times the TL 18.6 billion a year earlier; TL 34.9 billion of it came in the third quarter.
> - Energy carried the year: the refiner Tüpraş earned TL 121.3 billion in nine months, while Arçelik and Otokar made losses.
> - The holding company itself had about $963 million of net cash at the end of September 2026.

## How it makes money

Koç Holding mostly earns through the companies it owns. The biggest pieces are energy (Tüpraş, Turkey's main oil refiner, plus fuel stations and gas distribution through Opet and Aygaz), cars (Ford Otosan, a joint venture with Ford, and Tofaş, with Stellantis), consumer durables (Arçelik, which sells under brands such as Beko and Grundig) and finance (Yapı Kredi, one of Turkey's biggest private banks). Smaller businesses include buses and military vehicles (Otokar), tractors, tourism and retail. The holding collects dividends from these companies and decides where to invest; group companies account for up to 7% of Turkey's exports.

## The stock's story

Vehbi Koç opened a small shop in Ankara in 1926 and built it into an industrial empire; in 1963 his businesses were joined into Koç Holding. His son Rahmi Koç became chairman in 1984, grandson Mustafa V. Koç in 2003, and after Mustafa's death Ömer Koç took over in 2016; Levent Çakıroğlu has been CEO since 2015. Yapı Kredi joined the group in 2005. For investors who count in dollars, the stock has swung hard: it lost about three-quarters of its value from the end of 2007 to early 2009, more than half in the 2018 lira crisis and over 40% in 2020. After Turkey moved back to tighter policy, the shares rose almost fourfold in dollars from mid-2022 to May 2024, then roughly halved again by April 2025.

## Competitors

As a whole, Koç Holding's closest peer is Sabancı Holding, Turkey's other big family conglomerate, which also owns a bank, energy and industrial companies. Each Koç business has its own rivals: Tüpraş competes with imported fuel, Ford Otosan and Tofaş with Hyundai, Toyota, Renault and Chinese car makers, Arçelik with Bosch-Siemens, Samsung and Haier, and Yapı Kredi with Garanti BBVA, İş Bankası and Akbank.

## Main risks

- **The lira and inflation:** profits are in Turkish lira; when the lira falls, a foreign investor's return falls with it, as in 2018 and 2021.
- **Refining margins:** a large part of profit comes from Tüpraş, and fuel margins can swing sharply with oil markets.
- **Weak exports:** Arçelik and the car makers sell a lot in Europe; slow demand cut Arçelik's and Ford Otosan's revenue in 2026.
- **Holding discount:** shares of conglomerates often trade below the value of what they own, and minority shareholders follow the family's decisions.
- **Country risk:** politics, rates and Turkey's credit rating move foreign money in and out quickly.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Koç Holding spreads your money across many large Turkish businesses at once, but it does not protect you from the country's big swings: in dollars its share price has halved several times in the past 20 years. Buying a fixed amount regularly means you get more shares when the price is low and fewer when it is high, without trying to time the market.
