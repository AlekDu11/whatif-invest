**Carvana** is an online used-car dealer based in Tempe, Arizona. You choose a car on its website or app, get a loan and a price for your old car online, and the car is delivered to your door or picked up from one of its glass "car vending machine" towers. It makes money on the car itself, on the car loans it arranges and sells on to investors, and on add-ons such as warranties. In 2022 its shares lost about 98% and the company nearly ran out of money; since then it has become the fastest-growing big car retailer in the US.

> **Key takeaways**
> - In July–September 2026 Carvana sold a record 217,385 retail cars, up 39% from a year earlier, and revenue rose 42% to $8.04 billion.
> - Net income was $483 million in that quarter, and the company expected full-year 2026 adjusted EBITDA near the top of its $2.7–3.0 billion range.
> - The shares were split 5-for-1 in May 2026; in split-adjusted terms they fell from about $67 in 2021 to about $2 in early 2023.
> - The founding Garcia family controls the votes through special shares, and the business depends on cheap car loans.

## How it makes money

Carvana buys used cars from people and from auctions, inspects and reconditions them at its own centres, and sells them online. Each sale earns money in three ways: the **car itself** (the gap between what Carvana paid and the sale price), **financing** (Carvana gives the buyer a loan and then sells bundles of these loans to banks and other investors) and **extras** such as extended warranties and gap insurance. It also sells older cars it does not want to keep through **ADESA**, the network of wholesale car auctions it bought from KAR Global in 2022 for about $2.2 billion. ADESA's sites are also being turned into reconditioning centres, so cars can reach buyers faster.

The company's pitch is scale: the more cars it sells through the same centres and software, the lower the cost per car. That is why its operating margin rose to 8.9% in the September 2026 quarter.

In the quarter to 30 September 2026 Carvana sold 217,385 retail units (+39%) and had total revenue of $8.04 billion (+42%), both records. GAAP operating income was $714 million (an 8.9% margin), net income $483 million (6.0% of revenue) and adjusted EBITDA $800 million (10.0% of revenue, down from 11.3% a year earlier). For the fourth quarter the company expected more retail units than in the third, as long as the market stays stable.

## The stock's story

Carvana was started in 2012 by Ernie Garcia III, who is still CEO, as a project inside DriveTime, the used-car dealer run by his father, Ernest Garcia II. It listed on the New York Stock Exchange in April 2017. Online car buying boomed during the pandemic, and in WhatIfStack's split-adjusted data the shares rose from under $4 in 2017 to about $67 in mid-2021. Then interest rates jumped, used-car prices fell and Carvana was left with heavy debt from the ADESA deal: the shares sank to about $2 by early 2023 and many expected bankruptcy. A debt exchange with bondholders in 2023 bought it time, costs were cut sharply, and growth and profits returned. The shares climbed back above their 2021 peak in 2025, the company joined the S&P 500 index in December 2025, and in March 2026 the board approved its first stock split (5-for-1), which took effect in May 2026.

## Competitors

Carvana's closest rival is [CarMax](/en/kmx/), the largest US used-car retailer, which sells both in stores and online. It also competes with big dealer groups such as AutoNation and Lithia Motors, with car-listing sites such as CarGurus and Cars.com, and with the thousands of local dealers that still sell most used cars in the US.

## Main risks

- **Car loans and interest rates:** most buyers borrow; higher rates make cars less affordable, and if investors stop buying Carvana's loan bundles on good terms, profits fall.
- **Used-car prices:** a sudden drop in prices can leave Carvana holding cars worth less than it paid, as in 2022.
- **Debt and the past:** the 2022–23 crisis showed how quickly the company can get into trouble when growth stops.
- **Control and related parties:** the Garcia family holds most of the votes through Class B shares, and short sellers (for example Hindenburg Research in January 2025) have questioned Carvana's dealings with family-linked DriveTime and its accounting; the company rejected those claims.
- **A very volatile stock:** the shares can move 10% or more in a day on results or loan-market news.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Carvana is one of the most extreme examples of how much a single stock can swing: about 98% down from 2021 to 2023, then many times up again. Someone buying a fixed amount every month through that period bought very cheaply at the bottom, but someone who stopped in 2022 locked in heavy losses — and most companies that fall 98% never come back. Regular buying spreads the entry price, but it does not make a high-risk company safe; many people hold a stock like this only as a small part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
