**Agilent Technologies** makes the machines and supplies that laboratories use to find out what is inside a sample: a new drug, a blood or tissue sample, river water, food or a microchip material. Drug companies, hospitals, environmental labs and chemical plants buy its instruments, then keep buying columns, chemicals, parts and service for years. It was spun off from Hewlett-Packard in 1999.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: $1.88 billion in the quarter to 31 July 2026, +8.1% (+7.3% core growth).
> - Revenue from GLP-1 weight-loss drug work grew more than 70%; China grew 9%.
> - Full-year forecast for fiscal 2026: $7.49–7.51 billion of revenue and $6.18–6.21 of adjusted EPS.
> - The stock lost 93% from 2000 to 2002 and only regained that peak in November 2020.

## How it makes money

Agilent has three segments. Life Sciences and Diagnostics Markets ($746 million in fiscal Q3 2026, about 40% of revenue) sells liquid chromatography systems (machines that separate a mixture into its parts) and mass spectrometers that identify each part, plus genomics, pathology and contract drug manufacturing. Agilent CrossLab ($786 million, about 42%) is the recurring part: consumables, repairs, maintenance, software and training for machines already in labs, and it has the highest operating margin, 34%. Applied Markets ($346 million, about 18%) serves chemicals, advanced materials, environmental testing and forensics. Pharma is the biggest customer group, and its revenue grew 12% in the quarter.

## The numbers right now

Agilent's fiscal year ends on 31 October. In the third quarter, to 31 July 2026 and reported on 26 August, revenue rose 8.1% to $1.88 billion. GAAP earnings per share were $1.28 (+8%) and adjusted EPS $1.62 (+18%), helped by $0.06 of tariff refunds. Revenue linked to testing for PFAS ("forever chemicals") rose 20%. In June Agilent closed the purchase of Biocare Medical, a maker of tissue-staining tools for cancer diagnosis, and issued $600 million of notes around the deal. For the fourth quarter it guided to $1.98–2.0 billion of revenue and adjusted EPS of $1.71–1.74.

## The stock's story

Agilent's November 1999 IPO raised $2.1 billion, then the largest in Silicon Valley history. The dot-com bust followed: from March 2000 to October 2002 the price fell 93%, and it did not get back to that peak until November 2020. After the pandemic-era rush of lab spending, the stock fell again, by about 45% from September 2021 to April 2025, when markets slumped on US tariff news. In 2026 it recovered and set a record close of $181.48 on 28 October.

## Competitors

Agilent's main rivals are Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waters, Danaher, Shimadzu and Bruker. In pathology it competes with Roche and Leica Biosystems (part of Danaher). Its edge is a large installed base of instruments that keeps feeding CrossLab with recurring service and consumables revenue.

## Main risks

- **Customers' budgets:** labs delay buying instruments when drug companies, biotech start-ups or Chinese buyers cut spending, as happened after 2021.
- **China and tariffs:** China is an important market, and trade rules can change costs and demand quickly; the latest quarter even got a one-off boost from tariff refunds.
- **Strong rivals:** Thermo Fisher and Danaher are much larger and fight hard on price.
- **Acquisitions:** deals like Biocare and BioVectra must earn back what was paid.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Agilent shows both sides of holding one stock for a long time. Someone who bought at the 2000 peak waited about two decades to break even on the price alone, while someone buying steadily through the bad years paid far lower average prices. Today the stock is near a record again. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
