**American Express** is the company behind the green, gold and platinum cards: it issues the cards, lends to the people who carry them and runs its own payment network that shops connect to. Unlike Visa or Mastercard, it sits on both sides of a purchase — it knows the cardholder and the merchant — and earns from the swipe, the annual fee and the interest. It is listed on the New York Stock Exchange and employs about 76,800 people.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: $20.1 billion in Q3 2026, +9% a year; net income $3.7 billion, +28%.
> - Card members spent $454 billion on Amex cards in the quarter, +8%.
> - Full-year 2026 guidance raised to $18.30–18.60 earnings per share; quarterly dividend $0.95, up 16% this year.
> - The stock fell 84% in 2007–2009 and about 50% in early 2020, and recovered both times.

## How it makes money

Amex has three main income streams. First, a fee from the merchant every time a card is used (the "discount revenue"), which rises with spending. Second, annual card fees: premium cards such as Platinum cost hundreds of dollars a year, and in 2025 net card fees reached a record $10 billion, up 18%. Third, interest on balances that card members carry. The company reports four segments; in Q3 2026 U.S. Consumer Services brought in $9.8 billion (+11%), Commercial Services (small business and corporate cards) $4.6 billion, International Card Services $3.7 billion and Global Merchant and Network Services $2.1 billion.

Its customers lean affluent, and Millennials and Gen Z were its fastest-growing group in 2025, up 20%. The refreshed U.S. Platinum Card, launched in September 2025, brought new perks — and higher costs for rewards and benefits.

## The numbers right now

In the quarter to 30 September 2026 Amex reported revenue net of interest expense of $20.1 billion, up 9%, and net income of $3.7 billion, up 28%. Diluted earnings per share were $5.42; without a one-off gain from selling its stake in travel firm GBTG, they were $4.68, up 13%. Credit stayed healthy: the net write-off rate (loans written off as lost) fell to 1.8% from 1.9%. In the quarter it bought back 5 million shares for $1.6 billion and paid $600 million in dividends. Over the last twelve months revenue was about $72.9 billion and the profit margin about 17%.

## The stock's story

Amex has been through every big crisis. In 2007–2009 the stock lost 84% from peak to trough as unemployment rose and card loans went bad, and it took until 2013 to get back. In 2020 it fell about 50% in 33 days when travel and restaurant spending stopped, then regained the high within a year. The record close so far is about $385 in December 2025; in mid-November 2026 the stock traded roughly 18% below it, inside a 52-week range of $291–387.

## Competitors

On the card side Amex competes with big U.S. banks such as JPMorgan Chase (Sapphire), Capital One and Citi; on the network side with Visa and Mastercard, which are accepted in more places worldwide. Its edge is the closed loop — data on both buyer and seller — and a brand that lets it charge high annual fees.

## Main risks

- **Recession:** when people lose jobs they spend less and repay less; write-offs and loan-loss provisions rise fast.
- **Costs of rewards:** points, lounges and travel credits are expensive; Amex must keep raising fees and spending to stay ahead.
- **Regulation:** caps on card interest rates or merchant fees, often debated in the U.S. and Europe, would hit income directly.
- **Competition** for premium customers from banks and fintech cards keeps marketing spending high.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Amex is a mature, profitable company that pays a growing dividend ($0.95 a quarter in 2026) and buys back shares, so part of the return comes as cash. But it is a financial stock tied to consumer spending: it has fallen by half or more in recessions and took years to recover after 2008. Regular buying spreads purchases across good and bad months; dividends can be reinvested to buy more shares. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
