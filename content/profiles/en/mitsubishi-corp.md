**Mitsubishi Corporation** is the biggest of Japan's five large trading houses, or *sogo shosha*. Despite the name it does much more than trade: it owns stakes in gas fields, copper and coal mines, power plants, food companies, car distributors and the Lawson convenience-store chain, and earns money from all of them. Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway is one of its largest shareholders. Its shares trade in Tokyo under the code 8058.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Net income: ¥800.5 billion in fiscal 2025 (to 31 March 2026), down 16% from ¥950.7 billion, after one-off gains a year earlier.
> - April–September 2026: net income +74% to ¥619.7 billion on higher copper and coking-coal prices and more LNG business.
> - In November 2026 it raised its fiscal 2026 forecast to a record ¥1.2 trillion and announced a buyback of up to ¥1 trillion.
> - The dividend is planned at ¥125 a share for fiscal 2026, up from ¥110 — the company has a progressive dividend policy.

## How it makes money

Mitsubishi Corporation is a group of hundreds of businesses in eight segments. The biggest earners in fiscal 2025 were **Mineral Resources** (¥204.5 billion of net income — copper mines in Chile and Peru and steelmaking coal in Australia), **Environmental Energy** (¥160.9 billion — mainly liquefied natural gas, LNG, from projects in Australia, Asia, the Middle East and North America), **Smart-Life Creation** (¥91.0 billion — consumer businesses including Lawson, which it owns jointly with KDDI), **Urban Development & Infrastructure** (¥85.1 billion), **Food Industry** (¥83.3 billion — including salmon farming and grain trading), **Mobility** (¥57.6 billion — mostly selling and financing Mitsubishi Motors and Isuzu vehicles in Asia), **Power Solution** (¥43.4 billion) and **Materials Solution** (¥26.3 billion). From April 2026 energy and power are reported together. It also buys and sells businesses all the time: gains from selling assets were a large part of profit in recent years.

In the six months to September 2026 net income rose 74% to ¥619.7 billion from ¥355.8 billion a year earlier, and underlying operating cash flow rose 63% to ¥728.1 billion. Higher copper and Australian coking-coal prices lifted mining profit, and the energy business gained from more LNG deals, dividends and asset sales; salmon farming and overseas grain trading were weaker. In its "Corporate Strategy 2027" plan, set in 2025, the company aims for net income of more than ¥1.2 trillion and a return on equity of at least 12% by fiscal 2027, while keeping net debt at no more than about 0.6 times equity (it was 0.38 times in March 2026).

## The stock's story

The Mitsubishi group traces its roots to a shipping business started by Yataro Iwasaki in 1870. After World War II the occupation authorities broke up the old trading company in 1947; today's Mitsubishi Corporation was put back together in 1954. For decades trading houses were seen as slow, cheap conglomerates: in dollar terms our data show the shares roughly flat from 2008 to 2020, with deep falls in the 2008–2009 crisis and again when commodity prices collapsed in 2015–2016. That changed in August 2020, when Berkshire Hathaway revealed stakes of about 5% in the five big trading houses. Rising commodity prices, larger buybacks and dividends followed, and our data show the shares rising about fivefold in dollar terms from mid-2020 to early 2026. The company split each share into three in January 2024. By the end of 2025 Berkshire's stake had passed 10% (10.8%). Katsuya Nakanishi has been president and CEO since April 2022, when he took over from Takehiko Kakiuchi.

## Competitors

Its closest rivals are the other big Japanese trading houses — Mitsui & Co., Itochu, Sumitomo Corp. and Marubeni — all also owned in part by Berkshire. In its individual businesses it competes with mining groups such as BHP and Glencore, energy companies like Shell and TotalEnergies in LNG, and food and retail groups in Japan and Asia.

## Main risks

- **Commodity prices:** much of the profit comes from copper, coal and LNG, so a fall in prices can cut earnings fast.
- **One-off gains:** profit in some years depends on selling assets, which does not repeat every year.
- **Complexity:** hundreds of businesses in many countries make it hard for outsiders to see where problems are building.
- **Currency and Japan:** results are in yen; a stronger yen lowers the value of overseas profit, and a weaker one hurts dollar-based investors.
- **Big investors:** part of the recent rise came from Berkshire's buying; any sign it is selling could weigh on the price.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Mitsubishi Corporation spreads its bets across energy, metals, food and consumer businesses and pays a rising dividend, but it is still tied to commodity cycles, and the stock went nowhere for more than a decade before 2020. Buying a fixed amount regularly means you get more shares when commodities slump and fewer at the peaks, without trying to time the cycle.
