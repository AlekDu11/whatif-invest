**American International Group (AIG)** is one of the world's big insurers for companies: it covers factories against fire and storms, ships and planes, directors against lawsuits, and large firms against cyber attacks and political risk. It also sells travel, accident and health cover and insurance for wealthy households. Today's AIG is much smaller and simpler than the giant that needed a government rescue in 2008.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Net premiums written: $6.7 billion in Q3 2026, +7% a year; net income $844 million, +63%.
> - Combined ratio 91.3%: AIG paid out about 91 cents in claims and costs per dollar of premium.
> - In 2008 it took a $182.3 billion taxpayer rescue, and the shares lost about 95% under one CEO.
> - In Q3 2026 it returned $787 million to shareholders; dividend $0.50 a quarter.

## How it makes money

An insurer earns in two ways. It collects premiums and tries to pay less than that in claims and expenses — that gap is "underwriting income". Then it invests the premiums until claims are due and keeps the investment income. AIG has three segments. North America Commercial ($2.48 billion of net premiums in Q3 2026, +2%) and International Commercial ($2.39 billion, +13%) insure businesses: property, liability, marine, aviation, energy, financial lines. Global Personal ($1.78 billion, +6%) covers individuals — accident and health, travel and high-value homes.

Over the past years AIG separated its life insurance and retirement business, Corebridge, to focus on property and casualty cover. In 2026 it also bought renewal rights to a retail insurance book of about $2 billion of premiums from Everest Group and took a stake in Convex Group.

## The numbers right now

In the quarter to 30 September 2026 AIG reported net income of $844 million, up 63%, and adjusted after-tax income of $2.06 per share. Net premiums written in general insurance rose 7% to $6.7 billion. Storms in North America cost $283 million in catastrophe losses (versus $100 million a year earlier), pushing the combined ratio up 4.5 points to 91.3% and underwriting income down 29% to $564 million. Net investment income was $1.1 billion, up 42%. AIG bought back about 7 million shares for $527 million and paid $260 million in dividends; shares outstanding fell to 518.7 million from 544.5 million a year earlier. Debt was 20.8% of total capital.

Eric Andersen, formerly president of broker Aon, became CEO on 1 June 2026; Peter Zaffino, who led the turnaround, moved to executive chair.

## The stock's story

AIG's history has a deep scar. In September 2008, after huge losses on credit derivatives, the shares fell 61% in a single day, and the U.S. government took an 80% stake. The total rescue reached $182.3 billion. In 2009 AIG did a 1-for-20 reverse split. Shareholders from before the crisis never got their money back. Since then the stock has recovered slowly: on a split-adjusted basis it went from $64 in November 2016 to about $79.50 in November 2026 — +46% in ten years before dividends. Over the last 52 weeks it moved between $71 and $87.

## Competitors

AIG competes with Chubb, Travelers, Zurich, Allianz, AXA and the Lloyd's of London market for large commercial risks, and with Berkshire Hathaway's insurance units. Its strengths are a global network in many countries and decades of experience with complex, large risks.

## Main risks

- **Catastrophes:** hurricanes, wildfires and storms can wipe out a quarter's underwriting profit, as Q3 2026 showed.
- **Pricing cycle:** insurance prices move in cycles; in Q3 2026 premiums grew 10% without North America Property but only 7% with it, so that line is already a drag.
- **Reserves:** if old claims (for example liability lawsuits) turn out bigger than set aside, profits are restated down.
- **Leadership change:** the new CEO must keep the discipline that rebuilt the company.

## What it means if you buy regularly

AIG is now a steadier business than in 2008, pays a dividend ($0.50 a quarter) and shrinks its share count with buybacks. But insurance profits swing with disasters and interest rates, and the 2008 story shows that a single company can wipe out shareholders. Regular buying spreads purchases over time; dividends can be reinvested. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
