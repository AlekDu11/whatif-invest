**Eli Lilly** is the American drugmaker behind Mounjaro and Zepbound, the diabetes and weight-loss injections that turned a 150-year-old company from Indianapolis into the world's most valuable healthcare business, worth more than $1 trillion.

## How it makes money

Lilly invents medicines, protects them with patents and sells them to health systems, insurers and pharmacies in about 90 countries. While a patent lasts, a successful drug can earn billions a year; after it expires, cheaper copies arrive and sales fall. So the company reinvests heavily: $13.3 billion, or 20.5% of sales, went into research in 2025.

Today one molecule dominates: tirzepatide, sold as Mounjaro for type 2 diabetes and Zepbound for obesity. In the third quarter of 2026 Mounjaro brought in $10.7 billion (+65%) and Zepbound $5.4 billion (+49%). Total revenue was $24.4 billion, up 39%, and Lilly raised its 2026 forecast to $91–92 billion — up from $65.2 billion in 2025. Its weight-loss pill Foundayo (orforglipron) has started global launches.

## Why it matters

Lilly made the first commercial insulin in 1923, and diabetes is still at the heart of its business a century later. Its other areas include cancer, immunology and Alzheimer's disease. It employs about 51,000 people. One surprising fact: a single drug ingredient, tirzepatide, now earns more per quarter than many entire pharmaceutical companies.

## Main risks

Lilly depends heavily on one drug family, and rivals such as Novo Nordisk and new entrants are fighting for the same patients. US politicians are pushing drug prices down. Clinical trials fail, and expensive acquisitions may not pay off. The stock is priced for strong growth — around 46 times earnings — so disappointing results can hit it hard. Buying a fixed amount regularly smooths price swings but does not remove the risk.
