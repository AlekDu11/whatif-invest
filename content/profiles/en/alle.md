**Allegion** makes the things that keep doors shut and let the right people through: Schlage locks on homes, Von Duprin panic bars on school and hospital exits, door closers, keypads and smart locks. It sells mainly to builders, locksmiths, distributors and building owners, and more and more of its products are electronic. The company is registered in Dublin, Ireland, and was spun off from Ingersoll Rand in December 2013.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: $1.17 billion in Q3 2026, +9.6% a year (+7.2% organic, without acquisitions and currency).
> - Very profitable for a hardware maker: operating margin of 24.3% in the quarter.
> - Full-year 2026 outlook raised to $9.05–9.10 of adjusted earnings per share, after $8.14 in 2025.
> - The share price set a record of $195.42 on 5 November 2026; its worst year since the spin-off was 2022 (−18.6%).

## How it makes money

Allegion owns more than 40 brands, including Schlage, Von Duprin, LCN, Ives, CISA, SimonsVoss, Interflex and Brisant (a UK maker of home security hardware bought in 2025). The business has two parts. **Americas** — about four-fifths of sales — sells locks, exit devices, door closers, steel doors and access-control systems for offices, schools, hospitals and homes. **International** covers Europe, Asia and Oceania, where it is smaller and growing slowly.

Much of the demand comes from replacing and upgrading doors in existing buildings, not only from new construction. That makes sales steadier than for a typical building-products company. Electronic locks and access systems, which connect to phones, cards and building software, are where Allegion is pushing hardest.

## The numbers right now

In the quarter to 30 September 2026 Allegion earned $1,172.6 million in revenue, up 9.6% year on year (7.2% organic). Americas grew 11.7% to $942.4 million, while International rose only 1.8% to $230.2 million and was slightly down organically. Operating income was $284.6 million, a 24.3% margin, and adjusted earnings per share rose 13% to $2.60.

So far in 2026 the company generated $539.4 million of available cash flow, spent $280.3 million on buying back its own shares and paid $140.2 million in dividends ($0.55 per share each quarter). It had $388.1 million of cash and $2.03 billion of debt at the end of September. Management now expects 2026 revenue growth of 9.5–10.5% (5–6% organic). For context, full-year 2025 revenue was $4.07 billion.

## The stock's story

Allegion has been a steady climber rather than a rocket. Since the 2013 spin-off it has had more up years than down years: its best was 2019 (+53.7%), its worst 2022 (−18.6%), when rising interest rates hit housing and construction stocks. In 2025 it gained 23.8%, and in November 2026 it reached a record of $195.42.

## Competitors

Allegion's main global rivals are Sweden's Assa Abloy (much larger, owner of many lock brands worldwide) and Switzerland's dormakaba. In North American home locks it also competes with Fortune Brands Innovations and with cheaper private-label products sold by big retailers. Tech companies selling smart locks and app-based access add pressure at the high end. Allegion's strengths are trusted brands, a dense network of locksmiths and distributors, and products written into building safety codes.

## Main risks

- **Construction cycle:** a slowdown in office, school or home building would cut demand for new doors and hardware.
- **Weak International business:** sales outside the Americas were flat organically in Q3 2026.
- **Costs and tariffs:** steel, zinc and brass prices and US import tariffs can squeeze margins if price rises don't keep up.
- **Technology shift:** if electronic and cloud-based access control moves faster than Allegion, newer rivals could take share.
- **Acquisitions:** the company buys smaller firms regularly; paying too much or integrating badly would hurt returns.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Allegion is a quality industrial company with high margins and a growing dividend, but it is still tied to construction and the economy — it fell almost a fifth in 2022. Regular buying spreads your purchases across good and bad years; a single stock still carries more risk than a broad index fund, so many people hold it only as one part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
