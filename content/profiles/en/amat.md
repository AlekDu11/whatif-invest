**Applied Materials** does not make chips — it builds the machines that chip factories use to make them. Companies like TSMC, Samsung, Intel and memory makers buy its equipment to lay down, etch and inspect the ultra-thin layers inside every processor and memory chip. The AI boom has just given the Santa Clara, California, company its best year ever.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Record revenue: $10.41 billion in the quarter to 25 October 2026, +53% a year; $34.45 billion for fiscal 2026, +21%.
> - China's share of sales fell to 25% last quarter from 29% a year earlier; Taiwan was the largest region at 28%.
> - For the next quarter Applied guided to $11.1 billion, plus or minus $500 million, and expects chip-equipment spending to grow more than 30% in 2027.
> - The stock dropped to $316.95 on 5 November 2026 — 57% below its 52-week high of $739.67.

## How it makes money

About three-quarters of revenue comes from **Semiconductor Systems** — the machines themselves ($8.00 billion last quarter, up from $4.88 billion). They deposit materials atom by atom, etch tiny patterns, polish wafers flat and check them for defects. Memory is a growing part: DRAM rose to 33% of this segment's sales from 28%, because AI servers need huge amounts of fast memory.

**Applied Global Services** ($1.90 billion) is the steady part: spare parts, upgrades and service contracts for the huge fleet of machines already running in factories. The rest ($517 million), shown as "Other", includes equipment for making TV and phone screens.

## The numbers right now

In fiscal 2026 Applied earned $10.09 billion of net profit, up 44%, at a 50.0% gross margin. In the fourth quarter alone it generated a record $2.62 billion of free cash flow and returned $2.42 billion to shareholders — $2.00 billion in buybacks, the most in a quarter in over eight years, and $421 million in dividends.

CEO Gary Dickerson says AI keeps pushing demand for advanced chips above supply and that customers are asking for machines earlier. Management named one limit for 2027: there are not enough finished cleanrooms — factory buildings — to put the new equipment in.

## The stock's story

Applied has traded on Nasdaq for decades and moves with the chip cycle. In 2022, when chip demand cooled, it lost about half its value. In 2026 the AI building wave sent it to a 52-week high of $739.67 by summer. Then the swings got violent: $547.25 on 2 October, $316.95 on 5 November amid doubts about AI spending, then a rebound of about 25% into the 12 November results — still up 49% for the year at that point.

## Competitors

The closest rivals are Lam Research (etching and deposition), Tokyo Electron of Japan and KLA (inspection and measurement). ASML of the Netherlands makes the lithography machines that "print" chip patterns — a step Applied does not sell — so the two are more neighbours than rivals. Chinese makers such as Naura are growing fast at home.

## Main risks

- **Boom and bust:** chip factories order machines in waves; when they stop adding capacity, equipment orders are among the first things cut.
- **China rules:** in October 2025 Applied said a new US export rule would cut fiscal 2026 revenue by about $600 million. That rule has been suspended under the US–China trade truce, now until 10 January 2027, and could return.
- **Few big buyers:** a handful of chipmakers place most orders.
- **High expectations:** any sign of slower AI spending can hit the stock hard, as October–November 2026 showed.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Applied is a "picks and shovels" way to own the chip boom, but it is cyclical: drops of 40–60% have happened in a matter of months. Buying a fixed amount every day or month spreads your entry over good and bad stretches, yet one company can stay down for years. Many people hold such a stock as one part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
