**Monero** is digital cash built so that nobody — not an exchange, not an analyst, not a government — can see who paid whom or how much. Launched in April 2014, it is the oldest large privacy coin, and its coins are truly interchangeable, like banknotes. In November 2026 all XMR together were worth about $9 billion.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Every Monero payment hides the sender, the receiver and the amount — by default, not as an option.
> - About 18.8 million XMR circulate; since June 2022 a fixed 0.6 XMR per block is added forever, under 1% a year.
> - Binance delisted XMR in February 2024, and EU anti-money-laundering rules bar exchanges from handling privacy coins from 2027.
> - Even so, XMR set a new record in January 2026 as privacy coins came back into demand.

## How it works

On Bitcoin, every address and amount is public. Monero hides all three parts of a payment: **ring signatures** mix your transaction with decoy ones so the real sender is unclear, **stealth addresses** give the receiver a fresh one-time address every time, and **RingCT** hides the amount. A new block arrives about every two minutes. XMR is mined with ordinary computer processors (the RandomX algorithm) rather than special machines, so anyone with a PC can take part. The code is open source and maintained by volunteers; there was no pre-sale and no company behind the coin.

## Why people think it has value

XMR is used to pay fees and to send value privately, and it is what miners earn. Because no coin carries a visible history, a coin can never be "tainted" or blocked for where it has been — which is what supporters mean by true fungibility. Demand comes from people who want financial privacy, from activists to ordinary users; critics point out that darknet markets and ransomware gangs use it too.

## Its story so far

Monero started in April 2014 as a fork of Bytecoin, a project based on the CryptoNote protocol. Over the years it made privacy mandatory, added RingCT in 2017 and switched to RandomX mining in 2019 to keep out specialised machines. Its earlier peak came in January 2018, during that year's crypto boom. In June 2022 the planned "tail emission" began. Binance delisted XMR in February 2024, and others followed in some countries. In August 2025 a mining pool called Qubic claimed more than half of Monero's hash rate and rewrote several recent blocks — a rare real-world "51%" episode. In January 2026 XMR still climbed to a new all-time high as privacy coins rallied.

## Alternatives

Monero's main rival is [Zcash]({lang}/zec/), where privacy is optional and based on zero-knowledge proofs. [Dash]({lang}/dash/) offers optional mixing. As a coin with a long history and simple payments it is also compared with [Bitcoin]({lang}/btc/) and [Litecoin]({lang}/ltc/).

## Main risks

- **Regulation:** exchanges in the EU, Japan, South Korea and elsewhere cannot list or must drop privacy coins, and more countries may follow.
- **Liquidity:** fewer exchanges mean it can be harder to buy or sell at a fair price.
- **Mining attacks:** the 2025 Qubic episode showed that hash power can be concentrated.
- **Reputation:** use by criminals keeps it under pressure from banks and regulators.
- **Big swings:** like most crypto, XMR can lose half its value or more in months.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Monero has moved through several booms and deep drops, and its access to exchanges keeps shrinking in some places. Buying a fixed amount regularly averages the price you pay without guessing the bottom, but it does not remove the risk of losses or of being unable to sell easily where you live. Many people keep coins like this to a small share of savings they can afford to lose. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
