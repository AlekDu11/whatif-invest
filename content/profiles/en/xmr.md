**Monero** is digital cash built so that nobody — not an exchange, not an analyst, not a government — can see who paid whom or how much. Launched in April 2014, it is the largest privacy coin and one of the few cryptocurrencies whose coins are truly interchangeable, like banknotes.

## How it works

On bitcoin, every address and amount is public. Monero hides all three parts of a payment: **ring signatures** mix your transaction with decoy ones so the real sender is unclear, **stealth addresses** give the receiver a fresh one-time address every time, and **RingCT** hides the amount. XMR is mined with ordinary computer processors (the RandomX algorithm), not special machines, and pays network fees. A new block arrives about every two minutes.

## Why it matters

Monero is the go-to coin for people who want financial privacy, from activists to ordinary users — and also, critics point out, for darknet markets and ransomware. Its supply is not capped: since June 2022 a small permanent "tail emission" of 0.6 XMR per block (under 1% a year now) keeps paying miners. A surprising fact: in August 2025 a mining pool called Qubic claimed more than half of Monero's hash rate and rewrote several recent blocks, a rare real-world test of a "51%" scenario.

## Main risks

Regulation is the big one. Binance delisted XMR in February 2024, Kraken and others dropped it in parts of Europe, Japan and South Korea ban privacy coins on exchanges, and new EU anti-money-laundering rules bar exchanges from handling them from 2027. Fewer exchanges means less liquidity and harder buying or selling. Mining concentration can threaten security, and the price swings sharply. Regular buying smooths out the swings but does not remove the risk.
