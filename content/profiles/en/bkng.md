**Booking Holdings** owns Booking.com, Priceline, Agoda, Kayak and OpenTable — websites and apps where people book hotels, holiday flats, flights and restaurant tables. It does not own hotels: it earns a commission or a margin on every stay sold through its platforms. In the summer quarter of 2026 travellers booked 345 million room nights through it.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Q3 2026: 345 million room nights (+7%) and $54.5 billion of gross bookings (+10%).
> - Revenue was $8.9 billion after a one-off $900 million cut linked to a US FTC case; without it, adjusted revenue was $9.8 billion (+9%).
> - On 2 April 2026 the company split its stock 25-for-1, so one share that cost over $4,000 became 25 shares.
> - In nine months of 2026 it spent $9.9 billion on buybacks; it also pays a quarterly dividend of $0.42 per share.

## How it makes money

Booking.com, based in Amsterdam, is the main engine: millions of hotels, apartments and guest houses list their rooms there. In the "agency" model the property pays Booking a commission after the guest stays; in the "merchant" model Booking takes the payment itself and keeps a margin, and it also earns on payment services, flights and car rentals. Merchant revenue keeps growing (about $7.0 billion in Q3 2026, up roughly 14%) while agency revenue shrinks slowly ($2.5 billion). Advertising, mainly on Kayak and OpenTable, added $332 million.

In the quarter to 30 September 2026 net income was $2.7 billion, or $3.61 per share; adjusted earnings per share rose 17% to $4.66 and adjusted EBITDA rose 9% to $4.6 billion. The $900 million revenue reduction and a $100 million accrued loss relate to an FTC matter: in July 2026 FTC staff said they planned to recommend a complaint against Priceline and a partner over disclosures, fees, customer support and billing. No settlement had been agreed when the results came out. For the fourth quarter the company expects room nights to grow 1–3% and adjusted revenue 0–2%.

## The stock's story

Priceline was founded in 1997 by Jay Walker and became famous for "name your own price" hotel and flight deals. Its best decision was buying the small Dutch site Booking.com in 2005; Agoda (2007), Kayak (2013) and OpenTable (2014) followed, and in 2018 the company renamed itself Booking Holdings. Glenn Fogel has been CEO since 2017. After the dot-com crash and the 2020 lockdowns, when travel almost stopped, the stock recovered both times. In February 2026 the price was above $4,000, which made it one of the most expensive US stocks, and the company announced the 25-for-1 split that took effect in April.

## Competitors

The closest rivals are Expedia Group (Expedia, Hotels.com, Vrbo) and Airbnb for holiday homes. Google is both a partner and a threat: travellers start their search there, and Booking pays large sums for Google ads. Hotel chains such as Marriott and Hilton push people to book directly through their own apps, and Trip.com is strong in China and Asia.

## Main risks

- **Travel is cyclical:** a recession, a pandemic or a war quickly cuts bookings.
- **Regulators:** the EU named Booking a "gatekeeper" under the Digital Markets Act, and the US FTC case may bring payments and changes to how prices and fees are shown.
- **Dependence on Google:** if search costs rise or AI assistants book trips themselves, customer acquisition gets more expensive.
- **Currency swings:** most bookings are in Europe, so a strong dollar lowers reported results.
- **Slower growth ahead:** the company itself guided to almost flat adjusted revenue for Q4 2026.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Booking is a large, very profitable company, but its stock reacts sharply when travel slows — in 2020 it fell hard within weeks. Buying a fixed amount regularly means you buy more shares when the price is low and fewer when it is high, without trying to guess the next travel season. The 2026 split changes nothing in the company's value; it only makes one share cheaper. Many people hold one company only as part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
