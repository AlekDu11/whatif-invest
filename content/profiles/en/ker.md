**Kernel** is one of Ukraine's biggest farming and food-export companies: it grows grain, crushes sunflower seeds into oil and ships both abroad through its own terminals. It says it supplies about 8% of the world's sunflower oil exports to more than 70 countries. Its shares have traded in Warsaw since November 2007, but by early 2025 its main owner held about 95%, and in late 2026 it began buying out the rest at PLN 19.93 a share before delisting.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Fiscal year to June 2026: revenue $4.40 billion (+7%), EBITDA $558 million (+20%), net profit $322 million (+35%).
> - Russian attacks on ports hit the July–September 2026 quarter: grain exports fell 54% and edible oil sales 48%.
> - Founder Andriy Verevskyi's company Namsen owns about 95%; the remaining 4.83% can be sold to it at PLN 19.93 until 29 December 2026.
> - After Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022, Kernel's market value fell by more than 60%.

## How it makes money

Kernel reports in US dollars and has three segments. **Oilseed processing**: it buys sunflower seeds from Ukrainian farmers, crushes them and sells bottled and bulk oil plus sunflower meal for animal feed — 3.19 million tonnes processed and 1.41 million tonnes of oil sold in fiscal 2026. **Infrastructure and trading**: grain storage (silos), rail and ships, export terminals in Ukrainian ports, and trading of grain and oils through its Avere arm. **Farming**: it grows corn, wheat, sunflower and rapeseed on about 530,000 hectares for the 2026 harvest, 48% more than a year before.

Farming profits swing with harvests and with accounting: in July–September 2025 the segment earned $111 million of EBITDA, but $59 million of that came from revaluing crops in the field.

In the fiscal year from July 2025 to June 2026 Kernel earned $4.40 billion in revenue, $558 million of EBITDA (profit before interest, tax and depreciation) and $322 million of net profit. Its silos took in a record 4.34 million tonnes of grain, 60% more than a year earlier. Crushing fell 8% because Ukraine had fewer sunflower seeds to buy.

The new fiscal year started badly. Russian strikes damaged port facilities in July 2026 and blocked most Black Sea shipments for the quarter; terminal throughput fell 59%. Rerouting via the Danube and western borders helped only partly. Kernel also expects its corn yield to drop 13% to 8.1 tonnes per hectare after heat and drought.

## The stock's story

Kernel listed in Warsaw in 2007 and raised about PLN 1.2 billion from the IPO and three later share sales up to 2011. The full-scale war in 2022 cut its market value by more than 60%. In March 2023 Namsen, Verevskyi's holding, proposed leaving the exchange; through share purchases and a new share placement it lifted its stake from about 41% to about 95% by early 2025. In late 2026 it began squeezing out the last minority holders at PLN 19.93, a price set by Luxembourg's regulator CSSF using valuations from KPMG and Grant Thornton. In the 52 weeks to November 2026 the shares traded between PLN 18.60 and PLN 23.20.


## Competitors

Kernel competes with other Ukrainian sunflower oil producers and grain exporters, and on world markets with global traders such as Cargill, Bunge and ADM. Its edge is the whole chain in one company, from fields to its own port terminals.

## Main risks

- **War:** ports, plants and logistics are targets; one quarter of strikes cut exports by more than half.
- **Delisting:** once trading in Warsaw ends, shares become very hard to sell; the official exit is the PLN 19.93 buyout.
- **Weather and prices:** harvests and crushing margins change from year to year, as the 13% lower corn yield shows.
- **Currency:** Kernel earns in dollars while the shares trade in złoty, so the exchange rate moves the price too.

## What it means if you buy regularly

In practice there is little left to buy regularly: one owner held about 95% by 2025 and the remaining shares were being bought out in late 2026, so almost no free float was left. Its history shows that war and a delisting can matter more to small shareholders than profits. For regular buyers the broader lesson is to spread money across many companies and markets. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
