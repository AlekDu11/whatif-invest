**Chainlink** is the network that tells blockchains what is happening in the outside world — prices, interest rates, reserves — and lets them talk to each other. More than $110 billion in crypto apps depends on its data, and banks such as Swift, DTCC, Fidelity and UBS are testing or using it.

## How it works

A blockchain cannot read the internet by itself. Chainlink solves this with "oracles": independent node operators that fetch data from many sources, agree on one answer and post it on-chain. Lending apps use these price feeds to decide when a loan is under-collateralised. Its second product, CCIP, moves tokens and messages between more than 60 blockchains. The project was launched in 2017 by Sergey Nazarov and Steve Ellis.

## What LINK is used for

LINK pays node operators for their work, and holders can stake (lock) LINK to help secure the network and earn rewards. Supply is capped at 1 billion tokens, about 767 million of which are in circulation. A "Chainlink Reserve" buys LINK with part of the fees the network earns, linking usage to the token.

## Why it matters

Chainlink is the market leader among oracles, with roughly 60–68% of the value they secure, and it has recorded more than $30 trillion in transaction value. One surprising fact: most people who use DeFi have never heard of Chainlink, yet many of their loans depend on its price feeds every minute.

## Main risks

LINK is volatile: it peaked at $52.70 in May 2021 and trades far below that today. Adoption by banks does not automatically mean demand for the token, because fees can be paid in other ways. Rival oracles (Pyth, RedStone) compete on price and speed, and a bad data feed or bug could cause losses. Rules for crypto differ by country. Buying a fixed amount regularly smooths the swings but does not remove the risk.
