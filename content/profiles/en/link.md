**Chainlink** is the network that tells blockchains what is happening in the outside world — prices, interest rates, reserves — and moves tokens and messages between chains. ## How it works

A blockchain cannot read the internet by itself. Chainlink solves this with "oracles": independent node operators that fetch data from many sources, agree on one answer and post it on-chain. A lending app uses these price feeds to decide when a loan has too little collateral. Its second big product, CCIP, moves tokens and messages between blockchains; in 2026 more than $15 billion of assets moved onto it from other bridges, including $7.7 billion of wrapped bitcoin (WBTC). The project was launched in 2017 by Sergey Nazarov and Steve Ellis.

## What the token is used for

LINK pays node operators for their work, and holders can stake (lock) LINK to help secure the network and earn rewards. The Chainlink Reserve buys LINK with part of the fees the network earns, so more usage can mean more demand for the token. Big finance is a growing source of attention: Swift, DTCC and the Bank of England's test lab have worked with Chainlink, Charles Schwab added LINK to its crypto offer, and US LINK ETFs from Grayscale and Bitwise held about 16.7 million LINK in Q3 2026.

## Its story so far

LINK was sold in a September 2017 token sale for about $0.11. It became one of the stars of the 2020 DeFi boom, when lending apps needed reliable prices, and peaked at $52.70 in May 2021. In the 2022 crash it fell below $6. CCIP launched in 2023, staking followed, and banks began pilot projects. LINK rose again in 2024–2025 but stayed far below its record and slid in 2026, trading near $9 in November even as Chainlink's own usage figures kept growing.

## Alternatives

Rival oracles include [Pyth Network]({lang}/pyth/), [Band Protocol]({lang}/band/), [API3]({lang}/api3/) and [Tellor]({lang}/trb/), plus newer services such as RedStone. For moving assets between chains, CCIP competes with bridges like LayerZero and Wormhole.

## Main risks

- **Big swings:** LINK has lost more than 80% from its peak; such drops can happen again.
- **Usage vs token:** banks using Chainlink does not automatically mean demand for LINK, because services can be paid in other ways.
- **Competition:** Pyth, RedStone and others compete on price and speed.
- **Technical risk:** a wrong price feed or a bridge bug could cause large losses for the apps that rely on it.
- **Concentration and supply:** the team and the Chainlink company hold a large share of tokens, and more LINK enters circulation over time; rules also differ by country.

## What it means if you buy regularly

LINK's history shows long periods far below its record, even while the network grew. Buying a fixed amount regularly means you buy more when the price is low and less when it is high, without trying to guess the bottom — but it does not remove the risk of losses. Many people keep crypto to a small part of their savings that they can afford to see fall sharply. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
