**Rocket Lab** launches small satellites into orbit with its Electron rocket and, increasingly, builds the satellites and their parts too. Governments, space agencies and companies pay it to put spacecraft in space, and buy solar panels, reaction wheels and complete satellite platforms from it. Founded in New Zealand in 2006 by Peter Beck, who is still CEO, it is now based in Long Beach, California, and listed on Nasdaq in August 2021 through a SPAC merger.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: a record $267.8 million in Q3 2026, up about 73% a year, with a net loss of $79.7 million.
> - Backlog (signed but not yet delivered work) jumped 52% in one quarter to $3.57 billion.
> - Neutron, its bigger reusable rocket, was pushed back in 2026 to no earlier than 2027 after test problems.
> - In June 2026 it agreed to buy satellite operator Iridium for about $8 billion including debt; closing is expected in mid-2027.

## How it makes money

Rocket Lab has two businesses. **Launch services**: Electron carries small satellites from pads in New Zealand and Virginia, and HASTE is a version used for suborbital test flights, mostly for US defence. **Space systems**: it designs and builds spacecraft and components for clients such as NASA, the US Space Force and Globalstar. In Q3 2026 product sales brought in $197.5 million and services $70.3 million. The big bet is **Neutron**, a medium rocket meant to compete for larger launches; Rocket Lab already holds 16 Neutron missions, with a base price of $70 million for several of them.

In the quarter to 30 September 2026 revenue reached $267.8 million against $155.1 million a year earlier. Adjusted gross margin was 38.4%, and the adjusted EBITDA loss narrowed to $18.0 million. The company signed about $1 billion of new launch contracts and $470 million of space systems work in the quarter. It held $4.45 billion in cash, largely because it sold $3.91 billion of new shares in the first nine months of the year. For Q4 2026 it guides to $270–290 million of revenue. In June 2026 it agreed to buy satellite operator Iridium for about $8 billion including debt — $27 in cash plus Rocket Lab shares for each Iridium share — with closing expected in mid-2027.

## The stock's story

Rocket Lab has had two extreme rides. After the 2021 listing it fell about 83% from its September 2021 peak to April 2024. Then strong growth and defence contracts sent it to $150.23 on 27 May 2026, a record at that time. Then it dropped sharply: in August 2026 the company warned Neutron's first flight could slip to 2027, investors worried about dilution from share sales, and shares slid further when the delay was confirmed in October.

## Competitors

In launches the giant is SpaceX, whose Falcon 9 and rideshare missions set the price for the whole industry; others include Firefly Aerospace, Blue Origin and United Launch Alliance. In satellites and parts Rocket Lab competes with traditional defence contractors such as Northrop Grumman and Lockheed Martin. Its advantage is being one of very few companies that can both build a satellite and launch it.

## Main risks

- **Neutron execution:** the rocket is already years late, and a failure or further delay would hurt the growth story.
- **Dilution:** billions of dollars of new shares have been sold, and the Iridium deal will be paid partly in stock.
- **Still loss-making:** about $227 million lost over the last 12 months; profit depends on Neutron and higher volumes.
- **Big acquisition:** Iridium is larger than anything Rocket Lab has bought and needs regulatory approval.
- **SpaceX** can cut prices and launch far more often than anyone else.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Rocket Lab has fallen more than 80% once and about 63% again within months, while its revenue kept growing. Regular buying spreads purchases over those swings, but a young, unprofitable company can stay volatile for years, so many people treat a stock like this as a small part of a broader portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
