**Orlen** is one of Poland's biggest companies and Central Europe's largest energy group. It refines crude oil into petrol and diesel, produces and sells natural gas, generates and distributes electricity, makes plastics and chemicals, and runs petrol stations across Poland, Germany, Czechia, Lithuania and other countries. Listed in Warsaw since 1999, it grew much bigger in 2022 by absorbing rivals Lotos and the gas company PGNiG; the Polish state owns 49.9% of the shares.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Net profit: PLN 5.6 billion in Q3 2026 and PLN 21.3 billion in the first nine months of the year.
> - Q3 revenue was PLN 80.1 billion (+31% a year) — but profit came in below analysts' forecasts.
> - Shareholders received a record dividend of PLN 8 per share for 2025, PLN 9.3 billion in total.
> - The stock broke above PLN 150 for the first time in July 2026, after its market value doubled in a year.

## How it makes money

Orlen reports four main segments, measured by LIFO EBITDA (operating profit before depreciation, with fuel stocks valued at recent prices). In Q3 2026: **Downstream** — refineries in Płock, Gdańsk, Mažeikiai in Lithuania and in Czechia plus petrochemicals — earned PLN 6.3 billion from 10.1 million tonnes of crude processed. **Upstream & Supply** — oil and gas fields, mainly in Norway and Poland, producing 183,000 barrels of oil equivalent a day, plus gas imports and trading — earned PLN 4.7 billion. **Energy** — power plants, wind and solar farms and the Energa grid — earned PLN 2.7 billion. **Consumers & Products** — petrol stations, their shops and sales to retail customers — earned PLN 1.5 billion. About a third of group revenue comes from outside Poland.

In the quarter to 30 September 2026 Orlen made PLN 80.1 billion in revenue, about PLN 14.7 billion of LIFO EBITDA (+66% a year) and PLN 5.6 billion of net profit. Analysts had expected more — around PLN 15.3 billion of EBITDA and over PLN 7.5 billion of profit. It invested PLN 7.8 billion in the quarter and at the end of September held slightly more cash than debt. In all of 2025 it earned PLN 11.2 billion on PLN 267.3 billion of revenue. Management said it will pay about PLN 2.4 billion under Poland's windfall tax law, to be booked in Q4.

## The stock's story

For years Orlen traded cheaply, partly because investors worried that the state's interests came before shareholders'. The 2022 mergers with Lotos and PGNiG multiplied its size, and a new management under CEO Ireneusz Fąfara took over in 2024 after the previous chief, Daniel Obajtek, was dismissed. Strong profits and record dividends then fuelled a rally: the market value doubled in twelve months and the share price passed PLN 150 for the first time in July 2026. By November 2026 it had eased back from that level; our figures are in US dollars, so zloty moves count too.

## Competitors

In fuel retail Orlen competes with Shell, BP, Circle K and supermarket stations; in refining and petrochemicals with Hungary's MOL and Austria's OMV; in Polish power with PGE, Tauron and Enea. Its strengths are scale, a dominant position in Poland's fuel and gas supply and its own gas output in Norway.

## Main risks

- **State control:** the government is the largest shareholder; Orlen's pricing programme kept Polish fuel the cheapest in the EU, which helps drivers but can limit margins.
- **Taxes:** the Polish windfall tax costs about PLN 2.4 billion, and Lithuania has floated its own levy on energy firms, which Orlen warned could affect investment at Mažeikiai.
- **Commodity prices:** oil, gas and refining margins swing profit from quarter to quarter.
- **Huge spending plans:** offshore wind (Baltic Power), grids and new gas fields need tens of billions of zloty; delays or overruns hurt returns.
- **Energy transition:** demand for petrol and diesel in Europe is expected to shrink over time.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Orlen pays a large dividend, but its share price follows oil, gas and politics, and for a foreign buyer the zloty adds another swing. Regular buying evens out the cycles of a commodity company, yet a single state-controlled energy stock is a concentrated bet — many people keep it as a small part of a broader portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
