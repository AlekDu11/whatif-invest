**Baker Hughes** is a Houston-based energy-technology company. It sells drilling, well and production services to oil and gas producers, and it builds big machines — gas turbines, compressors and equipment for liquefied natural gas (LNG) plants — that are now also used to power factories and data centres. Its weekly count of active drilling rigs, published since 1944, is one of the best-known numbers in the oil industry.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Q3 2026: revenue $8.03 billion (+14% on the year), orders $10.76 billion (+31%).
> - Its order backlog (remaining performance obligations) reached a record $46.4 billion at the end of September 2026.
> - It completed the all-cash purchase of Chart Industries on 16 July 2026, paying about $14.2 billion in cash.
> - It pays a $0.23 quarterly dividend and bought back no shares in the first nine months of 2026.

## How it makes money

Baker Hughes has two main businesses. **Oilfield Services & Equipment** (OFSE) helps oil and gas companies drill and complete wells and keep them producing; it earned $3.66 billion of revenue in Q3 2026 and moves with how much producers spend on drilling. **Industrial & Energy Technology** (IET) makes gas turbines, compressors, LNG equipment and industrial products, and earns steady money from servicing them for decades; it had $3.48 billion of revenue in the quarter. Since July 2026 a third piece, **Chart**, adds tanks, heat exchangers and other equipment for LNG, hydrogen and industrial gases ($888 million of revenue in Q3).

## The stock's story

The company's roots go back to two early-1900s inventors: Reuben C. Baker, whose Baker Oil Tools started in 1907, and Howard Hughes Sr., whose Hughes Tool Company sold a famous rock drill bit from 1909. The two businesses merged in 1987 to form Baker Hughes. In 2017 General Electric combined its oil and gas unit with it, and GE later sold its stake; the company took its current name in 2019 and trades on Nasdaq. Under CEO Lorenzo Simonelli, in charge since 2017, it has shifted from pure oilfield services towards gas and LNG technology, selling smaller units such as Precision Sensors & Instrumentation and Surface Pressure Control in 2026 and buying Chart.

## Competitors

In oilfield services the main rivals are SLB and Halliburton. In gas turbines and power equipment Baker Hughes competes with GE Vernova, Siemens Energy and Mitsubishi Heavy Industries; in LNG equipment, with other engineering suppliers.

## Main risks

- **The oil cycle:** if oil prices fall, producers cut drilling and OFSE sales shrink.
- **Big projects:** IET depends on large LNG and gas projects, which can be delayed or cancelled.
- **Debt and integration:** the Chart deal raised debt to about $18 billion; integration must go well.
- **Energy transition:** a faster shift away from oil and gas would hurt the older part of the business.
- **Competition:** SLB, Halliburton and the big turbine makers fight hard on price and technology.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Baker Hughes is tied to energy spending, so its shares can swing hard when oil and gas prices move — they fell sharply in the 2020 oil crash. Buying a fixed amount regularly spreads purchases over those ups and downs instead of betting on one moment. The dividend adds a small cash return. Many people hold one energy stock only as part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
