**KGHM Polska Miedź** digs copper and silver out of the ground in south-west Poland, melts it into pure metal and sells it to factories that make cables, wires and electronics. It is one of the world's largest silver producers and among the ten biggest copper miners. Founded in 1961 and based in Lubin, it has been listed on the Warsaw Stock Exchange since July 1997, and the Polish state is its largest shareholder.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: 36.7 billion zł in January–September 2026, +42% a year, almost all thanks to higher metal prices.
> - Net profit for the nine months: 7.5 billion zł, 7.4 times more than a year earlier.
> - Output barely moved: 533 thousand tonnes of payable copper (+1%) and 1,073 tonnes of silver (+8%).
> - From its 2025 profit KGHM paid a dividend of 1.50 zł per share; net debt was only 0.3 times a year's EBITDA in Q3 2026.

## How it makes money

KGHM sells metal, so its income depends on two things it cannot control: world copper and silver prices and the dollar-zloty rate. The core is in Poland: underground mines in the Legnica-Głogów copper basin and the Głogów and Legnica smelters. In the first nine months of 2026 the Polish operations produced 440.9 thousand tonnes of copper, about 83% of the group's total, plus almost all of its silver.

The rest comes from abroad. KGHM owns 55% of the Sierra Gorda open-pit mine in Chile (63.1 thousand tonnes of copper for KGHM in nine months) and KGHM International, with mines such as Robinson in Nevada (29.3 thousand tonnes).

In the third quarter of 2026 (July–September) revenue rose to 12.04 billion zł from 8.32 billion a year earlier, and net profit jumped to 1.94 billion zł from 433 million. For January–September, adjusted EBITDA (profit before interest, tax and depreciation) was about 13.2 billion zł, up 83%. The cash cost of producing copper (C1) was $2.54 per pound, and debt was low: net debt equalled only 0.3 times a year's EBITDA. Investment spending reached almost 2.5 billion zł. From its 2025 profit KGHM paid a dividend of 1.50 zł per share.

The downside of high prices: Poland's special tax on extracting copper and silver rose 110% year on year, because it grows with metal prices.

## The stock's story

KGHM's share price moves with copper and silver. The special mining tax, in force since 18 April 2012, has weighed on it ever since. In late 2015, when metal prices were falling and the government said the tax would stay, the stock dropped below 70 zł. The 2025–2026 metals boom changed the picture: in the 52 weeks to mid-November 2026 the shares traded as high as 396.40 zł. Then they cooled: on 18 November 2026 they closed at 264.30 zł, 13.8% lower than five days earlier, as copper fell to its lowest level in more than six months.

## Competitors

KGHM competes with the big copper miners: Chile's state-owned Codelco, Freeport-McMoRan, BHP, Glencore and Southern Copper; in silver, with Mexico's Fresnillo. Its strengths are silver-rich ore, its own smelters and closeness to European customers; its weaknesses are deep, ageing mines and a tax rivals do not pay.

## Main risks

- **Metal prices:** a fall in copper or silver hits profit fast, because most costs stay the same.
- **Mining tax:** the government has talked about easing it for years, but in Q3 2026 it still more than doubled.
- **The state as owner:** decisions on dividends, investments and management can follow political goals.
- **Exchange rate:** metal is priced in dollars, so a stronger zloty means fewer zlotys per tonne.
- **Projects:** big investments such as expanding Sierra Gorda (about $725 million) can run late or over budget.

## What it means if you buy regularly

KGHM is a cyclical stock: it follows metal prices up and down, and long flat stretches are normal. Regular buying puts part of your money in at cheap moments, such as late 2015, and part at expensive ones, such as the 2026 peak. A commodity company can lose much of its value when prices turn, so many people hold it only as part of a broader portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
