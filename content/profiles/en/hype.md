**Hyperliquid** is a crypto exchange that runs entirely on its own blockchain, mostly for perpetual futures — bets on a price going up or down with no expiry date. Its coin, HYPE, secures the network, and most of the exchange's trading fees are spent buying HYPE back from the market. HYPE's market value was about $15 billion in November 2026.

> **Key takeaways**
> - HYPE launched on 29 November 2024 with no venture-capital investors: 31% of the 1 billion tokens went straight to users in an airdrop.
> - Hyperliquid handled about $2.6 trillion of trades in 2025, almost twice Coinbase's volume, according to Artemis data.
> - In December 2025 validators agreed to treat about 37 million HYPE bought back by the Assistance Fund as burned — over 13% of the supply then circulating.
> - The record price so far is $97.96 (September 2026); the team's 23.8% share has been unlocking month by month since November 2025.

## How it works

The chain has two parts. **HyperCore** is the exchange itself: order books for perpetual futures and spot trading, where every order and liquidation is recorded on-chain at the speed of a normal exchange. **HyperEVM**, launched in February 2025, is an Ethereum-compatible layer where other apps — lending, stablecoins, trading tools — plug into the exchange. Traders deposit stablecoins and trade bitcoin, ether, hundreds of other coins and, since the HIP-3 upgrade in October 2025, markets created by outside "builders", including oil and stock-style contracts. A community vault called HLP acts as a market maker and takes over liquidated positions.

## Why people think it has value

HYPE is staked with validators to secure the network, used to vote on upgrades and paid as gas on HyperEVM. The main argument for its value is the link to real revenue: for core crypto markets about 97% of fees go to the Assistance Fund, which buys HYPE every day. Gross revenue peaked at $457 million in the third quarter of 2025. Institutional demand grew too: Bitwise's spot HYPE ETF started trading on the NYSE in May 2026.

## Its story so far

Hyperliquid was built by a small, self-funded team led by Jeff Yan, a former high-frequency trader at Hudson River Trading. The exchange went live in 2023, and the HYPE token followed on 29 November 2024 in one of crypto's largest airdrops. March 2025 brought two tests: a whale's huge ether position left the HLP vault about $4 million down, and a squeeze on the JELLY memecoin pushed the exchange to delist that market within hours — a quick decision that raised questions about how decentralised it is. In the 10 October 2025 crash, open interest fell from $14.7 billion to $6.5 billion in one day. The first team unlock came on 29 November 2025. By September 2026 open interest was back above $14 billion and HYPE set its record. In November 2026 a third-party wrapped Monero token, XMR1, crashed 99.8% on Hyperliquid after its deployer key was compromised.

## Alternatives

On-chain rivals include [Aster]({lang}/aster/), [dYdX]({lang}/dydx/), [GMX]({lang}/gmx/) and [Jupiter]({lang}/jup/)'s perps market. For many traders the real alternative is still a large centralised exchange such as Binance, whose coin is [BNB]({lang}/bnb/).

## Main risks

- **Token unlocks:** the core contributors' 23.8% keeps unlocking every month, and less than a quarter of the maximum supply circulates.
- **Concentration:** the Hyper Foundation controls a large share of staked HYPE, and the validator set is small.
- **Manipulation and losses:** the JELLY squeeze, whale liquidations and the XMR1 crash show how thin markets and leverage can hit the HLP vault and traders.
- **Volume dependence:** buybacks shrink when trading slows — revenue fell to $202 million in the second quarter of 2026 — and newer rivals compete on fees.
- **Regulation:** many countries restrict leveraged crypto futures, and US regulators are still deciding how such venues may serve American users.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Our price history for HYPE starts only in September 2026, so the "$10 a day" figure above covers just a few weeks. Buying a fixed amount regularly averages your entry price, but it cannot protect you from falling volume or new supply. Many people keep a single exchange token to a small share of savings they can afford to lose. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
