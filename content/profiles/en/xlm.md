**Stellar** is a payment network built to move money across borders in about five seconds for a tiny fraction of a cent. Banks, fintechs and fund managers use it to issue digital dollars, euros and tokenised funds, and its coin, XLM ("lumens"), pays the fees. XLM had a market value of about $4.5 billion in November 2026, around number 20 among cryptocurrencies.

> **Key takeaways**
> - About 50 billion XLM exist after a 2019 burn of roughly 55 billion coins; no new ones are created, and about 35 billion were in circulation in November 2026.
> - A payment settles in about 5 seconds, and the fee is 0.00001 XLM per operation.
> - The record price so far is $0.88 (January 2018); in November 2026 XLM traded more than 80% below it.
> - The value of real-world assets such as tokenised funds on Stellar passed $4 billion in 2026, according to industry trackers.

## How it works

Stellar has no miners. Instead, it uses the Stellar Consensus Protocol: a set of trusted organisations — such as the Stellar Development Foundation (SDF), universities and payment companies — vote to confirm each new ledger every few seconds. Companies called "anchors" hold real money and issue matching tokens on the network, and Stellar's built-in exchange can swap one currency for another along the way. Since February 2024 the network also runs smart contracts, called Soroban. A further upgrade, "Bell", was put to a validator vote on 23 November 2026, alongside a plan to make accounts quantum-safe by 2027.

## What the token is used for

XLM pays the small fee on every transaction and every account must hold a minimum balance, which keeps spam out. It also works as a bridge currency: two rare currencies can be swapped through XLM when no direct market exists. Most money moving on Stellar is not XLM but stablecoins and tokenised assets — Circle's USDC, MoneyGram's cash-to-crypto service and Franklin Templeton's US government money-market fund all run on it. That brings attention to the network, but it does not automatically mean more demand for the coin.

## Its story so far

Stellar was launched in 2014 by Jed McCaleb, a co-founder of Ripple, and Joyce Kim, and started as a fork of Ripple's code before writing its own consensus system in 2015. In the 2017 boom XLM set its record of about $0.88 in January 2018, then lost more than 90% by early 2020. In November 2019 the SDF burned about 55 billion lumens and stopped the 1% yearly inflation, fixing the supply at around 50 billion. XLM jumped again in 2021 and in late 2024, each time together with XRP, but stayed far below its record and slid in 2026, trading near $0.13 in November.

## Alternatives

Stellar's closest rival is [XRP]({lang}/xrp/), which also targets cross-border payments and comes from the same roots. Other networks for fast, cheap transfers and tokenised assets include [Hedera]({lang}/hbar/), [Algorand]({lang}/algo/) and [Solana]({lang}/sol/); banks also build their own private systems.

## Main risks

- **Big swings:** XLM has lost more than 90% from its peak; such drops can happen again.
- **Usage vs token:** fees are tiny and most transfers are in stablecoins, so payment volume adds little direct demand for XLM.
- **Concentration:** the SDF still holds billions of lumens outside circulation and releases them for grants and partnerships, which can weigh on the price.
- **Competition:** XRP, Solana, other blockchains and banks' own networks compete for the same payments.
- **Regulation:** rules for payments and stablecoins differ by country and can change quickly.

## What it means if you buy regularly

XLM's history shows short, sharp rallies and long periods far below its record. Buying a fixed amount regularly means you buy more when the price is low and less when it is high, without trying to guess the bottom — but it does not remove the risk of losses. Many people keep crypto to a small part of their savings that they can afford to see fall sharply. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
