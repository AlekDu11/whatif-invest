**Stellar** is a payment network built to move money across borders in about five seconds for a tiny fraction of a cent — and XLM, or "lumens", is the coin that keeps it running. It was launched in 2014 by Jed McCaleb, a co-founder of Ripple, and Joyce Kim.

## How it works

Instead of miners, Stellar uses its own voting system, the Stellar Consensus Protocol, in which trusted organisations confirm each new ledger every few seconds. Banks, fintechs and payment firms ("anchors") issue digital dollars, euros and other assets on the network, and Stellar's built-in exchange can swap one for another on the way. XLM pays the fee — 0.00001 XLM per operation — and every account must hold a small minimum balance, which keeps spam out.

## Why it matters

Stellar is one of the main rails for real-world payments and tokenised assets: Circle's USDC runs on it, MoneyGram uses it for cash-to-crypto transfers, and Franklin Templeton put a US government money-market fund on Stellar. Since 2024 the network also supports smart contracts (Soroban). A surprising fact: in 2019 the non-profit Stellar Development Foundation (SDF) burned about 55 billion lumens — more than half of all coins — and ended new issuance, fixing the supply at roughly 50 billion.

## Main risks

The SDF still holds a large share of lumens (about 14–15 billion are outside circulation) and releases them for grants and partnerships, which can weigh on the price. Payment volume on the network does not translate directly into demand for XLM, because fees are tiny and most transfers are in stablecoins. Stellar competes with XRP, Solana and banks' own systems, and its price is still far below its January 2018 record. Regular buying smooths out the swings but does not remove the risk.
