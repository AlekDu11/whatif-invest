**Dogecoin** is the cryptocurrency that started as a joke — a Shiba Inu meme turned into money in December 2013 — and grew into one of the most traded coins in the world, with its own spot ETFs in the US.

## How it works

Dogecoin was created by programmer Billy Markus and Adobe employee Jackson Palmer as a lighthearted copy of Bitcoin's idea. Like Bitcoin, it runs on a public blockchain secured by miners. A new block arrives about every minute instead of every ten, so transfers confirm faster and fees are usually tiny. Since 2014 it has been "merge-mined" with Litecoin: the same machines secure both networks at once.

## What DOGE is used for

DOGE is mostly used for tipping, small payments and trading. It has no smart contracts and no company behind it; volunteers and the Dogecoin Foundation maintain the code. Its price is driven by community mood, social media and celebrity attention — Elon Musk's posts fuelled the 2021 boom. Since January 2026, US investors can also buy it through spot ETFs such as 21Shares' TDOG on Nasdaq.

## Why it matters

Unlike Bitcoin, Dogecoin has **no maximum supply**. About 5 billion new coins are created every year, on top of roughly 157 billion already in circulation. A surprising detail: because the number of new coins is fixed, inflation as a percentage falls a little every year — now about 3.3%.

## Main risks

Dogecoin is extremely volatile: by November 2026 it was more than 90% below its 2021 peak. New coins keep diluting holders, and much of the supply sits in a few large wallets. Demand depends on hype that can disappear quickly. There are no cash flows or revenue behind the price. Buying a fixed amount regularly smooths the swings but does not remove the risk.
