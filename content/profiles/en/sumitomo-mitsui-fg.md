**Sumitomo Mitsui Financial Group (SMFG)** is one of Japan's three "megabanks", alongside MUFG and Mizuho. Through Sumitomo Mitsui Banking Corporation (SMBC) it lends to Japanese companies and households, finances big projects and deals around the world, and it also runs the broker SMBC Nikko, the credit-card business Sumitomo Mitsui Card and the Olive banking app. For years Japanese banks earned almost nothing on deposits because of zero and negative interest rates; since the Bank of Japan started raising rates in March 2024, their profits — and SMFG's shares — have climbed.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Net profit: a record ¥1.58 trillion in the fiscal year to March 2026, up about 34% on the year.
> - In April–September 2026 net profit was ¥1.003 trillion, up from ¥933.5 billion a year earlier.
> - On 13 November 2026 SMFG raised its forecast for the year to March 2027 to ¥1.9 trillion (from ¥1.7 trillion) and lifted its share buyback to ¥400 billion.
> - Planned dividend for the year to March 2027: ¥200 per share, after a 2-for-1 share split in autumn 2026.

## How it makes money

Like any bank, SMFG earns the difference between what it pays on deposits and what it charges on loans — the "interest margin". When Japanese rates were zero that margin was thin; each Bank of Japan rate rise lets the bank charge more on loans faster than it pays more to savers. The second big source is fees: arranging loans and bonds for companies, advising on mergers, selling investment products through SMBC Nikko, card and payment fees from Sumitomo Mitsui Card, and asset management. A growing share of profit comes from outside Japan — lending to large companies and infrastructure projects in the Americas, Europe and Asia, and stakes in banks in fast-growing markets such as India, Indonesia, Vietnam and the Philippines.

For the fiscal year that ended on 31 March 2026, SMFG reported a record net profit of ¥1.58 trillion, against ¥1.18 trillion a year earlier, and ordinary profit of ¥2.30 trillion. In the first half of the next fiscal year (April–September 2026) net profit reached ¥1.003 trillion. The July–September quarter alone was 10% lower than a year earlier, but only because that earlier quarter had included a ¥94 billion gain from selling its stake in India's Kotak Mahindra Bank.

On 13 November 2026 the group raised its full-year net profit forecast to ¥1.9 trillion and more than doubled its planned buyback, from ¥180 billion to ¥400 billion. It planned an interim dividend of ¥100 and a full-year dividend of ¥200 per share.

## The stock's story

SMBC was created in April 2001 by merging Sumitomo Bank with Sakura Bank (itself the heir of the old Mitsui Bank), and the SMFG holding company was set up in December 2002, when Japanese banks were still cleaning up bad loans from the 1990s bubble. The shares rose in the mid-2000s recovery, then lost more than two-thirds of their value between 2006 and 2010 in the global financial crisis. For the next decade they went mostly sideways while rates stayed near zero. The turn came with inflation and the end of negative rates: in WhatIfStack's data the shares more than quadrupled between January 2022 and mid-2026. SMFG split each share into three in October 2024 and each share into two again in autumn 2026, so old per-share prices are not comparable with today's. In November 2026 CEO Toru Nakashima warned that a slowdown in data-centre investment could affect results, and said the bank now limits such lending to large, highly rated cloud companies.

## Competitors

At home SMFG competes with Mitsubishi UFJ Financial Group (MUFG), Mizuho, Resona, Japan Post Bank, regional banks and fast-growing online banks such as Rakuten Bank and SBI. In investment banking and global lending it faces Wall Street and European banks; in cards and payments it competes with Rakuten, PayPay and others.

## Main risks

- **Interest rates:** profits now depend a lot on the Bank of Japan continuing to raise rates; a pause or cut would squeeze the margin.
- **Bad loans:** a recession in Japan or abroad, or trouble in hot areas such as data centres or private credit, could force the bank to write off loans.
- **Overseas exposure:** stakes in emerging-market banks and lending abroad bring currency and country risk.
- **Bond losses:** when rates rise, the value of bonds the bank holds falls.
- **Regulation and capital rules:** stricter rules could limit buybacks and dividends.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Bank shares move with the economy and with interest rates, so SMFG can fall sharply in a crisis — as it did in 2008–2009 — even when the long-term story looks good. Buying a fixed amount regularly averages out those swings instead of betting on one entry point. For a buyer outside Japan, the yen exchange rate also changes the result. Many people hold a single bank only as part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
