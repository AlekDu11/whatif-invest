**Adobe** makes the tools behind a huge share of the world's pictures, videos and documents: Photoshop, Premiere Pro, Illustrator, Acrobat and the PDF format itself. Almost all of it is sold as a monthly or yearly subscription, and the business keeps setting revenue records. Yet the stock trades at a fraction of its 2021 peak, because investors fear that AI image and design tools will make paid creative software less necessary.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: a record $6.76 billion in the quarter to 28 August 2026, +13% a year; full-year guidance $26.58–26.63 billion.
> - Annual recurring revenue (subscriptions on a yearly basis) reached $27.5 billion; "AI-first" products grew more than 150%.
> - The stock closed at a record $688.37 on 19 November 2021 and dropped below $190 in June 2026.
> - Anil Chakravarthy becomes CEO on 1 December 2026; Shantanu Narayen, CEO since 2007, moves to executive chair.

## How it makes money

Adobe now reports by type of customer. Creative and marketing professionals — designers, video editors, agencies and corporate marketing teams — brought $4.65 billion of subscription revenue in the quarter (+13%). Business professionals and consumers — people who use Acrobat, Adobe Express or the phone apps — added $1.91 billion (+16%). Large companies also buy Adobe's marketing and customer-experience software to run websites, campaigns and analytics. About 97% of revenue is subscriptions, and Adobe says its apps reached 1 billion monthly active users.

## The numbers right now

In the third quarter of fiscal 2026 Adobe earned $6.76 billion, with GAAP operating income of $2.35 billion — a margin of about 35%. GAAP earnings per share were $4.62. Cash flow from operations was a record $2.52 billion, and the company spent $2.23 billion buying back about 9.5 million of its own shares. It held $4.36 billion in cash plus $1.28 billion in short-term investments against $6.36 billion of debt. For the fourth quarter it guided to $6.80–6.85 billion of revenue. Adobe pays no dividend; it returns cash through buybacks.

## The stock's story

Adobe was one of the big winners of the move to subscriptions in the 2010s, and the stock hit its record close of $688.37 in November 2021. It fell hard in 2022, recovered in 2023, then slid again as generative AI arrived. In 2026 the pressure grew: in March Narayen said he would step down, the finance chief left in June, and new AI design tools raised doubts about whether people will keep paying for Creative Cloud. Right after a record second-quarter report, the shares fell below $190. Over the last 52 weeks they traded between $190.12 and $362.71, and the price is about 13 times yearly earnings.

## Competitors

In design Adobe faces Canva, which wins many non-professional users, and Figma for interface design. In video, Blackmagic Design's DaVinci Resolve offers a strong free version. The fastest-growing threat comes from AI tools that create images, video and layouts from a text prompt, made by companies such as OpenAI, Google, Midjourney and Anthropic. Adobe's defence is its huge base of professional users, file formats everyone uses, and its own Firefly AI models built into the apps.

## Main risks

- **AI makes creation cheaper:** if good-enough images and videos come free from chatbots, fewer people may pay for professional tools.
- **Slower growth:** Adobe targets 10.2% growth in ending recurring revenue this year, solid but far below its earlier pace.
- **Leadership change:** a new CEO from 1 December 2026, after the finance chief left in June, means strategy may shift.
- **Pricing pressure:** with cheap or free rivals a click away, raising subscription prices gets harder, and many casual users may drop paid plans.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Adobe is highly profitable and still growing, but its share price has more than halved from the peak while revenue kept rising — a reminder that a strong business and a rising stock are not the same thing. Regular buying spreads purchases over such long slumps, but nobody knows how long a slump lasts, so many people hold a single stock like this as one part of a broader portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
