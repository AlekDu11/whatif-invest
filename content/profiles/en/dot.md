**Polkadot** is a blockchain built to connect many other blockchains, so they can share security and pass tokens and messages to each other. It was designed by Gavin Wood, a co-founder of Ethereum, and its network went live in 2020. Its token, DOT, is worth {{mcap:dot}} today ({{rank:dot}} among cryptocurrencies).

## How it works

Polkadot has a central chain, the relay chain, secured by validators who stake DOT. Other blockchains, once called parachains and now usually called rollups, plug into it: they run their own apps and rules but borrow the relay chain's security instead of building their own. Since September 2024 projects no longer win long slot auctions; they buy "coretime" — processing time on the network — in flexible amounts (Agile Coretime). In 2025–2026 balances, staking and governance moved to Asset Hub, a system chain of the network. The next big step the developers are working on is JAM, a redesign of the core of Polkadot.

## What the token is used for

DOT has three jobs. Holders stake it to secure the network and earn rewards; in 2026 the waiting time to unstake was cut from 28 days to about two days. DOT is also the vote: all upgrades and the large on-chain treasury are decided in public referendums. And projects pay in DOT for coretime. Until 2026 DOT had no maximum supply and about 120 million new tokens were created each year; a referendum approved on 14 September 2025 set a cap of 2.1 billion, and from 14 March 2026 yearly issuance fell to about 55 million DOT, with further cuts every two years.

## Its story so far

Gavin Wood published the Polkadot whitepaper in 2016, and the Web3 Foundation raised about $145 million in a 2017 token sale; weeks later a bug in a Parity wallet froze a large part of those funds for good. The network launched in May 2020 and DOT became transferable in August 2020, when each token was split into 100 new ones. The first parachain auctions in late 2021 fuelled a rally to about $55 in November 2021. After that DOT fell for years: it dropped below $1 for the first time in June 2026 and set new lows in November 2026, about 98% below its peak — even after the supply cap took effect.

## Alternatives

Polkadot's closest rival in the "many connected chains" idea is [Cosmos]({lang}/atom/). For building apps, it competes with [Ethereum]({lang}/eth/) and its layer-2 networks, as well as fast chains like [Solana]({lang}/sol/), [Avalanche]({lang}/avax/) and [NEAR]({lang}/near/). [Kusama]({lang}/ksm/) is Polkadot's own experimental "canary" network, run on the same technology.

## Main risks

- **Big swings:** DOT has lost more than 95% from its peak; long falls can continue even while the technology improves.
- **Weak demand:** fees and coretime sales have stayed small compared with bigger networks, and some projects moved elsewhere.
- **Competition:** Ethereum's layer 2s and fast single chains attract most developers and users.
- **Supply:** the cap is far above today's supply, so new DOT is still created every year, and governance can change economic rules again.
- **Regulation and execution:** rules for tokens differ by country, and large upgrades such as JAM carry technical and delivery risk.

## What it means if you buy regularly

DOT shows that a well-funded project with strong engineering can still lose most of its value for years. Buying a fixed amount regularly means you buy more when the price is low and less when it is high, without trying to guess the bottom — but it does not remove the risk of losses. Many people keep crypto to a small part of their savings that they can afford to see fall sharply. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
