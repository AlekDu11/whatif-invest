**Turkish Airlines** (Türk Hava Yolları, ticker THYAO on Borsa Istanbul) is Turkey's national airline and one of the biggest in the world. Its trick is geography: from its hub at Istanbul Airport most of Europe, Africa, the Middle East and Asia are within a few hours, so it flies to more countries than almost any other airline and sells millions of connecting trips. It also runs a large cargo business and the low-cost airline AJet.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Q3 2026 revenue: $8.3 billion, +19.3% on the year; net profit about $1.29 billion, −6.4%.
> - 28.5 million passengers in the quarter (+4.7%), with 87.1% of seats filled.
> - Fuel cost about $2.61 billion in the quarter, up 58.5% — jet fuel prices more than doubled during 2026.
> - Fleet of 567 aircraft at the end of September; 225 Boeing jets ordered in 2025 (150 firm), delivered 2029–2034.

## How it makes money

Most of the money comes from tickets: passenger revenue was $6.8 billion in the July–September quarter. A big share of passengers only change planes in Istanbul on the way between two other countries — Turkish Airlines competes for them with Gulf airlines and European hubs. Cargo is the second engine: Turkish Cargo earned about $1.23 billion in the quarter, up 45.6%, flying to 376 destinations in 135 countries. AJet, the budget arm, flies 103 aircraft and carried 20.1 million people in the first nine months of 2026. The airline reports in US dollars because most of its revenue is in foreign currency.

## The numbers right now

In the third quarter of 2026 revenue reached $8.3 billion and profit from main operations was $929 million, 12.3% less than a year earlier, because costs grew faster than sales: total operating expenses rose 25.3% and fuel alone by 58.5%. The EBITDAR margin (profit before interest, taxes, depreciation and aircraft rent) fell 4.2 points to 25.4%. Net profit was about $1.29 billion.

For January–September revenue was $21.42 billion (+20.2%) and net profit $1.71 billion (−15.4%). The airline flies to 305 international destinations in 132 countries. It cut its 2026 capacity growth plan to 3.5–4% because of late aircraft deliveries and engine problems that grounded some jets, and it expects the fourth quarter to be weak, with fuel costs per seat-kilometre up more than 80%.

## The stock's story

Founded in 1933 as a small state airline, Turkish Airlines listed on the Istanbul exchange in 1990 and joined Star Alliance in 2008. It grew fast in the 2010s, survived a terror attack on Istanbul Atatürk Airport and a coup attempt in 2016, and moved to the much larger Istanbul Airport in 2019. Covid stopped almost all flying in 2020; then travel came back strongly and the shares rose many times over in lira. In dollars the picture is more modest because the lira lost most of its value: in our dollar-based data the stock peaked in April 2024. The Turkish state still owns a large stake through the Türkiye Wealth Fund. In 2025 the airline bought into Spain's Air Europa and ordered up to 225 Boeing jets; in 2026 it moved its 800-aircraft target from 2033 to 2036.

## Competitors

On transfer traffic it competes with Emirates, Qatar Airways and Etihad, and with European hub carriers Lufthansa, Air France-KLM and IAG. At home and on short European routes it faces Pegasus and other low-cost airlines; in cargo it competes with Qatar Airways Cargo, Emirates SkyCargo and the big express companies.

## Main risks

- **Fuel:** fuel is the biggest single cost, and only part of next year's needs is hedged.
- **Wars and politics:** conflicts in the Middle East and around the Black Sea close routes and scare travellers.
- **The lira and Turkey's economy:** high inflation raises local costs such as salaries, and the share price in lira can swing with the currency.
- **Aircraft and engines:** late deliveries and engine groundings slow growth.
- **State control:** with the state as a big shareholder, the airline may put national goals ahead of small shareholders.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Airline stocks are among the most cyclical on the market: profits can vanish in a fuel spike, a war or a pandemic, and shares can fall by half in months. Buying a fixed amount regularly spreads your purchases over good and bad times, so you buy more shares when the price is low. Our $10 a day result is in dollars, so it already includes the lira's fall. Many people hold one company only as part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
