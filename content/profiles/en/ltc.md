**Litecoin** is one of the oldest cryptocurrencies still in wide use: a lighter, faster copy of bitcoin launched in October 2011 by former Google engineer Charlie Lee, often called "silver to bitcoin's gold". People use it for cheap, quick payments and as a long-running alternative to bitcoin. In November 2026 all litecoins together were worth about $3.1 billion, around number 24 among cryptocurrencies.

> **Key takeaways**
> - There will never be more than 84 million LTC — four times bitcoin's limit; about 77.9 million exist already.
> - A new block arrives every 2.5 minutes instead of 10, and a normal payment usually costs a fraction of a cent.
> - Since October 2025 Americans can buy it through a spot ETF on Nasdaq (Canary's LTCC).
> - The record price so far is $410 (May 2021); in November 2026 LTC traded about 90% below it.

## How it works

Litecoin works much like bitcoin: a public blockchain kept by thousands of computers and secured by miners, who add a block of transactions every 2.5 minutes and receive new LTC as a reward. It uses a different mining algorithm, Scrypt, and since 2014 it has been mined together with Dogecoin, so the same machines secure both networks. Every 840,000 blocks — roughly every four years — the reward halves: in August 2023 it fell to 6.25 LTC, and the next halving, to 3.125 LTC, is expected around mid-2027. Since 2022 users can also send optional private transactions through the MimbleWimble Extension Block (MWEB).

## What the token is used for

LTC is meant to be spent and moved: it pays for goods and services, transfers between people and fees on its own network. Because it has run for 15 years with very little downtime and is listed on almost every exchange, many payment services accept it, and it is often someone's "first altcoin". Holders also see it as a cheaper, more liquid cousin of bitcoin with a fixed, known supply. Litecoin has served as a testing ground too: SegWit and the Lightning Network ran on it before bitcoin.

## Its story so far

Charlie Lee launched Litecoin on 7 October 2011 from bitcoin's code. It rose with the first crypto wave in 2013 and reached about $375 in December 2017 — when Lee sold almost all his LTC to avoid a conflict of interest. After the 2018 crash it lost more than 90%, then set its record of $410 in May 2021. In 2022 MWEB privacy went live, and some exchanges, for example in South Korea, delisted LTC in response. After the August 2023 halving the price lagged bitcoin, and in October 2025 the first US spot Litecoin ETF started trading.

## Alternatives

As payment coins, LTC competes with [Bitcoin Cash]({lang}/bch/), [Dogecoin]({lang}/doge/) and [Dash]({lang}/dash/), and with [Bitcoin]({lang}/btc/) itself plus its Lightning Network. For private payments people also choose [Monero]({lang}/xmr/) or [Zcash]({lang}/zec/). For everyday payments in crypto, stablecoins pegged to the dollar are now used much more.

## Main risks

- **Big swings:** LTC has lost more than 70% several times and is still far below its 2021 record.
- **Unclear role:** bitcoin, stablecoins and fast new networks offer most of what Litecoin does, so demand may fade.
- **Mining:** the reward halves again around 2027, and miners' income partly depends on Dogecoin.
- **Regulation:** the privacy option led some exchanges to delist it, and rules differ by country.
- **Storage:** if an exchange fails or you lose your private key, the coins can be gone for good.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Litecoin's history shows sharp peaks in 2017 and 2021 and long years far below them. Buying a fixed amount regularly means you buy more when the price is low and less when it is high, without trying to guess the bottom — but it does not remove the risk of losses. Many people keep crypto to a small part of their savings that they can afford to see fall sharply. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
