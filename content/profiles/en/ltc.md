**Litecoin** is one of the oldest cryptocurrencies still in wide use: a lighter, faster copy of bitcoin launched in October 2011 by former Google engineer Charlie Lee, often called "silver to bitcoin's gold".

## How it works

Litecoin works much like bitcoin — a public blockchain secured by miners — with a few changes. A new block arrives every 2.5 minutes instead of 10, so payments confirm faster, and fees are usually a fraction of a cent. It uses a different mining algorithm (Scrypt), and since 2014 it has been mined together with Dogecoin, so the same miners secure both networks. There will never be more than 84 million LTC, four times bitcoin's limit, and the miners' reward halves about every four years (most recently in August 2023, to 6.25 LTC).

## Why it matters

Litecoin has run for 15 years with very little downtime and is listed on almost every exchange and accepted by many payment services, which makes it a common "first altcoin". It has also served as a testing ground: SegWit and the Lightning Network ran on Litecoin before bitcoin, and in 2022 it added optional private transactions (MWEB). Since October 2025 Americans can buy it through a spot ETF on Nasdaq (Canary's LTCC). A surprising fact: Charlie Lee sold almost all his LTC in December 2017, near the top, to avoid a conflict of interest.

## Main risks

Litecoin has few features that bitcoin, stablecoins or faster new networks do not offer, so its role is easy to question. Its price is still far below its 2021 record and has lagged bitcoin for years. The private-transaction option led some exchanges, for example in South Korea, to delist it, and crypto rules differ by country. Regular buying smooths out the swings but does not remove the risk.
