**Berkshire Hathaway** is the company Warren Buffett turned from a failing textile mill into one of the most valuable businesses on earth — a holding company that owns dozens of firms outright and a stock portfolio worth more than $330 billion. Greg Abel took over as CEO in January 2026.

## How it makes money

Insurance is the engine. GEICO and Berkshire's reinsurers collect premiums up front and pay claims later; that pool of money, called "float", is invested. Next come the BNSF railroad, Berkshire Hathaway Energy (utilities and gas pipelines), industrial firms such as Precision Castparts, and familiar brands like Duracell, See's Candies, Fruit of the Loom and Brooks. On top sits a portfolio that includes Apple, American Express and Alphabet.

In Q3 2026 revenue rose 11% to $105.7 billion, while operating earnings fell 6% to $12.7 billion as insurance underwriting profit dropped 43%. BNSF profit rose 19%.

## Why it matters

Berkshire is a bet on the whole US economy in one share: its businesses move freight, keep the lights on and insure cars. It holds more than $350 billion in cash and short-term Treasuries, so it can buy big when others are forced to sell. One surprising fact: Berkshire has paid no dividend since 1967 — it reinvests profits or buys back its own shares instead ($9.6 billion in Q3 2026). One Class B share is worth 1/1,500 of a Class A share.

## Main risks

Berkshire must now prove it works without Buffett, who also gave up the chairman role in September 2026. Its huge size makes fast growth hard. A big hurricane or other disaster can wipe out a year of insurance profit, and GEICO is cutting prices to win back customers. A large cash pile earns less if interest rates fall. Regular buying smooths price swings but does not remove the risk.
