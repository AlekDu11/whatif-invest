**PGE Polska Grupa Energetyczna** is Poland's largest power company: it digs lignite (brown coal), burns it in giant plants such as Bełchatów, runs the wires to about 5.8 million homes and businesses and sends many of them their electricity bill. The Polish state owns 60.86% of it, so politics matters as much as power prices. It now wants to swap coal for offshore wind, solar, gas, storage and grids.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Q3 2026 (preliminary): EBITDA 4.61 billion zł, of which about 2.89 billion zł recurring; net profit 3.0 billion zł versus 1.3 billion zł expected by analysts.
> - Strategy to 2035: about 210 billion zł of investment, with EBITDA targeted at about 15 billion zł in 2030 and over 30 billion zł in 2035.
> - Dividends return from 2026 profit — the first since 0.25 zł per share was paid in 2016.
> - The share price, 16.29 zł on 19 November 2026, is still below the 23 zł IPO price of November 2009.

## How it makes money

PGE is several businesses in one. Conventional generation runs two lignite mines and five coal plants and produces about 29% of Poland's gross electricity. Distribution owns 304,000 km of power lines and earns regulated fees from about 5.8 million connection points. Supply sells power to about 5.7 million customers. District heating makes it Poland's largest heat producer. Renewables had 2,661 MW of wind, solar and hydro capacity in 2024, backed up by gas plants.

The growth project is offshore wind. Baltica 2, a 1.5 GW farm about 40 km off Ustka, is a 50/50 project with Denmark's Ørsted: 107 turbines that should power about 2.5 million households, with full commissioning planned by the end of 2027.

## The numbers right now

Reported EBITDA of 4.61 billion zł includes 1.73 billion zł of one-off items, mostly a 1.67 billion zł non-cash change in the reserve for restoring land after mining; a 299 million zł settlement over the Turów unit 7 contract added more, while a new reserve for past water-use fees took away 245 million zł. Without one-offs, recurring EBITDA was about 2.89 billion zł.

In 2025 the group had recurring EBITDA of 12.89 billion zł but a net loss of 3.5 billion zł. At the end of June 2026 net debt was only 1.75 billion zł. The new strategy (19 October 2026) splits the 210 billion zł roughly into 80 billion for renewables and storage, 70 billion for grids, 48 billion for gas, heat and nuclear, and only 5 billion to keep coal plants running. Debt is to stay at or below 2.8 times EBITDA, and the first dividend yield is capped at 5%.

## The stock's story

PGE listed in Warsaw on 6 November 2009 at 23 zł and paid dividends every year from 2010 to 2016, a total of 6.23 zł per share. Then rising CO2 costs and the burden of coal pushed the price far below the IPO level for years and dividends stopped. The previous government planned to move the coal plants of PGE, Tauron, Enea and Energa into a state agency called NABE, but the bill was withdrawn in February 2024, and in July 2025 the government dropped the idea, calling it economically unjustified. CEO Dariusz Marzec was dismissed in December 2025 and Dariusz Lubera has led the group since 15 January 2026.

## Competitors

In Poland PGE competes with Tauron, Enea and Energa (owned by Orlen), which have similar coal-to-green problems, and with Orlen and Polenergia in offshore wind. Its advantages are size and the country's largest grid business.

## Main risks

- **Coal stays on the books:** without NABE, PGE keeps its coal plants; its own plan expects coal to lose about 1.5 billion zł of EBITDA in 2035.
- **State control:** the government picks the management and can push priorities such as low household bills over shareholder returns.
- **Building 210 billion zł of projects:** delays or cost overruns would raise debt.
- **CO2 and power prices:** EU carbon costs and wholesale prices swing results from quarter to quarter.
- **Noisy profits:** reserves and one-off items, as in Q3 2026, can move reported profit by billions.

## What it means if you buy regularly

PGE shows that a big, important company can still leave shareholders below the IPO price 17 years later. Regular buying spreads entries across cheap and expensive years, and the dividend restart is a change worth watching, but the stock depends heavily on Polish politics and on one country's energy transition. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
