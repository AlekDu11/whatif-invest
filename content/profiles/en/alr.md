**Alior Bank** is a Polish bank for everyday people and small and mid-sized firms: current accounts, cash loans, mortgages, leasing and business credit, mostly sold through its mobile app and branches. It earns more on each złoty it lends than most rivals, thanks to a large share of consumer loans. In 2026 its largest shareholder was PZU, Poland's biggest insurer, which is state-controlled and held 31.91%.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Net profit: 473.2 million zł in Q3 2026, −16% a year, but 12% above analysts' forecasts.
> - Net interest margin of 5.17% — high by European standards, though down from 5.61% a year ago.
> - A tax rise for banks (corporate income tax up from 19% to 30% in 2026) pushed Alior's effective tax rate to 36%.
> - For 2025 it paid a dividend of 8.93 zł per share (1.16 billion zł); by Q3 2026 bad loans had fallen below 5% of the book (4.97%).

## How it makes money

Alior lives mainly on interest: the gap between what borrowers pay and what it pays savers. In Q3 2026 net interest income was 1.31 billion zł, against 244 million zł from fees and commissions. Retail is the larger side — 46.1 billion zł of gross loans and 82.9 billion zł of deposits — while business clients hold 26.3 billion zł of loans and 23.6 billion zł of deposits. The bank has about 1.74 million relationship clients and 1.84 million mobile banking users (mid-2026).

Mortgages are growing fast: Alior sold 1.83 billion zł of new home loans in Q3 alone.

In July–September 2026 Alior earned 473.2 million zł, down 16% from 563 million zł a year earlier but well above the 421.7 million zł analysts expected. Net interest income rose 1.4%, costs 1.7%, and the cost of risk (money set aside for bad loans) was just 0.41% of loans. The two drags were 54 million zł more in legal-dispute provisions and 102 million zł more in income tax. For nine months, profit was 1.24 billion zł, down 26%.

Capital is comfortable: the total capital ratio was 17.45%, about 3.1 billion zł above the regulatory minimum. With bad loans under 5%, the CEO, Piotr Żabski, says the bank could pay out up to 75% of profit, subject to the regulator (KNF).

## The stock's story

Alior was founded in 2008 by Italy's Carlo Tassara group and listed in Warsaw on 14 December 2012 at 57 zł a share. It grew by buying Meritum Bank (2015) and the core business of Bank BPH (2016); PZU bought in from 2015. The shares sank to all-time lows in 2020, when the pandemic, bad loans and a dividend ban hit Polish banks, and the bank posted a loss of 513 million zł in the first half of that year. Higher interest rates later turned it into a strong earner, and in September 2026 the shares reached 142.65 zł — about two and a half times the IPO price.

## Competitors

Alior competes with Poland's big banks — PKO BP, Bank Pekao, Santander Bank Polska, mBank and ING Bank Śląski — and with app-based consumer lenders. Being mid-sized, its edge is speed and pricing in cash loans and small-business credit, not scale.

## Main risks

- **Ownership question:** in December 2024 PZU signed a letter of intent to sell its Alior stake to Pekao; in June 2025 PZU and Pekao agreed to plan a merger and to work out "an optimal strategy" for Alior. As of September 2026 that deal was delayed and Alior's future (sale, merger or staying independent) was still undecided.
- **Legal costs:** an EU Court of Justice ruling of 23 April 2026 on consumer-credit charges cut Alior's Q2 interest income by 153.2 million zł; foreign-currency mortgage cases still need provisions.
- **Taxes:** the bank tax rate of 30% in 2026 only falls gradually to 23% by 2028.
- **Falling rates:** rate cuts by Poland's central bank would squeeze the high margin.
- **Business loans:** bad loans among business clients were above 11% in mid-2026, far higher than in retail (2.1%).

## What it means if you buy regularly

Alior is a cyclical bank stock: it lost most of its value before 2020 and then multiplied as rates rose. Dividends were large in 2025–2026 but depend on profits, taxes and the regulator, and a PZU–Pekao decision could move the price sharply either way. Regular buying spreads that uncertainty over time; many people keep a single bank as a small part of a broader portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
