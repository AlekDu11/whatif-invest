**Altria** is the company that sells Marlboro cigarettes in the United States. Through its units Philip Morris USA, U.S. Smokeless Tobacco (Copenhagen, Skoal) and Helix (on! nicotine pouches) it sells tobacco and nicotine products to American adults, and it owns NJOY e-cigarettes. Fewer Americans smoke every year, so Altria makes its money by raising prices and returns most of it to shareholders as dividends.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue net of excise taxes: $5.44 billion in Q3 2026, +3.6% a year, even though cigarette shipments fell 2.9%.
> - The dividend is $4.44 a share a year after the 61st increase in 57 years; dividends paid in the first nine months of 2026 came to $5.3 billion.
> - on! pouch shipments rose 13.3% to 47.8 million cans in the quarter, while NJOY ACE is still banned from the US market by a patent ruling.
> - 2026 adjusted earnings guidance raised to $5.66–5.74 per share, +4.5–6% on 2025.

## How it makes money

Almost everything comes from smoking. The smokeable segment (Marlboro plus cigars) brought in $5.43 billion of the $6.13 billion total revenue in Q3 2026, with an adjusted operating margin of 64.5% — very high for any business. The trick is pricing: Altria sells about 4% fewer cigarettes a year but charges more for each pack. Marlboro still holds 39.5% of the US retail market, down 1 point in a year, as smokers trade down to cheap brands (discount brands now have 34.1% of the market).

The oral segment — Copenhagen, Skoal and on! pouches — earned $688 million. Nicotine pouches are the growing part: they already make up 61.2% of the US oral tobacco category. Altria also owns a stake in brewer Anheuser-Busch InBev, which added $228 million of pre-tax income in the quarter.

## The numbers right now

In the quarter to 30 September 2026 Altria reported $6.13 billion in revenue (+1.0%) and $5.44 billion net of excise taxes (+3.6%). Diluted earnings were $1.51 per share (+7.1%), or $1.52 adjusted (+4.8%). It bought back 5.9 million of its own shares for $400 million and expanded the buyback programme by $1 billion. The company had $2.7 billion in cash against $24.1 billion of debt. For 2026 it now expects adjusted earnings of $5.66–5.74 per share, assuming NJOY ACE stays off the market all year.

## The stock's story

Altria was a market favourite for decades, but after a peak in June 2017 the shares slid for years. Two bad bets hurt: in 2018 it paid for a stake in vape maker JUUL valued at $12.8 billion, which was later worth only about $250 million when Altria walked away in 2023 and bought rival NJOY for $2.75 billion instead. NJOY then lost a patent fight with JUUL: a US trade commission order banned imports and sales of the NJOY ACE device, and Altria wrote off $873 million of e-vapor goodwill in 2025. Even so, steady dividends and buybacks lifted the stock: in July 2026 it reached its highest level since June 2017, and on 13 November it closed at a 52-week high of $78.64.

## Competitors

In cigarettes Altria's main rival is Reynolds American (owned by British American Tobacco: Newport, Camel), plus a long tail of cheap brands. In pouches it competes with Zyn from Philip Morris International — its former sister company, separated in 2008 — and BAT's Velo. In vapes the toughest competition is often illegal disposable e-cigarettes sold without US approval.

## Main risks

- **Fewer smokers:** US cigarette volumes fall every year; price increases can only offset that for so long.
- **Trading down:** Marlboro shipments dropped 6.4% in Q3 while Altria's own discount brands grew 40.5%: smokers are paying less per pack.
- **Smoke-free race:** on! lost share of the pouch market (13.5%, −1.3 points) to Zyn, and NJOY ACE stays banned while Altria appeals.
- **Regulation and lawsuits:** the FDA can restrict menthol or nicotine levels; tobacco litigation charges were $120 million in nine months.
- **Debt:** $24.1 billion of borrowings has to be serviced alongside a $5+ billion yearly dividend.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Altria is a "slow" stock: most of its long-term return has come from dividends, not price growth, and the price went nowhere for almost a decade after 2017. Regular buying with dividends reinvested buys more shares in weak years, which is when the yield is highest — but it also means betting on a business whose main product shrinks every year. Many people treat a stock like this as one income holding inside a broader portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
