**APA Corporation** is the parent of Apache, an American oil and gas producer based in Houston. It pumps crude and gas mainly in the Permian Basin of Texas and New Mexico and in Egypt's Western Desert, still runs older fields in the UK North Sea and is building a big offshore oil project in Suriname. When oil is expensive APA makes a lot of money; when oil collapses, so does the stock.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Q3 2026: revenue $2.77 billion (vs $2.12 billion a year earlier), adjusted profit $1.94 per share (vs $0.93).
> - Free cash flow of $699 million in the quarter; $537 million went back to shareholders, $450 million of it as buybacks.
> - Net debt fell to $3.22 billion from $3.98 billion at the end of 2025; the new target is $2 billion.
> - On 20 November 2026 the stock closed at $45.03, about 69% below its May 2008 peak.

## How it makes money

APA sells oil, natural gas and NGLs (liquids such as propane). In Q3 2026 it produced about 450,000 barrels of oil equivalent a day. Roughly two thirds came from the **United States**, mostly the Permian, where APA grew by buying Callon Petroleum in April 2024: an all-share deal that added about 145,000 net acres and roughly 103,000 barrels a day. **Egypt** delivered about 134,000 barrels a day and receives the highest oil price in the group, $99.72 a barrel. The **North Sea** is small, about 22,000 barrels a day, and APA plans to stop producing there by the end of 2029, blaming the UK's windfall tax and new emissions rules.

The next growth step is **Suriname**: the offshore GranMorgu project, where first oil is expected in mid-2028.

## The numbers right now

In the quarter to 30 September 2026 APA earned $698 million, or $2.00 a share (adjusted: $1.94). Its average realised oil price was $92.02 a barrel, while US gas fetched only $1.22 per thousand cubic feet, a sign of how weak gas prices are in West Texas.

APA pays a dividend of $0.25 a share each quarter and bought back 17.8 million shares this year through October. Management now expects about $2.9 billion of free cash flow in 2026, $600 million more than it said in August, and plans about $2.5 billion of capital spending in 2027, mostly more money for Suriname and exploration.

## The stock's story

Apache was a star of the 2000s oil boom: the shares peaked on 20 May 2008. After that it fell hard whenever oil did. The worst blow hit in 2020: the pandemic and a Saudi–Russian price war pushed oil to about $30 a barrel in March, Apache cut its dividend by 90% and halted drilling in the Permian, and by 1 April 2020 the stock had lost about 97% from its 2008 high. The oil rebound of 2021–2022 lifted the shares sharply, 2023–2024 were down years again, and in 2026 higher oil prices, debt cuts and buybacks pushed the shares back up to $45.03.

## Competitors

In the Permian APA competes with larger shale producers such as ExxonMobil, Chevron, ConocoPhillips, Occidental, Diamondback and EOG Resources. Its difference is the mix: few US peers of its size also have a large business in Egypt and an offshore project in South America.

## Main risks

- **Oil price:** almost all profit depends on it; the 2020 crash shows how fast the picture can turn.
- **Weak US gas prices:** Permian gas sold for just $1.22 in Q3, so the gas side earns little.
- **Egypt:** a big share of output sits in one country with its own economic and political risks.
- **Suriname:** a large offshore project can be delayed or cost more before first oil in 2028.
- **North Sea exit:** shutting fields costs money; APA plans about $285 million of abandonment and decommissioning spending in 2026.

## What it means if you buy regularly

APA is a cyclical stock: it can double in a good oil year and lose most of its value in a bad one. Buying a fixed amount regularly spreads purchases across high and low oil prices, and the dividend can be reinvested, but a single oil producer can stay far below its peak for many years, as APA has since 2008. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
