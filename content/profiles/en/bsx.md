**Boston Scientific** makes medical devices that doctors put inside the body through small cuts or blood vessels instead of open surgery: catheters that treat irregular heartbeats, the Watchman implant that lowers stroke risk, stents, devices for kidney stones and the gut, and nerve stimulators for chronic pain. Hospitals buy them, and the company earns more each time a new device becomes the standard way to treat a common disease. After a fast 2025, its 2026 was hit by a cyberattack and tougher competition.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Sales: $20.07 billion in 2025, up 19.9%; GAAP net income $2.90 billion ($1.94 per share).
> - Cardiovascular devices brought in $13.25 billion in 2025, about two-thirds of sales.
> - In the September 2026 quarter sales grew only 1.5%, to $5.14 billion, after an August cyberattack shut its plants for eight days.
> - In January 2026 it agreed to buy Penumbra, a maker of clot-removal devices, for about $14.5 billion.

## How it makes money

The company has two segments. **Cardiovascular** ($13.25 billion in 2025) includes electrophysiology — mainly Farapulse, a catheter that treats atrial fibrillation with short electric pulses — plus the Watchman heart implant, stents and balloons for blocked arteries, and devices for heart valves and leg arteries. **MedSurg** ($6.82 billion) covers endoscopy tools for the stomach and gut ($2.92 billion), urology products for kidney stones and the prostate ($2.71 billion) and neuromodulation — implants that ease chronic pain ($1.20 billion). Most devices are used once, so sales repeat with every procedure.

In the September 2026 quarter net sales were $5.14 billion, up 1.5% from a year earlier. Cardiovascular grew 1.9% to $3.41 billion and MedSurg 0.6% to $1.73 billion; neuromodulation rose 13.1%, while urology fell 3.2%. GAAP net income dropped to $582 million ($0.40 per share) from $755 million, and adjusted earnings were $0.73 per share. For the first nine months of 2026 sales were $15.78 billion against $14.79 billion a year earlier.

With those results the company cut its 2026 forecast for the third time: sales growth of about 4.25% and adjusted earnings of $3.16–3.18 per share, with fourth-quarter sales expected to fall 1–3%. Chief executive Mike Mahoney said the company would be challenged to grow sales and adjusted earnings in 2027, not counting Penumbra.

## The stock's story

In 2006 Boston Scientific won a bidding war with Johnson & Johnson for Guidant, a heart-defibrillator maker, for about $27 billion. Soon after, Guidant device recalls and heavy debt hit the company, and the stock spent years far below its 2004 levels. Mike Mahoney became CEO in 2012 and rebuilt it around faster-growing products: Watchman came with the purchase of Atritech in 2011 and Farapulse with a deal in 2021. After Farapulse won US approval in January 2024, sales boomed and the stock climbed to records in 2024 and 2025. In 2026 the mood turned: competitors' pulsed-field catheters took share, Watchman sales fell 6% in the third quarter, and a cyberattack found on 25 August 2026 stopped production and shipping. On 28 October 2026, results day, the shares fell as much as 12% during trading.

## Competitors

In heart-rhythm treatment the main rivals are Medtronic (whose Sphere-9 catheter gained share in the US in 2026), Johnson & Johnson and Abbott. Abbott's Amulet competes with Watchman. In other areas Boston Scientific fights Medtronic, Stryker, Edwards Lifesciences, Olympus in endoscopy and Teleflex in urology.

## Main risks

- **Competition in its growth engine:** Farapulse and Watchman drove the 2024–2025 boom; losing share there hits growth the most.
- **Rebuilding after the cyberattack:** some hospitals ordered from rivals during the shutdown and may not all come back.
- **A big acquisition:** Penumbra adds about $11 billion of cash cost and new debt, and the company expected it to cut adjusted earnings slightly in the first full year.
- **Recalls and regulation:** device safety problems, like the Guidant recalls in the 2000s, can cost years.
- **Hospital budgets and tariffs:** fewer procedures or higher costs squeeze profit.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Boston Scientific shows that even a fast-growing company can stall quickly when competitors catch up or something goes wrong in its operations. Buying a fixed amount on a schedule means a falling price buys more shares, but it does not protect you if growth does not come back. Many people hold one healthcare stock only as a small part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
