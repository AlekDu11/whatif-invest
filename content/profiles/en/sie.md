**Siemens** is Europe's biggest industrial technology company: its software, controllers and power gear run factories, trains, buildings and, increasingly, AI data centres. Werner von Siemens founded it in Berlin in 1847; today it is one of the largest companies on Germany's DAX index.

## How it makes money

Siemens has three core businesses. Digital Industries sells factory automation and design software (it bought US simulation firm Altair) — €19.7bn of revenue in fiscal 2026 at a 19% margin. Smart Infrastructure supplies electrical equipment and building systems — €25.1bn, boosted by data-centre demand. Mobility builds trains and rail signalling — €13bn. Group revenue for the year to September 2026 was €83.4bn (+8% comparable) and net income €9.5bn.

## Why it matters

Orders hit a record €108.2bn, up 22%, so Siemens now has a €143bn backlog — more than 1.7 years of sales already booked. Mobility alone won €26.6bn of orders, including high-speed trains for Italy and suburban trains for Berlin. Free cash flow was a record €12.1bn, and the board proposes a €5.50 dividend. One surprising fact: in fiscal 2027 Siemens plans to hand a 34.1% stake in its medical arm, Siemens Healthineers, directly to its own shareholders — one Healthineers share for every two Siemens shares.

## Main risks

Siemens lives on capital spending: when factories, railways and builders cut budgets, orders fall. Part of today's boom depends on data-centre construction, which could cool. China is a big market for automation and medical equipment and has been weak for Healthineers diagnostics. The Healthineers spin-off makes the company smaller and complicates comparisons — the 2027 profit outlook of €10.20–10.80 per share excludes it. A strong euro cuts the value of overseas sales. Buying a fixed amount regularly smooths price swings but does not remove the risk.
