**Alexandria Real Estate Equities** is a landlord for science: it owns and builds laboratory and office campuses where drug makers, biotech start-ups and research institutes do their work. It is a REIT — a real estate investment trust, a company that collects rent and must pay most of its profit out as dividends. Its buildings sit in the main US science hubs: Greater Boston, the San Francisco Bay Area, San Diego, Seattle, Maryland, Research Triangle in North Carolina and New York City.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: $671.2 million in Q3 2026, down about 11% from $751.9 million a year earlier.
> - Occupancy fell to 87.1% of its 36.5 million square feet; rents on renewed leases were 2.4% lower than before.
> - The quarterly dividend was cut 45%, from $1.32 to $0.72, in December 2025.
> - The stock peaked at the end of 2021 and has since lost more than 75% of its value.

## How it makes money

Alexandria earns rent. Tenants sign long leases — on average 7.7 years are left, and 10 years for the 20 biggest tenants — and 57% of annual rent comes from investment-grade or large listed companies. About 80% of rent comes from what Alexandria calls Megacampuses: clusters of buildings with shared labs, cafés and meeting space near top universities. It also develops new buildings, with 1.9 million square feet under construction.

Lab space is expensive to build, so the model works when biotech is well funded and companies are expanding. When money for drug research dries up, tenants shrink, leave or stop looking for new space.

## The numbers right now

In the quarter to 30 September 2026 Alexandria reported a net loss of $3.74 per share, mostly because of $637.9 million of impairments — write-downs on buildings and land it plans to sell for less than their book value. Funds from operations (FFO, the cash-profit measure REITs use), as adjusted, were $1.54 per share, against $2.22 a year earlier. Same-property net operating income fell 6.7%.

The company is selling assets to cut debt: about $717 million of deals were completed by late October, including a 29% stake in its Campus Point campus in San Diego for $475 million. It has $4.56 billion of liquidity and only 6% of its debt matures before the end of 2028. It kept its 2026 guidance of $6.38–6.42 of adjusted FFO per share and will give its 2027 outlook at an investor day on 2 December 2026.

## The stock's story

Alexandria was founded by Joel Marcus in 1994 and grew into the largest listed lab landlord in the US. During the biotech boom of 2020–2021 the stock reached its record at the end of 2021. Then biotech funding fell, many new labs were built at once, and empty space piled up. The shares fell in every year from 2022 to 2025, including about 48% in 2025, when the company cut its dividend. Peter Moglia has been sole CEO since 2022; Marcus remains executive chairman.

## Competitors

Alexandria's main rival is BioMed Realty, owned by Blackstone. Other lab landlords include Healthpeak Properties, Kilroy Realty and BXP (Boston Properties), plus many private developers who built new labs in 2021–2023. Alexandria's advantages are its size, its locations next to top research centres and long relationships with big pharma tenants.

## Main risks

- **Too much lab space:** new buildings in Boston, the Bay Area and San Diego compete for fewer tenants, which keeps pushing rents and occupancy down.
- **Biotech funding:** if drug start-ups raise less money, they rent less space or close.
- **Debt:** about $12.9 billion of notes and credit lines; asset sales at low prices mean more write-downs.
- **Lease expirations:** 1.4 million square feet with about $100.5 million of annual rent expire in 2027, and some tenants may wind down.
- **Dividend:** it was already cut once; the payout now depends on how fast the business stabilises.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Alexandria shows that a dividend stock is not automatically a safe stock: the share price lost more than three-quarters of its value and the payout was cut 45%. Monthly buying through a decline like this lowers the average purchase price, but a recovery is not guaranteed and can take many years. REITs like this are usually held as a small part of a broader portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
