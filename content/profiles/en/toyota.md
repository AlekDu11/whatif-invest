**Toyota** is the world's biggest carmaker: Corolla, RAV4, Camry, Land Cruiser, Prius and the luxury brand Lexus. It bet early on hybrids (cars with both a petrol engine and an electric motor), and that bet now carries the company: more than half of the Toyota and Lexus cars it sold from April to September 2026 had an electric motor. Its shares trade in Tokyo under the code 7203 and in New York as TM.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: ¥26.96 trillion in April–September 2026, +9.5% on the year; net income ¥2.24 trillion, +26%.
> - Electrified cars (hybrids, plug-ins, battery EVs) were 55.6% of Toyota and Lexus sales, up from 46.7%.
> - Full-year operating profit forecast raised to ¥3.9 trillion from ¥3.4 trillion, even with about ¥1.1 trillion of US tariff costs.
> - Dividend forecast ¥100 per share for the year (+¥5); buyback limit raised to ¥1.3 trillion.

## How it makes money

Almost all of Toyota's money comes from building and selling cars, trucks and parts under the Toyota, Lexus and Daihatsu brands. A second, steadier business is Toyota Financial Services: car loans and leases that keep earning interest for years after a car leaves the showroom. Japan is the profit engine, because cars built there and sold abroad bring back more yen when the yen is weak. In the half-year the dollar averaged ¥159 against ¥146 a year earlier, and exchange rates alone added about ¥685 billion to operating profit.

## The numbers right now

From April to September 2026 Toyota had revenue of ¥26.96 trillion and operating profit of ¥2.19 trillion, an 8.1% margin. Net income rose 26% to ¥2.24 trillion. It sold 4.76 million vehicles on a consolidated basis, about the same as a year earlier. North America swung back into profit thanks to price rises and a smaller tariff bill, while China stayed weak. Hybrids remain the core at 2.45 million in six months; battery EVs more than doubled to 231,000.

For the full year to March 2027 Toyota now expects revenue of ¥53.5 trillion (trimmed from ¥54 trillion), operating profit of ¥3.9 trillion and net income of ¥3.57 trillion, assuming ¥157 per dollar. The year to March 2026 brought ¥50.68 trillion of revenue and ¥3.77 trillion of operating profit. Materials are expected to cost about ¥1.3 trillion more than last year. The interim dividend is ¥50 per share, and Toyota will cancel another 1 billion treasury shares.

## The stock's story

Toyota grew out of a loom maker run by the Toyoda family. Its lean, just-in-time "Toyota Production System" was copied worldwide, and the Prius made it the hybrid leader. The hard years were the 2008–09 crisis, when Toyota posted its first operating loss in about 70 years, and the 2009–10 recalls over sudden acceleration. In 2021 the shares were split five-for-one, making 100-share lots cheaper for small investors. In 2026 Toyota group companies took Toyota Industries private, and Toyota spent about ¥3.66 trillion buying back its own shares in a tender offer linked to the deal. In April 2026 finance chief Kenta Kon took over as CEO from Koji Sato, who became vice chairman; Akio Toyoda stays chairman.

## Competitors

Toyota competes with Volkswagen, Hyundai-Kia, Stellantis, GM, Ford and its Japanese rivals Honda and Nissan. In electric cars the pressure comes from China's BYD and from Tesla. In China local brands are winning: Toyota and Lexus sales there fell to 685,000 in the half-year from 914,000.

## Main risks

- **Tariffs:** many cars Toyota sells in the US are made in Japan, Mexico or Canada; tariffs still cost about ¥1.1 trillion in this year's forecast.
- **The yen:** a large part of the profit comes from a weak yen; if the yen strengthens, earnings fall even if sales hold up.
- **The EV race:** Toyota is behind BYD and Tesla in battery cars; if buyers switch faster than expected, the hybrid lead matters less.
- **Costs and quality:** materials cost about ¥1.3 trillion more this year, and recalls and certification scandals have hurt Toyota and its group companies before.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Toyota is a huge, profitable company with a rising dividend, but car stocks move with the economy, the yen and trade policy, and they can fall hard in a recession. Buying a fixed amount regularly means you get more shares when the price is low and fewer when it is high, without trying to time the market.
