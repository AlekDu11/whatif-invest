**Toyota** is the world's biggest carmaker: Corolla, RAV4, Camry, Land Cruiser, Prius and the luxury brand Lexus. It bet early on hybrids — cars with both a petrol engine and an electric motor — and that bet is now paying off: more than half of the Toyota and Lexus cars it sold in the April–September 2026 half-year were electrified. Its shares trade in Tokyo under the code 7203 (and in New York as TM).

> **Key takeaways**
> - Revenue: ¥26.96 trillion in April–September 2026, +9.5% on the year; net income ¥2.24 trillion, +26.1%.
> - Electrified cars (hybrids, plug-ins, battery EVs) were 55.6% of Toyota + Lexus sales, up from 46.7%.
> - Full-year forecast raised: operating income ¥3.9 trillion (was ¥3.4 trillion), still including about ¥1.1 trillion of US tariff costs.
> - Dividend forecast ¥100 per share for the year; buyback limit raised to ¥1.3 trillion.

## How it makes money

Almost all of Toyota's money comes from building and selling vehicles — about 9.7 million a year under the Toyota, Lexus and Daihatsu brands — plus parts and service. A second, steadier business is Toyota Financial Services: car loans and leases that earn interest for years after a car is sold. Toyota sells everywhere, but two regions carry the profit: in the half-year Japan earned ¥1.25 trillion of operating income and North America ¥166 billion, back in profit after a loss a year earlier. A weak yen helps a lot, because cars built in Japan and sold abroad bring back more yen: exchange rates added about ¥685 billion to half-year operating income.

## The numbers right now

From April to September 2026 Toyota had revenue of ¥26.96 trillion and operating income of ¥2.19 trillion (an 8.1% margin). Net income rose 26.1% to ¥2.24 trillion, or ¥185.97 per share. It sold 4.76 million vehicles on a consolidated basis, about the same as a year earlier: Japan was up 5.6%, while China stayed weak. Battery EVs more than doubled to 231,000, but hybrids remain the core — 2.45 million in six months.

For the full year to March 2027 Toyota now expects revenue of ¥53.5 trillion (slightly lower than before) and operating income of ¥3.9 trillion (raised by ¥500 billion), with net income of ¥3.57 trillion. The forecast assumes ¥157 per dollar. The interim dividend is ¥50, and the yearly forecast is ¥100, up from ¥95.

## The stock's story

Toyota grew out of a loom maker; Kiichiro Toyoda started the car business in the 1930s. Its "Toyota Production System" — lean, just-in-time factories — was copied around the world, and in 1997 it launched the Prius, the first mass-produced hybrid. The hard years were the 2008–09 financial crisis, when it posted its first operating loss in about 70 years, and the 2009–10 recalls over sudden acceleration. In 2021 the shares were split five-for-one, which made them affordable for small Japanese investors. In April 2026 finance chief Kenta Kon became CEO, replacing Koji Sato, while Akio Toyoda stays chairman. In our dollar-based data the stock reached its highest monthly level in early 2026.

## Competitors

Toyota competes with Volkswagen, Hyundai-Kia, Stellantis, GM, Ford and its Japanese rivals Honda and Nissan. In electric cars the pressure comes from China's BYD and from Tesla, and in China itself local brands have taken a large share of the market from foreign carmakers.

## Main risks

- **Tariffs:** many of the cars it sells in the US are made in Japan, Mexico or Canada; US tariffs still cost about ¥1.1 trillion in this year's forecast.
- **The yen:** a big part of the profit comes from a weak yen; if the yen strengthens, earnings fall even if sales hold up.
- **The EV race:** Toyota is behind BYD and Tesla in battery cars; if buyers move faster than expected, the hybrid lead matters less.
- **China:** Toyota + Lexus sales there fell to 685,000 in the half-year from 914,000.
- **Costs and quality:** materials cost about ¥1 trillion more this year, and recalls or certification scandals have hurt Toyota and its group companies before.

## What it means if you buy regularly

Toyota is a huge, profitable company that pays a growing dividend, but car stocks move with the economy, the yen and trade policy, and they can fall a lot in a recession. Buying a fixed amount regularly means you get more shares when the price is low and fewer when it is high, without trying to time the market. Note that our $10 a day result is in dollars, so it includes the yen's moves. Many people hold one company only as part of a wider portfolio. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
