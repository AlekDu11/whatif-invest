What do you really pay when you buy, sell or send crypto? Usually three things: a trading fee (the exchange's commission), the spread (the gap between the buy and the sell price) and, when coins leave the exchange, a network fee (paid to the blockchain). The easiest way to see all of them at once is the "you will receive" line on the confirm screen.

Key takeaways

  • A regular Binance user's example trading fee is 0.1% of the trade — $1 on a $1,000 buy — and paying it with BNB gives up to 25% off (Binance support pages, updated January–February 2026).
  • Simple "Buy" buttons and card payments often cost more than the order book, because the price includes a spread and sometimes a card fee.
  • A Bitcoin network fee depends on the size of the transaction in data and on how busy the network is — not on how much money you send.

Fee 1 — the trading fee

This is what the exchange charges for doing the trade. It is a percentage of the amount.

  • Binance's own example uses a 0.1% rate for a regular user: buying 10 ETH costs a fee of 0.01 ETH. The fee is taken in the coin you receive — when you buy ETH you pay in ETH, when you sell for USDC you pay in USDC.
  • Many exchanges have two rates: maker (your order waits in the list until someone takes it, for example a limit order below the price) and taker (your order fills straight away at the current price). Maker is often the same or cheaper.
  • Big traders get lower rates (VIP levels). Beginners pay the standard rate.

Step 1 — Find the fee page. On every big exchange there is a page called Fees or Fee schedule (on Binance: your profile → Fees & Transactions Overview). Look at the spot rate for your level.

Step 2 — Turn on discounts you already qualify for. On Binance, keep some BNB in your Spot account and switch on Using BNB Deduction (Fee Level menu, or in the app: Account Center → Settings → BNB Discount). Binance says this gives up to 25% off spot fees. You do not have to buy BNB for this; it only helps if you already hold some.

Fee 2 — the spread

The spread is the difference between the price you can buy at and the price you can sell at at the same moment. If you buy and immediately sell, you lose the spread even with zero commission.

  • On the main trading screen (the order book) of a big coin like Bitcoin the spread is usually tiny.
  • On simple Buy / Sell / Convert screens the exchange shows one price that already includes its margin. Coinbase, for example, says in its pricing disclosures that simple buys and sells include a spread on top of the fee.
  • On small coins and memecoins the spread can be several percent, because few people are trading.

Step 3 — Compare two prices. Before a simple buy, open the coin's chart on the same exchange (or on our Bitcoin page) and compare the market price with the price on your confirm screen. The difference is roughly your spread.

Step 4 — Watch the card fee. Buying with a bank card is the fastest way in, and often the most expensive: exchanges and payment partners usually add a card fee of a few percent. A bank transfer or the exchange's P2P is usually cheaper but slower — see how to buy Bitcoin with a bank card.

Fee 3 — the network fee

When you withdraw coins to your own wallet or another exchange, the transfer is written into the blockchain. The people who run the network (miners or validators) take a fee for it.

  • On Bitcoin, the fee is priced in satoshis per virtual byte (sat/vB; a satoshi is one hundred-millionth of a bitcoin). A simple payment is about 140 vB, so sending 0.01 BTC or 9 BTC in the same kind of transaction costs the same.
  • When many people send at once, fees go up; when the network is quiet, they fall.
  • Exchanges show a withdrawal fee that covers this and is sometimes higher than the real network fee. It is often a fixed amount of the coin, not a percentage — so it hurts most on small withdrawals.
  • Different networks for the same coin cost very different amounts (USDT on one network may cost cents, on another several dollars). Choose the network your receiving address supports — see how to withdraw crypto to your own wallet.

Step 5 — Read the withdrawal screen before you confirm. It shows the network, the fee and "amount received". If the fee is large compared with the amount, wait and send a bigger amount once instead of many small ones.

Step 6 — Write it down

Add each buy with its real cost to your portfolio. Then your result shows what you actually paid, not just the coin's price change.

⚠️ Watch out

  • "Zero fees" banners. A trade can be free of commission and still have a wide spread. Always look at the "you will receive" number.
  • Many tiny withdrawals. A fixed withdrawal fee of a few dollars on a $20 transfer is a big share of your money.
  • The wrong network to save a few cents. If the receiving side does not support that network, the coins can be lost. Test with a small amount first.

What it means if you buy regularly

If you buy a small amount every week, fees add up: 0.1% on each buy is small, but a few percent card fee on each buy is not. People who buy regularly often top up by bank transfer, use a recurring buy on the main market, and withdraw to their own wallet only now and then in one larger transfer. Example from our calculator: $10 a day in Bitcoin for 3 years ($10,960 in total) became $9,103 (−16.9%), before fees. Past results, not a forecast. Bitcoin is at $61,485.10 +0.85% right now.
